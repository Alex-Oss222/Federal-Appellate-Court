## Scope, sources, and simulation controls

This file briefs the eighteen unique cases expressly requested. Walker and American Geophysical Union appear once each. The separate case-list file records docket numbers, dates, citations, and opinion-version qualifications.

The supplied **Master Jurisprudence: Judge Stone, Court of Appeals** supplies Stone's methodology, not governing law. The separately identified Case Brief Template was not available in the mounted files or the Project retrieval surface. The sections below therefore implement the substantive requirements in the project instructions without claiming to reproduce the unavailable template. No approved case-specific positions or adopted simulation holdings were supplied or located. Every Stone position, alternative, and negotiating limit below is a **proposal pending approval**.

Each simulation is fixed immediately before disposition on the specified 1994 decision date. The opinion being simulated supplies a historical account of the record and the comparison section; its eventual holding does not bind Stone's deliberations in that same case. Earlier published circuit decisions remain governing law unless a supplied, adopted simulation holding changes them. No such change is assumed. Later amendments and subsequent appellate proceedings do not supply governing law. For a selected amended opinion, the simulation is the actual reconsideration posture: the earlier version of that disposition is under review, not a separate unlisted case or an invented adopted simulation holding.

The judicial opinions reproduced at the linked sources are the principal primary materials examined. Statements about evidence and arguments are attributed to those opinions, not to independently inspected trial transcripts or appellate briefs. Where the underlying docket, filing, or exhibit was not obtained, the brief does not invent its contents. Reporter citations to antecedent authorities identify the rules discussed and applied in the source opinions; proposed extensions are expressly distinguished from those rules.

**Proposed assignment convention.** The historical panel is preserved except that Stone provisionally occupies its third-listed seat. This uniform administrative convention was selected without regard to the other judges' historical votes. It is not an assertion of an actual substitution, recusal, or approved simulation roster. Stone's precise commission date and any conflicts have not been supplied. Each proposal is conditional on his lawful eligibility by the simulation date. When the historical argument occurred in 1993, Stone cannot be placed retroactively at that argument: a lawful 1994 resubmission, with any required opportunity for reargument, would be necessary. No party consent or resubmission order is invented. Historical votes appear only in each historical-comparison section; no simulated colleague's vote or opinion assignment is predicted.

# 1. United States v. Castillo

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 92-1279. **Decision date:** January 28, 1994. **Citation:** 14 F.3d 802. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Shirley Wohl Kram. **Historical argument:** September 15, 1993. The simulation concerns the retrial of the firearm count and the associated sentencing and post-trial rulings, not a fresh appeal from every component of the original prosecution. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/14/802/612862/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

During a July 1989 undercover cocaine purchase, Officer Shawn Johnson encountered Castillo and Fernandez. Johnson testified that Fernandez displayed a firearm at his waistband and partially drew it, while Castillo required Johnson to ingest cocaine. No gun was recovered. Johnson was the only eyewitness who supplied the government's firearm account, and the initial arrest report did not mention a gun. The defense challenged both that omission and differences between descriptions of a gun at the waistband and an encounter described as occurring at gunpoint.

An earlier appeal, *United States v. Castillo*, 924 F.2d 1227 (2d Cir. 1991), reversed the firearm conviction because of prejudicial expert testimony and required reconsideration of a sentencing enhancement on the remaining convictions. At the firearm retrial the government called officers who repeated Johnson's earlier reports. The district court admitted the statements for rehabilitation, with instructions limiting their substantive use. Castillo was again convicted under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c).

Castillo also asserted that his attorney refused to let him testify, although he wanted to do so. His affidavit claimed that he believed counsel's decision was conclusive. He had been told at the first trial that the right to testify was his. A delayed Rule 33 motion raised the attorney issue. The sentencing record also supported a disputed two-level supervisory adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c). These are distinct questions: timeliness of the motion, the constitutional allocation of the decision to testify, evidentiary error, jury instructions, and guideline application. *Castillo*, 14 F.3d at 803-08.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final criminal judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; the district court's prosecution jurisdiction comes from 18 U.S.C. § 3231. Evidentiary rulings receive abuse-of-discretion review, with independent review of the governing evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional trial error requires a prejudice inquiry under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) and *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), not merely a finding that the remaining evidence could support conviction.

The then-existing Rule 33 allowed seven days for an ordinary new-trial motion and a longer period for newly discovered evidence. *United States v. Dukes*, 727 F.2d 34, 39 (2d Cir. 1984), prevents relabeling attorney conduct already known to the defendant as newly discovered evidence. That procedural bar does not itself adjudicate every possible collateral ineffective-assistance claim. The reasonable-doubt charge is assessed as a whole. The legal scope of § 3B1.1 is reviewed independently; its supported factual findings receive clear-error review.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

The government's strongest evidentiary argument is that the defense created an apparent inconsistency, not merely a general credibility attack. *United States v. Pierre*, 781 F.2d 329, 331-33 (2d Cir. 1986), permits a consistent statement with rehabilitative force beyond repetition to explain a claimed inconsistency. *United States v. Brennan*, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986), likewise recognizes that limited use. But *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), does not permit a party simply to multiply its witness's accusations through additional mouths.

The prior statements did not qualify for substantive admission under Rule 801(d)(1)(B): the asserted motive to explain Johnson's cocaine ingestion already existed when he made them. The difficult question is consequently whether repeating the waistband account actually clarifies the difference between that account and the gunpoint description. Repetition can show consistency, but that alone is not the independent rehabilitative function required by the precedents. Its practical effect also matters where the prosecutor relies on the repeated accusation as corroboration that a firearm existed.

The right to testify is personal. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987), identifies its constitutional importance. Prior advice that the right exists is relevant, but is not logically identical to a finding that Castillo understood he could override counsel at the later trial. Conversely, his later affidavit does not prove unconstitutional interference without attention to the trial record and counsel's account. The court should not decide a contested attorney-client episode through appellate factfinding.

The objection to an “abiding conviction” formulation is substantially answered by *United States v. Torres*, 901 F.2d 205, 242-43 (2d Cir. 1990). *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), does not make every challenged phrase a structural error; it addresses a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction. The supervisory adjustment has a different foundation: evidence of Castillo's handling of cocaine and transaction proceeds permits an inference of an organizing role, subject to *United States v. Beaulieau*, 959 F.2d 375, 380 (2d Cir. 1992). Mere possession of money or drugs, without a role-related inference, would not automatically suffice.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose reversal of the firearm conviction for a new trial because the repeated reports lacked a sufficiently distinct rehabilitative purpose and the error cannot confidently be treated as harmless. His reason is not distrust of police testimony as a category. It is the evidentiary structure of this prosecution: one eyewitness supplied the disputed firearm evidence, the weapon was not recovered, the initial report omitted it, and other officers' repetition risked supplying the corroboration the investigation itself lacked.

*Pierre* should be applied, not repudiated. A prior statement may resolve ambiguity when its context explains why two accounts are compatible. Here the central defect is that additional witnesses repeated the same substantive accusation. Calling that use rehabilitation does not explain how it resolves the asserted discrepancy. Nor does a limiting instruction necessarily neutralize a presentation that invites the jury to count multiple official witnesses as corroboration of a single source. The earlier *Castillo* decision makes particular care appropriate because it already identified the danger of bolstering the contested gun evidence; it does not conclusively decide harmlessness at a different trial.

Under *Kotteakos*, Stone should examine the actual prosecutorial use of the statements and their importance to this verdict. The government cannot prevail merely by identifying sufficient admissible evidence. Where the disputed element is supplied principally by the witness being bolstered, repetition by fellow officers can materially affect the jury's evaluation. A new trial, rather than acquittal, follows because the proposed error concerns the manner of proof, not a determination that no rational jury could find the firearm element.

Stone should separately uphold the Rule 33 timeliness ruling and reject the reasonable-doubt objection. He should not convert the time bar into a merits holding that counsel could prohibit testimony, or hold that prior advice necessarily establishes a later knowing waiver. The renewed trial may eliminate the practical need to resolve that episode here; any surviving collateral claim requires its own lawful channel and developed record. He can also accept the district court's supported supervisory findings while requiring resentencing to reflect the disposition of the firearm count. This approach implements the Master Jurisprudence's rules on actual-trial harmlessness, personal trial rights, review of facts, and separation of error from remedy, §§ 8-9 and Criminal Procedure and Punishment.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** inadmissible repetitive rehabilitation, prejudicial on this record, requiring a new firearm trial. The opinion may explain the limits of *Pierre* but should not prohibit all prior consistent statements offered for a genuinely clarifying purpose.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** reversal resting on the particular prosecutorial use and centrality of these statements, without an exhaustive account of all permissible rehabilitation. A remand for an attorney-interference inquiry would be independently acceptable only if that issue remains necessary and is reachable despite the Rule 33 problem; it is not an automatic substitute for the evidentiary remedy.

**Nonjoin limit:** treating repetition as corroboration solely because multiple officers heard the original witness, equating sufficiency with harmlessness, or announcing that counsel controls the defendant's personal choice to testify. Stone also should not join an acquittal mandate unsupported by an insufficiency holding.

# 2. United States v. Anderson

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-1097. **Decision date:** February 17, 1994. **Citation:** 15 F.3d 278. **Court below:** District of Connecticut, Judge T. F. Gilroy Daly. **Historical argument:** November 1, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/15/278/536351/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Anderson pleaded guilty to credit-card fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1029(a)(2), received six months' imprisonment and three years of supervised release, and began supervised release in August 1991. His conditions included restrictions on controlled substances and firearms and participation in substance-abuse treatment. The probation officer sought revocation in October 1992. The district court found violations in January 1993.

Chapter Seven's policy statements recommended six to twelve months' imprisonment. The statutory ceiling was twenty-four months. The district court imposed seventeen months, expressly emphasizing the need for structured drug and psychological treatment and access to an intensive institutional program. The record also reflected poor cooperation with supervision. The appeal therefore requires separation of a potentially proper custodial response to violations from an impermissible reason for selecting its duration. *Anderson*, 15 F.3d at 279-81.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The court reviews a final revocation sentence under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the applicable provisions of 18 U.S.C. § 3742. The district court's authority to revoke comes from § 3583(e). The principal legal questions are whether imprisonment following revocation may be lengthened to provide rehabilitation and whether Chapter Seven's recommended range is binding.

Statutory meaning and the legal force of the policy statements are reviewed independently. Supported factual findings receive deference, but a legally impermissible sentencing objective cannot be insulated as discretion. Review must use the statutory sentencing regime existing in February 1994, not later advisory-Guidelines or rehabilitation decisions.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 3582(a) directs the sentencing court, when determining whether and for how long to impose imprisonment, to recognize that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting correction and rehabilitation. Section 994(k) of Title 28 contains a related direction to the Sentencing Commission. Conversely, § 3583(e) incorporates several § 3553(a) considerations, including the defendant's need for medical care or other correctional treatment under § 3553(a)(2)(D).

The government's strongest reading treats revocation as a distinct statutory operation. Section 3583(e) permits the court to require imprisonment for all or part of the authorized period and expressly incorporates treatment considerations. It does not expressly incorporate § 3582(a). Rehabilitation can legitimately affect the original choice and length of supervision, so the government argues that the same consideration remains relevant when supervision fails.

Anderson's stronger structural response is that treatment remains relevant to many choices under § 3583(e), including modification or extension of conditions and nonprison supervision. Giving those words effect does not require construing them as authority to lengthen incarceration for treatment. The difference between “impose” and “require” is a weak basis for withdrawing a policy Congress attached to decisions about imprisonment. *United States v. Maier*, 975 F.2d 944, 946-48 (2d Cir. 1992), recognized rehabilitation's relevance to a noncustodial disposition while emphasizing the statutory rejection of imprisonment as its instrument. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989), describes that reform in the sentencing system's design.

Chapter Seven presents a separate question. *United States v. Bermudez*, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992), treats its revocation recommendations as advisory. *Williams v. United States*, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), addresses policy statements that operate with governing guidelines; it does not convert every Commission policy statement into a mandatory guideline. The distinction is compatible with *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), concerning authoritative guideline commentary. Consequently, an otherwise lawful sentence need not fall within six to twelve months merely because that is the Chapter Seven recommendation.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose vacatur of the seventeen-month sentence and remand for resentencing without using additional incarceration to obtain rehabilitation. He should accept the advisory character of Chapter Seven. These conclusions are complementary: the court has genuine discretion outside the policy-statement range, but not discretion to use an objective Congress has excluded from the decision to imprison.

The opinion should begin with the actual sentencing choice. Anderson was not merely directed toward treatment while otherwise serving an independently selected term. The judge used the need for a structured program as a reason for choosing seventeen months. The inquiry therefore cannot be resolved by observing that treatment is desirable or that § 3553(a)(2)(D) is relevant somewhere in the proceeding. It is relevant to the available measures Congress authorized for that purpose.

Section 3583(e)'s treatment cross-reference has substantial work to do even if prison duration cannot be increased for rehabilitation. Conditions can require treatment; the period and structure of supervision can reflect treatment needs; a court selecting lawful custody for other reasons can recommend available care. The construction Stone proposes preserves all of those functions. The government's alternative, in contrast, makes a change in the label of the sentencing proceeding sufficient to restore imprisonment's rehabilitative rationale, despite § 3582(a)'s direct instruction about imprisonment itself.

Stone should not announce a constitutional right to treatment outside prison or hold that rehabilitation is irrelevant to revocation as a whole. Nor should he direct a twelve-month sentence. Poor compliance, protection of the public, deterrence, and other incorporated lawful considerations may support a term above the Chapter Seven recommendation. The proper mandate requires the district court to exercise that discretion again, explain the permissible reasons, and refrain from selecting extra months because a particular program requires them.

This is an interpretation of the statutes available in 1994, informed by *Maier* and the distinction between imprisonment and other sentencing measures. It does not depend on the later Supreme Court treatment of rehabilitation. The Master Jurisprudence's Sentencing domain supports full use of lawful discretion, but § 3582(a), not Stone's preference, supplies the proposed limit.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the statutory prohibition on using prison to promote rehabilitation applies to the selection and duration of custody following revocation; vacate and remand while recognizing the advisory Chapter Seven range.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** decide only that this sentence cannot stand where the court expressly selected its additional duration to secure institutional treatment, reserving incidental treatment recommendations and mixed records not presented here.

**Nonjoin limit:** affirming on hypothetical permissible reasons without confronting the stated rationale, making the Chapter Seven range mandatory, or forbidding all consideration of treatment in the administration of supervised release.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was **James L. Oakes, Amalya L. Kearse, and Frank X. Altimari**. Stone provisionally replaces **Altimari**, producing **Oakes, Kearse, and Stone**. The November 1993 argument requires the resubmission qualification stated above; eligibility by February 17, 1994 is conditional.

## Historical disposition and comparison

**Historical result:** affirmed. **Author:** Oakes. **Join:** Altimari. **Separate opinion:** Kearse dissented from permitting rehabilitation-based imprisonment following revocation. Stone's proposed statutory construction aligns with that concern and differs from the historical judgment. His agreement that Chapter Seven is advisory does not imply approval of the sentence's rehabilitative basis. *Anderson*, 15 F.3d at 281-86.

# 3. Latsis v. Chandris, Inc.

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-7704. **Decision date:** March 24, 1994. **Citation:** 20 F.3d 45. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Loretta A. Preska. **Historical argument:** December 6, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/20/45/523011/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Antonios Latsis worked as Chandris's fleet electronics superintendent, servicing a passenger fleet of approximately five or six vessels. His duties included shoreside work and voyages. The evidence concerning vessel time differed sharply: Latsis described spending roughly seventy-two percent of his time aboard ship, while his supervisor estimated approximately ten percent.

In May 1989 Latsis developed an eye problem aboard the S.S. Galileo during a voyage from Baltimore to Bermuda. The ship's physician suspected a detached retina but did not arrange immediate specialist treatment. Latsis alleged that the delay caused the substantial loss of central vision in his right eye. Negligence and causation remained contested and were not resolved by the jury.

After returning to work, Latsis sailed with Galileo to Germany and spent approximately six months supervising electronic work during a major conversion. The project included substantial work to propulsion, machinery, and accommodation. The vessel returned to service as Meridian. At trial the parties agreed that Latsis's work contributed to the fleet's function or mission; the dispute was whether his relationship to the vessels made him a Jones Act seaman.

The jury was instructed in terms of permanent assignment or substantial work aboard vessels, but was told to exclude the German drydock period because Galileo was out of navigation. Latsis objected to that exclusion. The jury found that he was not a seaman and therefore did not reach malpractice, causation, or damages. Judgment was entered in June 1993. *Latsis*, 20 F.3d at 47-50.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The action arises under the then-codified Jones Act, 46 U.S.C. app. § 688, with appellate jurisdiction over the final judgment under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The correctness of the governing seaman standard and of the challenged legal instruction is reviewed independently. Evidence supporting competing inferences belongs to the jury.

The preservation distinction is important. Latsis objected to categorical exclusion of the drydock service. He did not preserve every challenge to the broader seaman-status charge. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 51 governs; unpreserved civil instructional error requires the demanding circuit standard described in *Schaafsma v. Morin Vermont Corp.*, 802 F.2d 629, 636-37 (2d Cir. 1986), and *Abou-Khadra v. Mahshie*, 4 F.3d 1071, 1078 (2d Cir. 1993). Rule 61 separately requires prejudice. A perceived opportunity to improve maritime doctrine does not dispense with either requirement.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander*, 498 U.S. 337, 353-55 (1991), eliminates a requirement that a seaman aid in navigation. It does not make every employee who performs a useful task for a vessel a seaman. *Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni*, 502 U.S. 81 (1991), likewise prevents occupational labels from conclusively excluding otherwise qualifying maritime workers.

Circuit decisions including *Salgado v. M. J. Rudolph Corp.*, 514 F.2d 750, 755 (2d Cir. 1975), *Klarman v. Santini*, 503 F.2d 29, 33 (2d Cir. 1974), and *Harney v. William M. Moore Building Corp.*, 359 F.2d 649, 654 (2d Cir. 1966), require a sufficiently substantial, more or less permanent relationship to a vessel or fleet. *Weiss v. Central Railroad Co.*, 235 F.2d 309 (2d Cir. 1956), is an older reminder that mixed shore and vessel duties do not themselves defeat status. The Fifth Circuit's formulation in *Offshore Co. v. Robison*, 266 F.2d 769 (5th Cir. 1959), is persuasive, not binding.

Chandris's strongest argument is that a major reconstruction takes a vessel out of navigation and that land-based ship-repair work cannot manufacture seaman status. *West v. United States*, 361 U.S. 118, 121 (1959), and *McKinley v. All Alaskan Seafoods, Inc.*, 980 F.2d 567 (9th Cir. 1992), support careful attention to the character of the vessel and work during reconstruction.

Latsis's stronger distinction concerns the question for which the repair period is offered. His injury occurred while Galileo was operating at sea. The subsequent assignment bears on whether his employment relationship was an enduring fleet assignment rather than an occasional shoreside service call. Whether a vessel is in navigation for liability arising during a particular repair operation is not necessarily identical to whether every day of an employee's continuing assignment must be erased from the evidence of that relationship. The scale of the conversion remains relevant; categorical exclusion risks preventing the jury from considering the relationship as a whole.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose a new trial on the preserved drydock-instruction issue. The holding should protect the jury's assessment of an enduring vessel relationship without declaring Latsis a seaman as a matter of law or imposing a numerical threshold absent from governing law.

The Jones Act protects a maritime employment relationship, not merely a job title. Useful shipboard work is necessary but not sufficient. Conversely, a fleet employee does not necessarily become a stranger to the fleet each time a vessel undergoes a lengthy refit. The proper inquiry considers the character, continuity, and substantiality of the actual assignment. Here Latsis sailed with the vessel to the repair location, supervised work connected to its return to service, and continued his fleet employment afterward. Those facts could matter even if the vessel was temporarily out of navigation.

Chandris must remain free to argue that the six-month conversion was qualitatively land-based and that Latsis's ordinary work lacked the requisite vessel connection. The competing ten-percent and seventy-two-percent accounts should not be resolved on appeal. But an instruction excluding the entire conversion period makes one legal characterization conclusive before the jury evaluates what that assignment shows. On this record, the period was sufficiently substantial to make its exclusion prejudicial to the status determination.

Stone should resist treating every imperfection in the broader charge as plain error. *Wilander* does not itself answer every question about the necessary relationship, and the presence of an important doctrinal issue is not a substitute for preservation. The preserved error supplies a sufficient path to a new trial. The mandate should direct a charge that distinguishes contribution to a vessel's mission from the necessary employment connection, permits relevant evidence of the continuing fleet relationship, and leaves genuine factual disputes to the jury.

An older illustration, Judge Learned Hand's *The J. S. Warden*, 175 F. 314, 315 (S.D.N.Y. 1910), emphasizes function in the vessel's work rather than traditional nautical titles. That district-court reasoning is historically useful but does not displace circuit requirements concerning connection and navigation. Nor may Stone import the later Supreme Court disposition of this litigation or its numerical guidance into a March 1994 opinion. The proposed analysis follows the Master Jurisprudence's ordinary-law, preservation, and record rules rather than creating a special solicitude test.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** a categorical direction to disregard this continuing drydock assignment was erroneous and prejudicial; order a new trial on seaman status and, if established, the remaining claims.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** reserve the general status of major-conversion work and hold only that this employee's continuous fleet relationship could not be assessed by automatically excluding that evidence. The jury may still give the period limited weight.

**Nonjoin limit:** directing judgment that Latsis is a seaman, imposing a fixed percentage unsupported by then-governing law, treating all ship repair as qualifying service, or bypassing Rule 51 because a broader doctrinal restatement would be attractive.

The historical panel was **James L. Oakes, Amalya L. Kearse, and Richard J. Cardamone**. Stone provisionally replaces **Cardamone**, producing **Oakes, Kearse, and Stone**. The December 1993 argument requires lawful resubmission in 1994; no historical participation by Stone is assumed.

## Historical disposition and comparison

**Historical result:** judgment vacated and case remanded for a new trial. **Author:** Oakes. **Join:** Cardamone. **Separate opinion:** Kearse dissented, disputing the basis for reversal under the preservation and drydock issues. Stone's proposed judgment is the same, but his proposed rationale concentrates on the preserved error and does not automatically adopt every broader formulation in the historical majority. *Latsis*, 20 F.3d at 50-57.

# 4. Whalen v. County of Fulton

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-7457. **Decision date:** March 29, 1994. **Citation:** 19 F.3d 828. **Court below:** Northern District of New York, Judge Frederick J. Scullin, Jr. **Historical argument:** November 22, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/19/828/628533/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

George and Elizabeth Whalen, adoptive parents of Michael, challenged child-welfare authorities' placement of Michael's biological sister, Elizabeth, with another family. They sued the counties and officials involved, alleging interference with association, access to courts, and substantive and procedural due process, together with state-law claims. The materials describe no established custodial relationship between the Whalens and the sister comparable to an existing parent-child household.

Individual officials sought summary judgment on qualified-immunity grounds. The district court denied the motion without prejudice to renewal after discovery. The appeal concerns that interlocutory order, not a final judgment finding a constitutional right to joint sibling placement. The institutional defendants' liability and state-law causes of action cannot be disposed of simply by deciding an individual officer's immunity. *Whalen*, 19 F.3d at 829-31.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The threshold is appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The district court has federal-question jurisdiction over the pleaded § 1983 claims, but that does not establish a right to immediate appellate review.

*Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp.*, 337 U.S. 541, 546-47 (1949), and *Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay*, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978), require an interlocutory collateral order conclusively resolving an important question separate from the merits that would be effectively unreviewable after final judgment. *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511, 526-30 (1985), permits immediate review of a denial of qualified immunity insofar as it presents a legal question. *Cartier v. Lussier*, 955 F.2d 841, 844 (2d Cir. 1992), applies that legal-question requirement.

Whether the order meets these conditions is reviewed independently. The court should decide that question before evaluating whether sibling-placement rights were clearly established. No later Supreme Court refinement of immunity-appeal jurisdiction belongs in the March 1994 analysis.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

The officials' strongest position is functional. Qualified immunity protects against trial and burdensome discovery, not merely damages. A court could defeat *Mitchell* by labeling every legal rejection “without prejudice” while requiring officials to bear the very litigation burdens immunity is meant to avoid. If the pleaded facts, taken as true, identify no clearly established right, additional discovery may be unnecessary.

The opposing jurisdictional argument is that the district court has not conclusively rejected immunity. It deferred resolution, preserved renewal before trial, and did not enter an order finally deciding the legal defense. Under *Cohen* and *Coopers & Lybrand*, provisional treatment is not necessarily an appealable collateral order. Immediate review of every scheduling decision involving an immunity motion would expand a narrow exception to finality.

The underlying merits illustrate why the officials' concern is substantial, but do not themselves confer appellate jurisdiction. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), requires clearly established law, not the possibility that a court might establish a right in this litigation. *Golino v. City of New Haven*, 950 F.2d 864, 870 (2d Cir. 1991), likewise demands attention to what a reasonable official could understand. *Smith v. Organization of Foster Families for Equality & Reform*, 431 U.S. 816 (1977), does not establish a categorical right of adoptive parents to obtain placement of another biological sibling. *Rivera v. Marcus*, 696 F.2d 1016 (2d Cir. 1982), involved a materially different, existing family relationship. Those distinctions make immunity a serious defense, not an appellate jurisdictional shortcut.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose dismissal of this appeal because the particular order did not conclusively resolve the immunity defense. He should make the holding narrow and acknowledge that labels cannot be decisive in a future case where a supposed deferral in fact finally rejects a purely legal entitlement to immunity.

The starting point is the order actually entered. It left renewal available after development of the record. The court of appeals should not convert that provisional ruling into a final rejection merely because it can identify a powerful merits argument for the officials. Otherwise the jurisdictional inquiry becomes circular: whenever an immunity claim appears strong enough, the court would assume authority to adjudicate it. *Cohen* requires conclusiveness independently of the apparent merits.

At the same time, the opinion should not endorse unlimited discovery or suggest that the district court can postpone legal immunity questions until trial as a matter of course. *Mitchell* and *Harlow* give immunity a procedural function. The district court should distinguish facts genuinely necessary to determine what the officials did and knew from a purely legal question about the right asserted. A plaintiff cannot overcome qualified immunity merely by predicting that discovery may persuade a court to recognize a previously unestablished right.

Because Stone's proposed disposition is jurisdictional, those observations should explain the boundary of the holding, not become a merits mandate granting or denying immunity. The opinion should not adjudicate the Whalens' asserted family right, reject all sibling-association claims, or dismiss municipal and state-law counts. Each would exceed the reason the court may act at this stage.

Stone's family-integrity commitments do not establish a right to placement of a child with a particular adoptive household. Existing family relationships, the state's interference with them, and prospective placement decisions require different analysis. His position here follows the Master Jurisprudence's decisional order, source separation, and immunity rules: an important constitutional interest neither supplies interlocutory jurisdiction nor defeats a clearly established-law requirement.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** dismiss because this provisional order fails collateral-order conclusiveness, expressly reserving orders that functionally and conclusively deny a legal immunity defense.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** a short jurisdictional disposition limited to this order's express availability of pretrial renewal, without discussing the ultimate scope of family rights.

**Nonjoin limit:** deciding the merits while professing to lack jurisdiction; holding that the words “without prejudice” invariably defeat immediate review; or implying that recognition of a new constitutional right would by itself defeat retrospective qualified immunity. A merits immunity ruling would require a separately justified conclusion that the appeal is properly before the court.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was **Thomas J. Meskill, John M. Walker, Jr., and Constance Baker Motley**, a senior Southern District judge sitting by designation. Stone provisionally replaces **Motley**, producing **Meskill, Walker, and Stone**. # 5. Walker v. Bates

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-2107. **Decision date:** April 29, 1994. **Citation:** 23 F.3d 652. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Judge David G. Larimer. **Historical argument:** December 15, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/23/652/482392/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Southport inmate Robert Walker was charged in December 1990 with possessing a metal rod and excess bedding. He maintained that the property had been left by a prior occupant of the cell. At a Tier III disciplinary hearing, hearing officer Bates refused Walker's requests to call three correction officers as witnesses and imposed 120 days in the special housing unit. Punishment began immediately.

Donald Selsky reversed the disciplinary decision in February 1991 and authorized a prompt rehearing. No rehearing occurred. Walker nevertheless remained in special housing until February 22. He then sought damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The district court dismissed the claim against Bates on the theory that administrative reversal cured the earlier procedural defect. Other named defendants had been dismissed and are not automatically restored by resolving this appeal. *Walker*, 23 F.3d at 653-55.

The published account contains an internally inconsistent year for one district-court order relative to the magistrate's recommendation. The substantive sequence is clear, but this brief does not silently invent a corrected docket date.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The federal civil-rights action falls within federal-question jurisdiction, and dismissal with prejudice is a final decision under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. Review of the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is de novo under the pleading law existing in 1994, including *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41 (1957). The court takes the properly pleaded account favorably to Walker; it does not find that every alleged deprivation occurred or that damages are established.

The question is whether administrative reversal after punishment has begun defeats the constitutional claim as a matter of law. Whether the witnesses could lawfully have been excluded, whether Bates is qualifiedly immune, and what injury he caused are separate questions that the asserted administrative-cure rule does not resolve.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), recognizes a qualified right to call witnesses at prison disciplinary hearings, subject to institutional safety and other legitimate limits. *Sher v. Coughlin*, 739 F.2d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 1984), and *McCann v. Coughlin*, 698 F.2d 112, 121-22 (2d Cir. 1983), supply the relevant pre-1995 framework for protected interests in punitive confinement. Later changes to prison-liberty doctrine cannot be used here.

Bates's strongest argument treats the hearing and administrative appeal as one integrated process. A functioning correction procedure identified and removed the error, and imposing damages despite correction may discourage prompt administrative review. *Young v. Hoffman*, 970 F.2d 1154, 1155-56 (2d Cir. 1992), supports the absence of a completed deprivation when administrative reversal occurs before the prisoner serves the disciplinary sentence.

That temporal qualification matters. Walker alleges that punishment began before review and continued for a substantial period. *Patterson v. Coughlin*, 761 F.2d 886, 893 (2d Cir. 1985), rejects the proposition that later correction necessarily erases a completed deprivation. *Russell v. Scully*, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 1994), must be read for the immunity and detention questions it actually addressed, not as a universal administrative-cure rule.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193 (1985), establishes that prison disciplinary adjudicators receive qualified, not absolute, immunity. Its procedural facts are relevant by analogy, but its holding does not itself conclusively decide every administrative-reversal question. *Ponte v. Real*, 471 U.S. 491 (1985), permits officials to justify witness exclusions in subsequent litigation; a federal requirement of contemporaneous written reasons should not be invented. Finally, *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), distinguishes the denial of process from proof of compensable injury.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose reversal of the dismissal and remand. A disciplinary appeal can prevent a deprivation if it corrects the error before punishment. It can also terminate an ongoing deprivation and mitigate injury. It cannot make time already spent under an allegedly unlawful punitive order disappear.

That conclusion does not constitutionalize every violation of a prison rule. Walker must establish a protected interest and a denial of the process the Constitution requires. The witness right is qualified. Bates may offer legitimate safety, relevance, duplication, or other permissible grounds, and the district court must consider them under *Wolff* and *Ponte*. The appellate court should not infer such grounds merely to rescue dismissal, but neither should it foreclose grounds the governing doctrine permits the official to establish.

The proposal rests on the character and timing of the deprivation. Once the hearing imposed and immediately activated punitive confinement, the question was whether adequate procedure supported that confinement. An administrative remedy capable only of later reversal does not necessarily furnish the predeprivation protection required for an established disciplinary process. The integrated-process argument has force in *Young*, where the sentence never operated; it loses that force where the prisoner has already borne the punishment.

The remedy must remain disciplined. Reversal does not direct a damages award for every day in special housing. The court must distinguish detention caused by Bates's order from any independent lawful basis for confinement and determine who was responsible for continued confinement after reversal. Under *Carey*, actual damages require causal proof; an established procedural violation without proven compensatory injury may support nominal damages. Qualified immunity remains governed by the law clearly established when Bates acted, not by the clarity supplied by this appeal.

Stone should expressly identify *Cleavinger*'s limited holding, rather than improve the argument by overstating Supreme Court authority. His broader proposed circuit rule is that reversal after disciplinary punishment begins is not an automatic defense to an accrued procedural-due-process claim. That is a principled account of *Patterson* and *Young*, not an exemption from prison administration. It follows the Master Jurisprudence's distinction among rights, responsible defendants, immunity, causation, and remedies.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** administrative reversal does not categorically extinguish a claim for disciplinary punishment already imposed without constitutionally sufficient process; reverse and remand.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** decide only the pleaded facts, in which confinement began immediately and a substantial period preceded reversal, leaving prompt interim review under materially different arrangements open.

**Nonjoin limit:** automatic damages for the full confinement period, strict liability for every witness refusal, treating *Cleavinger* as a direct holding on administrative cure, or importing later prison-liberty restrictions into the April 1994 decision.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was **Wilfred Feinberg, Roger J. Miner, and J. Daniel Mahoney**. Stone provisionally replaces **Mahoney**, producing **Feinberg, Miner, and Stone**. The December 1993 argument requires lawful 1994 resubmission.

## Historical disposition and comparison

**Historical result:** reversed and remanded. **Author:** Miner. **Join:** Feinberg. **Separate opinion:** Mahoney dissented. Stone's proposal reaches the historical judgment but independently emphasizes the distinction between *Cleavinger*'s actual immunity holding and the separate circuit-law administrative-cure issue. No liability or simulated panel consensus is assumed. *Walker*, 23 F.3d at 655-59.

# 6. United States v. Johns

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 92-1775. **Decision date:** June 13, 1994. **Citation:** 27 F.3d 31. **Court below:** District of Vermont, Judge Franklin S. Billings, Jr. **Historical argument:** April 19, 1994. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/27/31/542110/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Johns pleaded guilty to a cocaine conspiracy in September 1992. The prosecution included three identified sales, and the sentencing inquiry also examined other narcotics dealings. During the presentence investigation, Johns denied some additional transactions and purchases. The government possessed contrary statements and other evidence. The appeal does not depend on declaring the denials truthful or immaterial; their falsity and materiality were not the disputed appellate premise.

The district court imposed a two-level obstruction-of-justice enhancement under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1, denied an acceptance-of-responsibility reduction under § 3E1.1, and imposed fifty-one months' imprisonment. Initial appellate counsel sought to withdraw under *Anders*; replacement counsel addressed the guideline issue. The important distinction is between denial of guilt, affirmative obstruction, and acceptance of responsibility. They are not interchangeable classifications. *Johns*, 27 F.3d at 32-34.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final sentence is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and 18 U.S.C. § 3742. Whether § 3C1.1 and its commentary authorize the enhancement is a legal question reviewed de novo. Under *United States v. Perdomo*, 927 F.2d 111, 118 (2d Cir. 1991), guideline interpretation is not insulated by deference to sentencing findings. The remand must also address acceptance of responsibility on its own terms.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Application Note 1 to § 3C1.1 excludes a defendant's denial of guilt, apart from perjury, and certain refusals to admit guilt or provide information from the obstruction enhancement. The commentary also directs favorable evaluation of the defendant's statements. Application Note 3(h), however, identifies materially false information to a probation officer as potential obstruction. The provisions must be read together, not by allowing the general example to eliminate the explicit qualification.

The government's strongest argument is straightforward: materially false answers mislead the sentencing investigation and are not compelled by the Fifth Amendment. An accused can remain silent but has no constitutional entitlement to lie. *United States v. Rodriguez*, 943 F.2d 215, 218 (2d Cir. 1991), upholds an enhancement for lies about prior convictions; likely discovery of the truth does not make deception immaterial.

Johns's response is textual rather than constitutional. The Commission chose to protect some denials beyond the minimum compelled by the Fifth Amendment. *United States v. Austin*, 17 F.3d 27, 31 (2d Cir. 1994), recognizes that difference. *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), requires faithful attention to authoritative commentary. A denial does not cease to be a denial because the defendant states it in a complete sentence rather than uttering the word “no.” At the same time, fabrication, false accusations against others, perjury, and lies about collateral sentencing facts need not receive the same treatment.

*United States v. Stroud*, 893 F.2d 504, 507 (2d Cir. 1990), also requires attention to the willful obstructive conduct the guideline addresses. *Rodriguez* remains distinguishable because criminal-history deception is not simply denial of the misconduct for which the defendant is being sentenced. Acceptance presents a separate inquiry under § 3E1.1 and *United States v. Reyes*, 9 F.3d 275, 279 (2d Cir. 1993): removal of an obstruction enhancement does not compel a reduction for acceptance.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose vacatur of the sentence and remand without the obstruction adjustment on the stated basis, together with reconsideration of acceptance of responsibility under the proper distinction between the offense of conviction and other alleged conduct.

The guideline's denial exception has to constrain something. Nearly every false denial during a presentence interview could be described as materially false information to the probation officer. If that description always controlled, the exception would vanish precisely where it is most likely to matter. The sound reading treats the enumerated example as subject to the express qualification, while preserving enhancement for independently obstructive conduct outside that qualification.

The government is correct that the Constitution does not authorize lying. Stone should say so. But that proposition answers a different question from whether the Commission has instructed courts to withhold this particular additional punishment for a defined class of unsworn denials. The mandatory Guidelines in 1994 must be enforced in the defendant's favor as well as the government's. Refusing to apply the commentary because its protection exceeds the constitutional minimum would substitute judicial policy for the governing sentencing source.

The line is administrable without making every interview lie immune. A defendant who merely denies his own alleged criminal conduct invokes the commentary's particular protection. A defendant who manufactures documents, falsely implicates an innocent third party, commits perjury, or conceals prior convictions engages in conduct whose obstructive character does not depend solely on denial of guilt. The opinion should not decide unpresented variations more broadly than needed, but it should identify why *Rodriguez* remains intact.

Acceptance of responsibility should then be reconsidered separately. A defendant may avoid an obstruction enhancement yet fail to demonstrate affirmative acceptance. The sentencing court must identify what Johns falsely denied and how that conduct relates to the offense of conviction under the guideline version governing his sentence. It cannot mechanically repeat the obstruction analysis or mechanically award the reduction because the enhancement was removed.

This proposal uses the Master Jurisprudence's source separation and statutory-interpretation principles. Lenity is not needed if text and commentary resolve the issue. No new constitutional privilege or generalized permission to deceive is announced. The mandate should leave other supported sentencing findings intact while requiring a lawful recalculation and an independent acceptance determination.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** Note 1's denial protection limits Note 3(h); these unsworn denials cannot support the stated obstruction adjustment. Remand for recalculation and proper reconsideration of acceptance.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** confine the holding to denials of Johns's own alleged drug transactions and expressly reserve materially different affirmative deception.

**Nonjoin limit:** a constitutional right to lie, immunity for fabricated evidence or perjury, an automatic acceptance reduction, or treating all false information to probation as outside the commentary's express protection.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was **Frank X. Altimari, Joseph M. McLaughlin, and Dennis Jacobs**. Stone provisionally replaces **Jacobs**, producing **Altimari, McLaughlin, and Stone**. The argument occurred in 1994; Stone's eligibility by that sitting remains conditional on the unsupplied commission date.

## Historical disposition and comparison

**Historical result:** sentence vacated and remanded. **Author:** Jacobs. **Join:** McLaughlin. **Separate opinion:** Altimari dissented. Stone's proposal agrees with the historical judgment while emphasizing that the result comes from the Commission's commentary, not a constitutional entitlement to deception. The historical division supplies no simulated vote. *Johns*, 27 F.3d at 34-37.


# 7. City of New York v. United States Department of Commerce

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-6183. **Decision date:** August 8, 1994. **Citation:** 34 F.3d 1114. **Court below:** Eastern District of New York, Circuit Judge Joseph M. McLaughlin sitting by designation. **Historical argument:** January 5, 1994. The challenged administrative act is Secretary Robert Mosbacher's July 15, 1991 decision, although successors appear in the appellate caption. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/34/1114/552082/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Cities, states, organizations, individual residents, and intervenors challenged the refusal to adjust the 1990 census to account for differential undercounting. Their interests included representation and distributions of public funds. Wisconsin and Oklahoma opposed adjustment. The controversy concerned whether an enumeration supplemented by a post-enumeration survey, or PES, provided a better population measure, not whether every person could literally be counted without error.

A 1989 stipulation required a fresh consideration of adjustment, development of review guidelines, a survey capable of producing adjusted figures, and an explained decision. The Bureau surveyed approximately 170,000 households, compared the resulting information with the enumeration, and developed adjustment factors for 1,392 population categories. Its estimates indicated an overall undercount of approximately 5.3 million people. The estimated undercount was substantially higher for several racial and ethnic minorities than for non-Hispanic whites. These are survey estimates in the record, not findings of an independently known exact population. *City of New York*, 34 F.3d at 1117-23.

The Secretary acknowledged that adjusted figures would probably improve numerical accuracy nationally and in jurisdictions containing approximately two-thirds of the population. He nevertheless emphasized distributive accuracy, meaning the proportions assigned to different places, uncertainty in local estimates, possible changes in research findings, and the implications for later censuses. The advisory panel divided evenly; the Bureau's director favored adjustment while other senior advisers opposed it. The Secretary expressly disclaimed a belief that the 1990 survey had been manipulated.

After a thirteen-day bench trial, Judge McLaughlin found that adjustment produced a more accurate count for most purposes and that plaintiffs mounted a compelling attack on several aspects of the decision. He nevertheless found the Secretary's choice and application of the eight guidelines not arbitrary or capricious. He considered a focus on distributive accuracy compatible with the census's representative function and stated that disagreement over the better exercise of discretion was insufficient to overturn the decision. Judgment dismissed the complaint. 822 F. Supp. 906, 916, 920-29 (E.D.N.Y. 1993).

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. Federal constitutional and statutory claims invoke § 1331. Representation and funding injuries must be established by the plaintiffs asserting them; the breadth of the coalition neither creates standing nor converts an individualized representational injury into a merely generalized grievance. The reported controversy identifies concrete representational and allocation consequences, not simply disagreement with census policy.

Reviewability must distinguish the constitutional claim from an APA claim. *Franklin v. Massachusetts*, 505 U.S. 788, 800-06 (1992), holds that the President is not an APA agency and that the Secretary's apportionment report is not the final presidential apportionment decision. It nevertheless addresses the constitutional census challenge. A court cannot evade those limits by ordering the President under the APA. Claims concerning non-apportionment uses or independently reviewable administrative acts require their own finality analysis. The constitutional challenge to responsible officials does not disappear merely because the principal apportionment route is outside the APA.

The appropriate constitutional test is a legal question reviewed independently. Bench findings receive Rule 52(a) clear-error review. Neither standard authorizes the appellate court to choose the statistical estimator it considers best. The parties preserved their disagreement about the governing review standard. If relief becomes warranted, the court must identify the affected census use, lawful defendant, and administrable correction rather than ordering an undifferentiated national redistribution.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Article I, § 2 and the Fourteenth Amendment's apportionment provision connect representation to population. *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), *Kirkpatrick v. Preisler*, 394 U.S. 526 (1969), and *Karcher v. Daggett*, 462 U.S. 725, 730-31, 740 (1983), require good-faith efforts toward population equality in congressional districting. They do not directly decide how the federal government must choose between disputed nationwide census estimates. The difference is material: drawing boundaries with accepted population data and estimating the underlying population present different uncertainties and institutional choices.

*Department of Commerce v. Montana*, 503 U.S. 442, 459-66 (1992), recognizes the constitutional constraints and unavoidable compromises in interstate apportionment. *Franklin* examines whether the challenged census judgment is consistent with constitutional language and the goal of equal representation. Neither decision confers unreviewable discretion. Neither commands that any proposed increase in aggregate numerical accuracy automatically defeats concerns about distribution among states and smaller jurisdictions. [Montana](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/503/442/); [Franklin](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/505/788).

Plaintiffs' strongest case is therefore not simply that the survey counted more people. It is that the Secretary demanded superior accuracy at every level, discounted gains affecting most of the population, and preferred speculative risks in future censuses over the demonstrated advantages of the unmanipulated current survey. Those facts could show that the asserted accuracy rationale failed to engage the constitutional task. The government's strongest answer is that numerical and distributive accuracy differ, substantial experts disagreed, and the trial court found the actual guideline choices rational after extensive evidence. A preferable adjustment is not necessarily a constitutionally required adjustment.

Racial impact must be kept separate from those questions. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), and *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-68 (1977), do not permit automatic racial strict scrutiny from unequal effects alone. An apportionment claim can exist without proof of racial purpose, but that does not establish that the state-districting burden of justification transfers unchanged to every federal census judgment. Such a transfer would be a doctrinal extension requiring justification, not an already binding holding.

The statutory argument is independently substantial. Section 141(a) expressly contemplates sampling and surveys; § 195 excepts congressional apportionment from its direction to use sampling when feasible. Plaintiffs read that exception as limiting a mandate, not prohibiting supplementation authorized by § 141. Intervenors read it as preserving a special restriction on apportionment. The 1976 amendments and their explanation favor broader use of sampling, but broad purpose cannot make the express exception disappear. Any mandate compelling adjustment for congressional apportionment must resolve this tension; authorization for every census use cannot simply be assumed.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose a limited vacatur and remand for a distinct constitutional determination, without adopting automatic heightened racial scrutiny or directing use of the PES. The proposed holding is that review of the census's constitutional function cannot end solely with the conclusion that the Secretary's decision survives ordinary administrative review. The court must determine whether the choice reflects a good-faith performance of the representative task, while respecting genuine technical uncertainty and the difference between numerical and distributive accuracy. This application to the present census-choice problem is a proposed circuit development, not a quotation of a Supreme Court census-adjustment rule.

The remand should identify the unresolved work precisely. The district court should assess whether the requirement of improved accuracy at every geographic level was an actual, technically supportable decision criterion; whether the competing loss-function analyses support the stated distributive rationale; and whether the treatment of future manipulation and cooperation concerns rationally served the present constitutional task rather than displacing it. Its existing findings remain findings. The court need not conduct a new census, adopt plaintiffs' statistics, count expert votes, or infer partisan intent from the mere fact that particular states would gain or lose seats.

The strongest objection is that this simply renames a demand for stricter administrative review. Stone should answer it by limiting the inquiry to the constitutional objective and declining to add a least-restrictive-means requirement, an across-the-board government burden of persuasion, or a mandatory methodological checklist. Plaintiffs retain the burden of establishing a constitutional defect. The Secretary may prevail despite a method that is less accurate for some purposes if the record supports a good-faith choice among genuinely competing constitutional and statistical considerations. A court's belief that adjustment would be wiser is insufficient.

At the same time, the finding that plaintiffs made a compelling attack cannot be treated as immaterial solely because the Secretary possesses discretion. The district court should explain whether its findings establish more than disagreement over wisdom under the independent constitutional standard. Because it framed the dispositive inquiry as whether the decision was sufficiently unreasonable to be arbitrary or capricious, a targeted remand is preferable to an appellate finding of bad faith or an appellate endorsement of every rationale.

Stone should reserve the disputed statutory scope unless a correction is otherwise warranted. Before ordering any particular adjustment, the district court must address §§ 141 and 195 for each affected use and the remedial implications of *Franklin*. This preserves his commitments to equal political membership, genuine review, statutory limits, and no conversion of disparate impact into discriminatory purpose. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8, 15, 25; Elections and Democratic Participation; Administrative Law.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** independent constitutional review of the government's good-faith census judgment, with a focused remand on the actual accuracy rationale and no predetermined adjustment. He may explain why an unsupported demand for superiority everywhere would not automatically answer demonstrated improvements affecting most people.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** remand requiring explicit findings and conclusions under *Montana* and *Franklin*, reserving a more comprehensive census standard and every disputed statistical determination. Affirmance would become acceptable if the necessary findings and correct constitutional analysis establish a lawful choice; it is not presently an approved fallback based merely on administrative deference.

**Non-join limits:** automatic strict scrutiny from minority undercount, conclusive constitutional preference for numerical over distributive accuracy, an assumption that the Secretary acted from racial or partisan hostility, disregard of § 195, or an immediate judicial order reallocating congressional seats without the necessary statutory and remedial analysis.

The historical panel was William H. Timbers, Amalya L. Kearse, and Pierre N. Leval. Under the provisional third-seat convention, Stone replaces Leval, leaving Timbers, Kearse, and Stone. The January 1994 argument creates no pre-1994 participation problem, but Stone's commission date still must precede any simulated participation. McLaughlin's presence below is historically explained by his designation after elevation to the circuit; he is not placed on the appellate panel reviewing his own judgment.

## Historical disposition and comparison

The court vacated and remanded, holding that the government must justify the refusal to adjust as essential to a legitimate objective after the plaintiffs' showing. Judge Timbers dissented and would have affirmed on Judge McLaughlin's opinion, also noting contrary Sixth and Seventh Circuit decisions. *City of New York*, 34 F.3d at 1129-31.

His remand preserves plaintiffs' burden and genuine technical discretion while requiring an independently stated constitutional judgment. # 8. McCostis v. Home Insurance Co. of Indiana

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-9283. **Decision date:** August 8, 1994. **Citation:** 31 F.3d 110. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Loretta A. Preska. **Historical argument:** May 2, 1994. This appeal concerns the insurer's duty to defend an attorney, not a determination that the alleged billing fraud occurred or that every resulting liability must be indemnified. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/31/110/591994/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

McCostis was a law-firm partner and outside general counsel to Barr Laboratories, also serving as a director and secretary. After Barr became a client of Winston & Strawn, McCostis allegedly participated with Winston partner Lawrence Fox in a scheme that overbilled Barr by $756,251.75. Approximately $335,000 allegedly went to McCostis; more than $420,000 allegedly went to Fox and his designees. Winston reimbursed Barr and took an assignment of its claims.

Winston's twelve-count complaint against McCostis included federal racketeering, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, and negligent breach theories. It sought liability extending beyond the sum McCostis allegedly received. Home's professional-liability policy promised a defense against covered claims even when allegations were groundless, false, or fraudulent. Its definition of damages included monetary judgments and settlements but excluded fines, statutory penalties, sanctions, and the return or restitution of legal fees, costs, and expenses.

Home initially undertook the defense and then withdrew. McCostis sought declaratory relief. The district court granted Home summary judgment, reading the fee-return exclusion as encompassing the underlying action. The appellate dispute is whether the complaint necessarily seeks excluded restitution, or potentially seeks covered compensation, particularly for money allegedly received by someone else. *McCostis*, 31 F.3d at 111-12.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final declaratory judgment is appealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The insurance dispute proceeds under New York substantive law. Diversity is the apparent jurisdictional basis of this state-law coverage action, but the reported opinion does not set out a complete citizenship analysis; it should not be invented. Section 2201 supplies declaratory relief, not an independent jurisdictional grant. The federal claims in Winston's separate complaint do not themselves federalize the insurance contract dispute.

Summary judgment receives de novo review, with material factual inferences drawn for McCostis. Whether policy language is ambiguous is a legal question. If ambiguity requires extrinsic evidence of contractual intent, factual disputes cannot be settled against the nonmovant. The scope of the duty to defend must be analyzed independently of the eventual duty to indemnify.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

New York law gives the defense promise broad effect. *Colon v. Aetna Life & Casualty Insurance Co.*, 66 N.Y.2d 6, 8-9 (1985), and *Villa Charlotte Bronte, Inc. v. Commercial Union Insurance Co.*, 64 N.Y.2d 846, 848 (1985), require a defense where the complaint potentially brings the claim within coverage. Exclusions are narrowly construed. *Breed v. Insurance Co. of North America*, 46 N.Y.2d 351, 353 (1978). These rules do not create coverage contrary to unambiguous text, but they prevent an insurer from treating every contested allegation as an established excluded loss.

Home's best argument is economic substance. Winston reimbursed the client and sued on assigned rights for improperly collected professional charges. Recasting that recovery as RICO damages, negligence, or joint liability should not convert fee restitution into insured damages. The exclusion would have little practical work if an attorney could obtain coverage simply by attaching a tort label to a demand to refund the client's bill.

McCostis's strongest answer concerns the actual words and amounts. A return or restitution ordinarily suggests restoring what the defendant received or retained. Winston sought substantially more than the money allegedly paid to McCostis, including sums allegedly retained by Fox and others. Liability for another participant's receipt can plausibly be compensatory liability rather than the return of the insured's own fees. Home's interpretation may be sensible, but it is not the only sensible interpretation of the chosen language.

New York's sequence matters. Under *State v. Home Indemnity Co.*, 66 N.Y.2d 669, 671 (1985), an ambiguity is first examined through relevant extrinsic evidence. If that evidence fails to resolve the uncertainty, construction against the insurer becomes appropriate. *Matthews v. American Central Insurance Co.*, 154 N.Y. 449, 456-57 (1897), supplies the older contractual principle; *Ingersoll Milling Machine Co. v. M/V Bodena*, 829 F.2d 293, 306 (2d Cir. 1987), applies that sequence in federal court. Neither side is entitled to bypass a genuinely material intent inquiry merely because it favors an immediate legal conclusion.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose vacatur of summary judgment and remand. The exclusion cannot conclusively be applied to every liability alleged in Winston's complaint without resolving whether it reaches amounts the insured never received. The court should state the ambiguity concretely: does this policy exclude every compensatory judgment traceable to an improperly billed fee, or only the insured's restoration of fees and expenses? Both readings can be explained from the language. The distinction is not a judicial license to insure dishonest profit; it concerns the scope of the insurer's promise to defend against a complaint alleging several kinds of liability.

The district court should consider relevant extrinsic evidence of contractual intent, if available and admissible. If it establishes the parties' meaning without genuine factual dispute, the court may decide accordingly. If uncertainty remains, New York's insurer-against-drafter rule applies, together with the rule that a potentially covered theory triggers the defense obligation. The mandate should not direct findings about McCostis's actual participation in the fraud or about who ultimately retained particular amounts. Those belong to the underlying litigation unless a distinct coverage issue properly requires them.

Stone should expressly answer the argument that artful pleading cannot create coverage. That is correct but insufficient. The potential coverage here rests on the alleged allocation of money and the breadth of the damages demand, not merely on the complaint's labels. Conversely, the inclusion of negligence in one count does not automatically require a defense if every fact alleged falls wholly within an exclusion correctly construed. The task is to compare allegations and contract, not count causes of action.

The ultimate indemnity question remains open. A defense promise covering fraudulent allegations can operate before a finding of intentional wrongdoing without resolving whether public policy or another policy term would bar payment of a particular adjudicated liability. Home's initial undertaking and later withdrawal should not be turned into an unbriefed waiver or estoppel holding. The record identified in the opinion supports a contract-interpretation remand; it does not supply every element of those additional theories.

This is an ordinary state-law insurance dispute. Stone's concern with fair process reinforces attention to the defense promise but does not displace New York contract law or diminish either party's property rights. Master Jurisprudence §§ 8, 12, 24; Markets and Private Power.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the fee-return language is ambiguous as applied to liability for sums received by other participants, requiring the New York sequence of extrinsic evidence followed, if needed, by construction against the insurer.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** a remand confined to the potential coverage of the amounts beyond McCostis's alleged receipts, without defining every form of restitution or deciding indemnity. Stone could join a defense declaration after the relevant uncertainty is resolved against Home under the governing rules; that is a conditional legal consequence, not an approved present judgment.

**Non-join limits:** treating all allegations as proved, equating a defense obligation with indemnification of fraud, ordering coverage solely from a negligence label, or using generalized fairness to rewrite an unambiguous exclusion.

# 9. Pro-Choice Network of Western New York v. Schenck

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 92-7302 and 93-7918. **Decision date:** September 6, 1994. **Citation:** 67 F.3d 359. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Judge Richard J. Arcara. **Historical argument:** March 24, 1994. The simulation is the 1994 panel decision, not the later en banc decision reported at 67 F.3d 377. *Madsen*, decided during the appeal and addressed through supplemental submissions, is available law. [1994 panel opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/67/359/584635/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

The Pro-Choice Network, clinics, and physicians sued individuals and organizations associated with anti-abortion demonstrations in western New York. The complaint filed September 24, 1990 asserted a conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) and state-law claims, including trespass and New York civil-rights claims. The litigation concerned obstruction and harassment affecting access to medical facilities as well as protected advocacy on public sidewalks.

An initial restraining order prohibited obstruction of clinic access. A planned demonstration then proceeded without the anticipated blockade, and the order continued by agreement. The later record nevertheless included crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment, and contempt proceedings against several defendants. The significance of that history was disputed: plaintiffs treated it as proof that ordinary prohibitions had failed; defendants emphasized occasions on which the restraining order protected access and argued that the record did not justify excluding peaceful speakers.

The preliminary injunction prohibited obstruction and trespass, regulated health-endangering noise, and established fifteen-foot zones around entrances and driveways and around people and vehicles seeking access. It permitted two sidewalk counselors within specified areas but required them to cease counseling when an approached person declined it. Thus the decree combined fixed geographic restrictions, moving restrictions, and a listener-triggered command. Those provisions require separate analysis rather than one conclusion about whether anti-abortion protest is permissible.

After *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263 (1993), the district court dismissed the federal claim with leave to amend but retained the pendent state claims and declined to dissolve the injunction. The appeals from the original injunction and refusal to vacate were considered together. The principal district opinions are 799 F. Supp. 1417 (1992) and 828 F. Supp. 1018 (1993).

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The injunction orders are reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1). The federal claim supplied the original federal question; the continued exercise of pendent jurisdiction is assessed under *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), and *Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill*, 484 U.S. 343 (1988). Because this action began before § 1367's effective application to newly filed actions, the analysis should not mechanically invoke that statute as its original jurisdictional source.

*Bray* rejected the proposition that opposition to abortion necessarily supplies the class-based animus required by the pertinent § 1985(3) clause. It also requires the appropriate connection to the protected federal right, rather than incidental interference with interstate travel. Those federal limitations do not automatically extinguish otherwise cognizable state-law claims or invalidate the discretionary retention of a substantially developed case.

The legal First Amendment standard is reviewed independently; factual findings receive clear-error review; the equitable decree is reviewed for abuse of discretion within correct legal limits. The court must exercise the independent constitutional judgment required for speech restrictions, without inventing events not found below. New abstention and state-law arguments not properly raised in the relevant proceedings cannot become grounds for reversal merely because the federal claim changed. The parties remain free to present properly preserved questions of state-law remedial authority on remand.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 764-66 (1994), requires a content-neutral injunction affecting speech to burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest. This is more demanding than ordinary time, place, and manner review, but it is not strict scrutiny simply because an injunction binds identified anti-abortion defendants. Selection based on their conduct is different from suppression based on their viewpoint.

The interests in unobstructed clinic access, public safety, and safe medical treatment are substantial. The First Amendment does not authorize trespass, physical obstruction, assault, or threats. Conversely, a listener's objection to a message does not itself authorize the government to suppress public-sidewalk advocacy. *Boos v. Barry*, 485 U.S. 312 (1988), forecloses treating emotional offense to political expression as a general justification for suppressing it. Restrictions tied to medically consequential noise must be distinguished from restrictions justified merely by distress at the message.

The strongest defense of the zones is operational: a prohibition on actual obstruction may be difficult to enforce once a crowd surrounds a patient or blocks a doorway, and the prior violations can justify preventive space. *Madsen* itself sustained an entrance-area restriction on its record. A court need not await a completed medical injury before protecting access.

The strongest challenge concerns fit. A moving fifteen-foot exclusion can prevent peaceful communication even when a person is walking freely and no doorway is obstructed. A fixed zone may have a stronger access justification, but its location and extent must be connected to the actual obstruction problem. The existence of a numerical limit does not establish tailoring. The cease-and-desist provision goes further still if it prohibits otherwise lawful speech solely because a listener has rejected the message, rather than prohibiting following, intimidation, or interference with departure.

The noise provision is not necessarily invalid for using health and safety language. Read against the record and the medical setting, an objective prohibition on excessively loud sound that endangers patients can be enforceable without requiring a protester to know each patient's private condition. It would be different if enforcement turned on whether clinic occupants disliked the speaker's viewpoint. Rule 65(d)'s specificity requirement and the First Amendment both constrain that distinction.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance of the injunction's core prohibitions and of the decision not to dissolve it wholesale after *Bray*, while vacating the fifteen-foot speech exclusions and the cease-and-desist restriction on this record. The mandate should permit a renewed, separately justified fixed access zone if the district court identifies facts showing that a narrower obstruction prohibition cannot adequately protect the actual entrances and approaches. He should not declare fixed zones inherently invalid or make a previously violated injunction an indispensable legal prerequisite to every preventive remedy.

His starting point should be that neither the clinics' lawful medical work nor the protesters' religious and political advocacy is optional in the legal analysis. The relevant state-law rights support protection against trespass and interference; the First Amendment protects peaceful communication in traditional public forums. No hybrid abortion-and-speech doctrine gives one side an automatic advantage. *Madsen* supplies the governing test.

On the existing findings, the moving restriction captures too much conduct disconnected from access. A counselor standing or walking without blocking another person may be forced away as the protected person moves. The government must justify that actual burden, not substitute the proposition that some members of the group previously obstructed patients. An injunction may reach persons acting in concert with wrongdoers under Rule 65, but association does not erase the requirement that the prohibited conduct be adequately connected to the violation.

The listener-triggered command presents an independent defect. People must remain free to leave, refuse literature, and obtain treatment without being physically impeded. The decree can forbid blocking, coercive following, and harassment defined through objective conduct. It cannot simply make an unwilling listener the final censor of otherwise protected public advocacy. The initial permission to speak does not cure an unjustified prohibition on all further counseling after rejection.

A fixed entrance zone warrants a more cautious explanation. Doorways and vehicle entrances can require preventive clearance; the existence of peaceful events under the earlier order does not disprove every need for such clearance. Stone should therefore rest the present vacatur on insufficiently differentiated findings and scope, not announce that a district judge must allow another blockade before acting. A renewed decree must distinguish a stationary access corridor from an exclusion surrounding every moving person, consider whether passage remains genuinely possible, and explain why the selected dimensions are necessary. Those are applications of *Madsen*, not a judicially invented permanent footage rule.

Stone should retain the objective noise restriction and physical-access commands. Protecting political speech is not a reason to strip clinics of enforceable protection against actual interference. The appropriate remedy modifies the overbroad portions rather than dissolving the entire decree. Nor should the court resolve newly suggested claims under later-enacted federal legislation that were not the source of the appealed injunction. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 9, 25; Speech; Personal Liberty; Remedies.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** remove the unsupported moving exclusions and listener-controlled censorship, vacate the present fixed restriction for a distinct necessity analysis, and preserve all independently valid access, trespass, and health-related noise provisions.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** invalidate the moving zones and cease-and-desist provision while remanding only the fixed-zone portion for findings under *Madsen*. A properly supported fixed entrance restriction is legally acceptable in principle; this is not approval of the present fifteen-foot requirement or of facts not found.

**Non-join limits:** a right to obstruct access, a categorical ban on all buffer zones, an immunity from review because abortion access is important, a listener's veto based on disagreement, or treating all anti-abortion advocacy as inherently threatening or unprotected conduct.

# 10. Pagnucco v. Pan American World Airways, Inc.

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 92-9251, 92-9253, and 92-9255. **Selected version:** September 12, 1994 amended opinion, 37 F.3d 804. **Court below:** Eastern District of New York, Chief Judge Thomas C. Platt. The original appellate majority opinion was filed January 31, 1994, and the original dissent February 18. The rehearing petition was submitted April 6; that date should not be described as a new oral argument. The September opinion withdrew and replaced the earlier opinions. The simulation addresses the matters under reconsideration at that amended disposition, without inventing a previously approved simulation judgment. [Amended opinion and record account](https://hallapproved.com/us/cases/ca2/1994/7030408/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

On December 21, 1988, a bomb destroyed Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland, killing its 243 passengers and sixteen crew members. The claims here arose from the deaths of Robert I. Pagnucco, Walter L. Porter, and Harry M. Bainbridge. Their estate representatives were Judith A. Pagnucco, Molena A. Porter, and Dona Bardelli Bainbridge. Pan Am and its security affiliate, Alert Management Systems, defended the consolidated liability proceedings. The common liability determination also governed 207 other passenger cases; the three individual damages awards were separately tried.

Plaintiffs' theory was that an unaccompanied suitcase containing an explosive device in a radio-cassette player entered the interline baggage system at Frankfurt, traveled through Heathrow, and reached Flight 103. The precise route and mechanism were disputed and supported substantially by circumstantial evidence. A 1994 brief cannot turn that theory into a fact established by later criminal investigations or prosecutions.

The applicable Air Carrier Standard Security Program required positive passenger-baggage matching at the pertinent high-security airports, or physical inspection or exclusion of unaccompanied baggage. Pan Am had stopped interline matching at Heathrow and Frankfurt and used x-ray screening instead. Plaintiffs presented security warnings, evidence concerning training and implementation, and evidence that management knew the limits of x-ray screening. Pan Am contested causation and the inference of willful misconduct, and sought to introduce evidence that company personnel understood the FAA to have approved its procedures.

The trial judge restricted the latter evidence on the ground that an oral communication could not constitute the required regulatory waiver. The record distinguishes an uncertain proffer concerning security official Sonesen from a specific proffer concerning executive Cozzi, who would have described the task force's instruction to obtain FAA guidance and the reported approval on which he acted. The court also conditioned certain British regulatory evidence on Pan Am's acceptance of a violation of the American rule. Those rulings implicate different questions: legal authorization, the personnel's actual understanding, admissible proof of that understanding, and preservation.

After a thirteen-week liability trial, the jury found willful misconduct causally connected to the deaths. The three damages awards were $9.225 million for Pagnucco, $1.735 million for Porter, and $9 million for Bainbridge. The jury awarded no survival damages for conscious pain and suffering. The challenges to loss of society and parental care must be distinguished from unchallenged financial-contribution and service components. *Pagnucco*, 37 F.3d at 809-28.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final judgments support appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The Warsaw Convention supplies the substantive liability framework; the court must separately identify the applicable federal law of damages. Evidentiary rulings ordinarily receive abuse-of-discretion review, but a ruling that excludes relevant mental-state evidence because it could not establish a legally valid waiver rests on a reviewable legal premise. Rule 61 requires assessment of prejudice to substantial rights, not an automatic new trial for every mistaken evidentiary ruling.

Under Federal Rule of Evidence 103(a)(2), an excluded-evidence claim requires an adequate offer or a context making the substance apparent. The Cozzi proffer supplies a firmer preserved basis than disputed descriptions of what Sonesen would have said. The rejection of a proposed curative instruction concerning an e-mail is a separate preservation problem; it does not automatically waive a previously preserved claim that the defense was forbidden to present other evidence of its actual understanding.

Some objections to plaintiffs' experts and to miscellaneous misconduct evidence were not preserved in the form now asserted. Those cannot be pooled with preserved objections as though all received ordinary appellate review. The sufficiency of the causation evidence is assessed favorably to the verdict, not through Stone's preferred reconstruction of the bombing. The legal availability of damages categories is reviewed independently, with factual dependency and valuation questions left to the proper factfinder.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Articles 17 and 25 of the Warsaw Convention, together with the Montreal Agreement's liability limit, make willful misconduct consequential: when the governing requirements are met, the carrier loses the otherwise applicable limitation. Under *Grey v. American Airlines*, 227 F.2d 282, 285 (2d Cir. 1955), *Berner v. British Commonwealth Pacific Airlines*, 346 F.2d 532, 537 (2d Cir. 1965), and *Ospina v. Trans World Airlines*, 975 F.2d 35, 37 (2d Cir. 1992), regulatory noncompliance alone is not the entire inquiry. The required knowledge or reckless disregard of probable injury, and the causal connection, must be established. The basic willful-misconduct instruction itself was not challenged.

The government's authorization precedents, including *United States v. Duggan*, 743 F.2d 59, 83-84 (2d Cir. 1984), and *United States v. Durrani*, 835 F.2d 410, 422-23 (2d Cir. 1987), do not allow an unauthorized official to waive a binding legal requirement. That answers a claim of legal permission. It does not invariably answer whether evidence of a communication is relevant to a person's state of mind under a different liability rule. A statement offered to show its effect on a listener is not necessarily offered for its truth. *Vinieris v. Byzantine Maritime Corp.*, 731 F.2d 1061, 1064 (2d Cir. 1984), gives substantial weight to genuinely probative evidence where mental state is central.

Plaintiffs have a strong response: an alleged belief about legal compliance does not necessarily negate knowledge that passengers face a grave physical risk. Someone may knowingly endanger passengers while believing a regulator has tolerated the practice. Moreover, the warning history and the weaknesses in the proposed testimony may substantially reduce its probative value. *Cheek v. United States*, 498 U.S. 192 (1991), and *Ratzlaf v. United States*, 510 U.S. 135 (1994), concern particular criminal statutes; they do not convert Warsaw willful misconduct into a universal requirement of knowledge of illegality. The distinction supports an appropriately limited instruction, not either side's categorical position.

Several other evidentiary disputes require narrower treatment. Rule 8(e)(2), as then numbered, permits alternative defenses; requiring a party to abandon one merely to present another is not justified by an asserted inconsistency. See *Mathews v. United States*, 485 U.S. 58, 64 (1988). An x-ray demonstration can nonetheless be excluded as cumulative when the relevant visibility proposition is stipulated. Hypothetical alternative bombing theories require a proper expert foundation under Rule 702; the defendants need not prove an entire alternative history, but unsupported possibilities do not become admissible merely because causation is disputed.

Investigative findings in a civil case may qualify under Rule 803(8)(C), subject to trustworthiness and other applicable limits. *Beech Aircraft Corp. v. Rainey*, 488 U.S. 153, 167 (1988), and *Gentile v. County of Suffolk*, 926 F.2d 142, 151 (2d Cir. 1991), permit evaluative findings, not indiscriminate admission of every embedded accusation. Conversely, *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 363-65 (2d Cir. 1992), and *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135 (2d Cir.), modified, 856 F.2d 5 (1988), preserve the court's authority to state the law and the jury's responsibility to apply it. A security expert may explain practices and instruments without instructing the jury on the carrier's legal guilt.

On damages, *In re Air Disaster at Lockerbie*, 928 F.2d 1267, 1278-81 (2d Cir. 1991), supplies binding circuit law on the federal common-law framework and exclusion of punitive damages. *Sea-Land Services, Inc. v. Gaudet*, 414 U.S. 573 (1974), and *American Export Lines, Inc. v. Alvez*, 446 U.S. 274 (1980), support loss-of-society recovery in the maritime settings they govern. *Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham*, 436 U.S. 618 (1978), and *Miles v. Apex Marine Corp.*, 498 U.S. 19 (1990), require fidelity to applicable statutory limits, but do not mechanically impose a high-seas statute's damages restriction on this crash over land. The governing circuit framework leaves room for society damages to spouses and dependent children. It does not justify a presumption that all adult children were dependent.

Parental-care damages have a separate pecuniary foundation. *First National Bank in Greenwich v. National Airlines*, 288 F.2d 621, 624 (2d Cir. 1961), limits the ordinary assumption of such care to minority, while permitting proof of specific compensable contributions thereafter. Companionship cannot be relabeled financial guidance to evade the applicable category or to duplicate society damages.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose a new trial on willful-misconduct liability because the preserved exclusion of Cozzi's mental-state evidence rested on an improper equivalence between legal authorization and the defendant's actual understanding. His opinion should expressly reject a defense of binding oral waiver. Pan Am remained subject to the written security requirements. The question is whether the people directing its operations possessed the culpable state of mind required to remove the Convention's liability limit.

A jury could disbelieve Cozzi, conclude that reliance was contrived, or find that even an honestly understood communication did not overcome knowledge of probable injury. Those are reasons to test the evidence, not to declare it legally irrelevant. Evidence that a responsible official instructed personnel to consult the regulator, received a report of approval, and proceeded on that understanding has some tendency to distinguish a consciously reckless operational choice from a mistaken judgment. Rule 105 permits the court to tell the jury that no legally effective waiver occurred and that the evidence bears only on the appropriate mental-state question. Rule 403 remains available for a particularized objection; it cannot sustain an exclusion whose stated foundation denied the distinction altogether.

The error's consequence must be considered against plaintiffs' substantial warning evidence. Stone should not minimize prior bomb incidents, the shortcomings of x-ray detection, or the alleged failure to respond adequately to specific warnings. Yet the issue is not whether that evidence would permit another jury to find willful misconduct. It is whether exclusion of the defense's account of its own decisionmaking could materially have affected this jury's resolution of the central disputed mental element. A trial built around management's knowledge and disregard is not fairly assessed by counting the remaining adverse incidents. The Cozzi exclusion, reinforced by the improper restriction on alternative regulatory defenses, warrants retrial under the civil substantial-rights standard.

The mandate should not require admission of every Sonesen statement, every proposed expert, or every alternative bombing scenario. Those matters retain their independent preservation, foundation, hearsay, and cumulative-evidence requirements. Nor should it direct judgment for Pan Am on causation. The circumstantial chain was sufficient to be evaluated by a jury; a new trial addresses a defective process of deciding liability, not an appellate determination of how the bomb actually traveled.

Stone should resolve the recurring damages issues necessary to guide further proceedings. Under the governing circuit framework, the spouse and dependent-child society claims are legally available; Pagnucco's adult children's dependency cannot be assumed. Parental-care instructions require the minority limitation and a separate evidentiary basis for identifiable post-majority pecuniary contributions. Any new damages proceedings must avoid double counting. The district court should determine which unchallenged and genuinely separable components can remain fixed, subject to the new liability determination, rather than automatically reopening every dollar or treating all existing awards as collectible despite vacated liability. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8-9, 24-25; Evidence, Proof, and the Record; Statutory Interpretation; Remedies.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** a preserved and prejudicial mental-state exclusion requires a new liability trial; actual legal authority and evidence relevant to culpable understanding must be separately analyzed. The opinion should also state the correct society and parental-care rules governing the remand.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** a new trial based specifically on the Cozzi proffer and the restriction on alternative defenses, without deciding the disputed adequacy of other offers or announcing a general rule admitting regulatory communications. A remand confined to particular damages categories is acceptable only if Stone independently concludes that the liability error was harmless; that conclusion is not approved merely as a bargaining alternative.

**Non-join limits:** an effective oral waiver contrary to the governing regulatory procedure; automatic willful misconduct from any violation; exclusion of all state-of-mind evidence because it cannot establish authorization; appellate adoption of an unproved bomb route; unrestricted adult-child society awards; or an assumption that the Convention's loss of a limitation authorizes punitive damages.

# 11. Hathaway v. Coughlin

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-2732. **Decision date:** September 29, 1994. **Citation:** 37 F.3d 63. **Court below:** Northern District of New York, Judge Frederick J. Scullin, Jr. **Historical argument:** April 18, 1994. The relevant appeal concerns Dr. Foote's claim to qualified immunity after a trial ended without a verdict. *Farmer v. Brennan*, decided June 6, 1994, is available when the appeal is decided, although the alleged misconduct occurred much earlier. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/37/63/509064/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Hathaway had undergone hip-fusion surgery in 1973, with pins inserted into his hip. The fusion was unsuccessful, and he experienced arthritis and persistent pain. A specialist proposed additional surgery in 1978, which Hathaway then declined. Dr. Foote, a prison general practitioner, later provided measures including pain medication, orthopedic shoes, restrictions on lifting, and placement on a lower floor.

An x-ray in July 1980 revealed two broken pins. Hathaway was not promptly told of that finding. He repeatedly complained of pain over the following years, including numerous visits in 1981 and 1982. According to his trial testimony, after a nurse informed him of the broken pins in July 1981, he told Foote that he was willing to undergo surgery. Foote said a referral would be made. A renewed specialist referral did not occur until late 1982, after correspondence concerning the condition and the filing of this action.

The subsequent medical assessments were not uniform. One specialist considered pin removal not medically necessary but potentially helpful for pain; another recommended further surgery. An operation in October 1983 removed accessible portions of the pins and included further grafting. The later operation is relevant to the course of the condition, but its occurrence does not establish that every earlier conservative decision was constitutionally deficient.

An earlier appeal had reversed summary judgment because the medical-care claim presented factual issues. *Hathaway v. Coughlin*, 841 F.2d 48 (2d Cir. 1988). At the later trial, the other defendants obtained judgment for lack of personal involvement. Those dispositions are not the appeal now presented. The jury could not agree on Foote's liability, and the court declared a mistrial and denied his motions for judgment as a matter of law. *Hathaway*, 37 F.3d at 64-66.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

A denial of judgment as a matter of law following a mistrial is ordinarily interlocutory. The appeal is cognizable only to the extent it presents a legal denial of qualified immunity under *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511, 530 (1985), and the circuit's then-existing application in *Moffitt v. Town of Brookfield*, 950 F.2d 880, 884 (2d Cir. 1991). It is not a license to decide which witness should prevail at a retrial. Later restrictions on fact-dependent immunity appeals must not be projected backward into September 1994.

The legal sufficiency inquiry takes the evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to Hathaway. See *Hendricks v. Coughlin*, 942 F.2d 109, 112 (2d Cir. 1991). The court separately asks whether that account permits a constitutional violation and whether the law at the time of Foote's conduct clearly established the relevant obligation. The jurisdictional exception reaches the legal immunity question; it does not establish a verdict on damages or authorize review of unrelated defendants' judgments.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104-06 (1976), distinguishes deliberate indifference to serious medical needs from negligence, malpractice, and disagreement over treatment. *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 (1991), requires attention to culpability as well as the seriousness of the condition. *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 837, 842-45 (1994), explains that the official must know of and disregard the relevant substantial risk; actual knowledge may be inferred from evidence, but it is not replaced by a rule that the official merely should have known.

Hathaway's strongest case is not simply that a pin broke or that another physician would have chosen surgery earlier. It combines a serious and persistently painful condition, knowledge of broken hardware, failure to inform him, evidence that he reconsidered surgery once informed, and an extended interval without effective reassessment. A jury could find that a physician consciously persisted in an ineffective response rather than exercising an actual medical judgment about the changed problem.

Foote's strongest response is substantial. Hathaway had a complicated longstanding orthopedic condition, previously rejected surgery, received continuing treatment, and was later evaluated by specialists who did not regard pin removal as medically required. A physician does not become deliberately indifferent because conservative treatment fails to eliminate pain. A jury could find that Foote continued to exercise professional judgment, or that any delay amounted only to negligence.

The existence of those competing inferences is decisive at this stage. A court cannot select the professional-judgment inference simply because some care was provided. Equally, the jury must not be told that any inadequate treatment is deliberate indifference. The question remains actual awareness and disregard, applied to the condition, information, and available response.

Qualified immunity requires a historically grounded notice inquiry. *Estelle* predated the alleged conduct and made knowing disregard of serious medical needs actionable. *Farmer* supplies the governing articulation at decision, but cannot create notice retrospectively for a previously lawful act. The relevant proposition must therefore be defined at the appropriate level: not a right to a particular operation on a particular date, but a prohibition on consciously disregarding a known serious medical need through the course of conduct the plaintiff's evidence would establish.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance of the denial of qualified-immunity-based judgment as a matter of law and return the case for retrial. He should not announce that Hathaway has proved deliberate indifference. His conclusion is that a reasonable jury, crediting Hathaway's testimony and the documented sequence, could find it, and that the governing prohibition was sufficiently established by *Estelle*.

The medical details must do the work. Hathaway's earlier rejection of surgery is relevant but not permanently dispositive. The later discovery of broken pins and his testimony that he expressed willingness to proceed materially change the premise on which reliance on that refusal rests. Similarly, medication and accommodations are evidence of care, but they do not compel a conclusion that every subsequent delay reflected medical judgment. A jury could regard repeated reports of severe pain and the failure to implement a promised reassessment as evidence of knowing disregard.

Foote must remain able to show that he reasonably relied on specialist advice, understood the complaints differently, or made a genuine judgment that surgery's risks or limited likely benefits favored continued conservative treatment. Stone should not convert the eventual decision to operate into a retrospective constitutional prescription. The difference between unsuccessful treatment and deliberately withholding a response is the question for the factfinder, not a distinction abolished in favor of either party.

An expert is not invariably required to permit an inference from prolonged pain, withheld information, and unexplained failure to act on known facts. Some medical questions do require expert proof, particularly where a lay jury cannot evaluate a treatment choice or causal connection. This case should not establish that expert evidence is categorically unnecessary in prison medical litigation. The narrower conclusion is that the evidence described here can support the requisite inference without a universal expert-testimony prerequisite.

The remand should leave causation, compensable injury, and Foote's actual knowledge for determination under proper instructions. Liability must rest on his own conduct. No claim against the other officials is revived, and no damages are awarded merely because the first jury could not agree. Master Jurisprudence §§ 8-9, 18, 25; Criminal Procedure and Punishment; Personal Liberty; Remedies.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** a jury may find deliberate indifference from knowing persistence in ineffective treatment and delay in responding to a materially changed, serious condition, notwithstanding some ongoing care; immunity does not require judgment for Foote on the plaintiff-favorable record.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** affirm because the evidence concerning notification, renewed willingness to undergo surgery, and the delayed referral alone creates a triable account of knowing disregard. The opinion can reserve broader descriptions of ineffective-treatment liability and the general role of medical experts.

**Non-join limits:** an entitlement to surgery whenever an inmate requests it; constitutional liability for negligence alone; an automatic immunity rule whenever any treatment was supplied; or use of *Farmer* to impose notice that did not exist when Foote acted.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was Frank X. Altimari, Joseph M. McLaughlin, and Dennis Jacobs. Stone provisionally replaces Jacobs, leaving Altimari, McLaughlin, and Stone. The April 1994 argument is compatible with the service year, subject to the common commission-date and conflicts qualifications.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Altimari wrote the opinion, joined by Judge McLaughlin, affirming the denial of Foote's motion. Judge Jacobs dissented, emphasizing the treatment supplied, the specialists' judgments, and the distinction between medical disagreement and deliberate indifference. *Hathaway*, 37 F.3d at 66-70.

Stone's proposed judgment matches the historical disposition but is limited to the sufficiency of a plaintiff-favorable account and the legal immunity question. It does not reproduce the historical judges' votes in the simulation or resolve the factual dispute against Foote.

# 12. Scanio v. United States

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 94-2002. **Decision date:** October 11, 1994. **Citation:** 37 F.3d 858. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Chief Judge Michael A. Telesca. **Historical argument:** May 26, 1994. The appeal concerns the custody requirement for a federal prisoner's § 2255 motion, not the later-enacted one-year collateral-review limitation. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/37/858/509353/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Scanio was convicted in 1988 of structuring a currency transaction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5324(3) and 5322(a), and received one year of imprisonment followed by two years of supervised release. His conviction was affirmed in *United States v. Scanio*, 900 F.2d 485 (2d Cir. 1990). He began imprisonment November 30, 1990, transferred to a Salvation Army halfway house on July 29, 1991, and was released from that facility on November 27, 1991, immediately before Thanksgiving.

The district court treated supervised release as beginning on November 27, 1991 and expiring at midnight on November 26, 1993. The later disagreement concerned both the adequacy of that determination and the significance of the court's closure. The difference between supervised release and old-law parole matters; one cannot automatically transfer a rule about the duration of a parole sentence to a separately imposed term of supervised release.

As described in the appellate record account, counsel went to the federal courthouse on Friday, November 26, 1993 to file Scanio's petition, but was informed that the clerk's office was closed by the chief judge's order for the extended Thanksgiving holiday. Counsel returned and filed on Monday, November 29, the first reopening day. The motion alleged ineffective assistance of trial counsel. The district court dismissed it in a one-page order because Scanio was no longer in custody when it was docketed. Its reference to “probation” did not accurately identify the supervised-release term actually imposed.

This was a challenge to the federal conviction. The published opinion's isolated reference to a “state court trial” should not be repeated as an additional, unverified state prosecution. No merits determination of the ineffective-assistance claim is before this panel. *Scanio*, 37 F.3d at 859-62.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The dismissal is a final collateral judgment appealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the governing § 2255 appellate framework. Whether the district court had power to adjudicate the motion is a question of law; supported historical facts receive clear-error review. The court cannot assume § 2255 jurisdiction because the underlying claim is important.

The preserved dispute includes the expiration of custody and the effect of closure under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 6(a). A distinct constructive-filing theory would be a proposed refinement, not an argument that may silently be attributed to counsel. Before adopting it, the court must determine that it is properly reachable and afford both parties an opportunity to address its legal and factual predicates.

A dismissal for lack of custody does not answer whether another collateral vehicle is available. Any request for coram nobis would require the separate showing recognized in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502 (1954). It cannot be granted automatically by renaming an unsuccessful § 2255 motion.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 2255 requires the movant to be in custody under the challenged federal sentence. *United States v. Brilliant*, 274 F.2d 618, 620 (2d Cir. 1960), treats that requirement as jurisdictional. *Carafas v. LaVallee*, 391 U.S. 234, 238 (1968), preserves a properly commenced case after release; it does not dispense with custody at commencement. *Maleng v. Cook*, 490 U.S. 488, 491-92 (1989), prevents collateral consequences of a fully expired sentence from substituting for the required restraint. Supervision can qualify as custody under the principle of *Jones v. Cunningham*, 371 U.S. 236 (1963), without physical imprisonment.

The government's strongest argument is that custody is a status, not an ordinary deadline. Rule 6(a) computes filing periods; Rule 82 forbids enlarging jurisdiction through the civil rules. If the first legally effective filing occurred November 29 and every restraint under the sentence had already ended, treating the motion as merely timely would not answer the absence of custody.

Scanio's strongest response is that the legal system itself made the designated filing office inaccessible while he still possessed the required status. *Union National Bank v. Lamb*, 337 U.S. 38, 41 (1949), and *Simon v. Commissioner*, 176 F.2d 230, 232 (2d Cir. 1949), show that the word “jurisdictional” does not invariably defeat ordinary computation rules. Those decisions concern time periods, however, and therefore do not themselves establish that a terminated custody relationship continues through a holiday.

A different question is when a completed application is treated as lodged. *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266 (1988), recognizes filing upon delivery to prison authorities for forwarding in its particular setting. It does not control this represented applicant's circumstances: counsel was not a pro se prisoner dependent on the prison mail system. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that identifying the legally operative filing event is analytically distinct from enlarging a court's substantive jurisdiction. Any extension to an actual courthouse tender frustrated solely by official closure must be defended as a new rule on an open question, with administrable limits.

The record also must distinguish actual termination of imprisonment and commencement of supervised release from a temporary physical release that left an earlier custodial term legally running. *Stabile v. United States Bureau of Prisons*, 834 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1987), concerns the relationship between parole supervision and a sentence; it does not by itself resolve the supervised-release calculation. Neither a conclusory label in the district order nor an unexplained assumption that every early release shortens the total sentence should replace the applicable legal calculation.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose a limited vacatur and remand directed to the jurisdictional facts and the asserted pre-expiration attempt to file. He should not declare that Rule 6(a) prolongs custody until the next business day. That would answer the government's strongest objection by denying the difference between a person's status and a deadline. His proposed route instead asks whether the application was constructively lodged while the jurisdictional status still existed.

The rule he should propose is narrow: when counsel actually presents at the court's designated filing location during an otherwise available filing day with the completed application for filing, and an official closure alone prevents its receipt, filing at the first reopening may establish a constructive lodging at that earlier tender. The applicant must prove those predicates. Preparation at home, an uncommunicated intention to file, an ordinary mailing, or a visit without the completed application would not suffice. This is an extension by analogy from established filing principles, not a holding already supplied by *Houston*, *Lamb*, or § 2255.

The distinction performs real legal work. The court does not say that Scanio remained supervised on Monday. It identifies Friday as the filing event if an actual tender of the completed application was frustrated by the court's own closure. That preserves the statutory requirement of simultaneous custody and filing. It also prevents the government's exclusive control over receipt from making the otherwise available statutory procedure disappear before custody ends. The rationale comes from the administration of filing and the existing collateral-review channel, not from a freestanding constitutional power to overlook jurisdiction.

The reported visit to the courthouse provides a concrete reason to examine that question, but an appellate opinion should not invent findings about the completed papers, precisely what counsel did, or available means of lodging them. The parties should first be heard on the proposed legal characterization and its preservation. The district court should then determine any genuinely disputed predicate facts and explain the legal commencement and expiration of supervised release. An unsupported suspicion about a halfway house's authority would not justify extending custody; the actual legal arrangement must be established.

If the court concludes that the proposed filing rule is unavailable under governing law or that its factual predicates are not proved, and the sentence had fully expired before the first effective filing, dismissal follows. Stone cannot replace that conclusion with equitable sympathy. If an effective filing occurred during custody, later release does not defeat the proceeding under *Carafas*, and the district court may address the motion's remaining procedural requirements and merits. Nothing here determines the effectiveness of trial counsel or promises collateral relief. Master Jurisprudence §§ 3, 5, 8-9, 18; Courts, Jurisdiction, and Justiciability; Collateral Review.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** recognize a carefully bounded constructive-lodging rule for a proved, completed application actually brought for filing before custody ended and prevented solely by official closure; remand for the necessary facts and any required adversarial briefing.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** remand for an adequate explanation of the custody calculation and findings concerning the attempted filing, expressly reserving whether constructive lodging is legally available. This fallback does not predetermine jurisdiction after remand.

**Non-join limits:** treating collateral consequences alone as custody; extending supervised release by judicial equity; applying a later statutory limitation period; assuming that a visit without an application constitutes filing; or adopting the new theory without a fair opportunity for the government to address it.

The historical panel was Ellsworth A. Van Graafeiland, Lawrence W. Pierce, and Frank X. Altimari. Stone provisionally replaces Altimari, producing Van Graafeiland, Pierce, and Stone. The May 1994 argument is compatible with the stated service year, subject to the common eligibility qualifications.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Altimari wrote the opinion, joined by Judge Van Graafeiland, affirming dismissal because custody had expired before the November 29 filing and Rule 6(a) could not alter that status. Judge Pierce dissented, challenging the custody determination and the rejection of the holiday-computation principle. *Scanio*, 37 F.3d at 858-65.

Stone's proposed remand shares the concern about court-created loss of access but does not simply adopt an extension of custody through Rule 6(a). The constructive-lodging approach is a newly proposed theory requiring approval, briefing, and factual support, not a historical holding or an established simulation result.

# 13. In re Secured Equipment Trust of Eastern Air Lines, Inc.

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 94-5015 and 94-5017. **Decision date:** October 17, 1994. **Citation:** 38 F.3d 86. **Courts below:** Southern District of New York, Judge John E. Sprizzo, reviewing Bankruptcy Judge Burton R. Lifland. **Historical argument:** June 30, 1994. The appeal concerns the Trust's eligibility as a business trust, not whether every condition for involuntary Chapter 11 relief has been established. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/38/86/635981/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Eastern created the Secured Equipment Trust to raise $500 million through certificates secured by part of its aircraft fleet. The transaction formally involved sale of certificates, purchase of aircraft with the proceeds, and leaseback to Eastern. The parties agreed that its economic substance was secured financing. Three certificate series had different priority levels, interest rates, and maturities. First Fidelity served as collateral trustee; separate series trustees represented the certificateholders' interests.

Eastern's rental payments were designed to service the certificates' principal, premium, and interest. The indenture required any excess to return to Eastern. After full payment, the equipment would be reconveyed to Eastern and the Trust dissolved. Upon default, however, the collateral trustee had authority to possess, hold, lease, and sell the equipment. Thus the documents provided both a payment mechanism during performance and an extensive asset-management function upon default.

Eastern entered Chapter 11 on March 9, 1989 and stopped making timely payments. A January 1991 stipulation returned aircraft and $230 million in equipment proceeds to the Trust. The trustee thereafter actively maintained, marketed, leased, and sold aircraft, retaining legal, accounting, and aviation assistance. It took possession of sixty-seven commercial jets and 165 engines; forty-seven jets remained under management two years later. These activities were not a proposed future enterprise invented by petitioners; they were operations recognized in the bankruptcy court's account.

LNC Investments, Charter National Life Insurance, and Magten Asset Management, holders of $54.2 million in second- and third-priority certificates, filed an involuntary Chapter 11 petition against the Trust on March 28, 1991. The Resolution Trust Corporation later joined. The matter was transferred from New Jersey to the Southern District of New York. The collateral trustee and United Jersey Bank sought dismissal; the petitioning holders and Shawmut, the third-series trustee, opposed it. Judge Lifland held that the Trust was not an eligible business trust, and Judge Sprizzo affirmed. *In re Secured Equipment Trust*, 153 B.R. 409 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. 1993); 38 F.3d at 87-89.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The district court's final disposition of the bankruptcy appeal is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 158(d). The court of appeals reviews the bankruptcy court's legal conclusions independently and its factual findings for clear error. *In re Ionosphere Clubs, Inc.*, 922 F.2d 984, 988-89 (2d Cir. 1990). A legal definition does not become insulated merely because its application depends on a detailed financial arrangement.

The petitioning parties bear the burden of establishing debtor eligibility. Their appeal presents both the legal significance of the Trust's purposes and the effect of its authorized and actual operations. The panel should not resolve contested creditor qualifications, grounds for an order for relief, priorities, or eventual plan treatment that were not decided through the eligibility ruling.

The existence of alternative remedies is relevant to the parties' practical arguments, but does not itself establish or defeat statutory coverage. The series trustees had already filed claims in Eastern's bankruptcy, and ordinary proceedings could address actionable trustee misconduct. Neither the desirability of consolidating disputes nor the presence of another forum rewrites the definition of the proposed debtor.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 303(a) permits involuntary proceedings against an eligible “person.” Section 101(41) includes a corporation within that term, and § 101(9)(A)(v) includes a business trust within corporation. The Code does not separately define business trust. This is a genuinely open circuit question in October 1994, rather than an invitation to disobey an earlier controlling circuit definition.

The statutory context distinguishes an entity conducting organized business through trustees from an ordinary donative or custodial trust whose incidental transactions merely preserve property. Lower courts had recognized that distinction but had not produced a uniform test. *In re St. Augustine Trust*, 109 B.R. 494, 496 (Bankr. M.D. Fla. 1990), persuasively directs attention to the trust documents and the whole arrangement, rather than the bare fact of some business-like activity. Those bankruptcy decisions persuade; they do not bind the Second Circuit.

The earlier Bankruptcy Act is material. Its definition encompassed a business conducted through trustees with beneficial ownership or interest represented by certificates or other written instruments. Congress substituted “business trust” in the 1978 Code and described the corporation definition as similar to its predecessor. That continuity supports attention to collective investment, trustee management, and commercial operations. It does not demonstrate that every instrument labeled a trust or every holder of a security interest is independently eligible.

The appellees' strongest argument is that this Trust does not conduct an enterprise for its own account. It holds collateral for a financing obligation; excess proceeds return to Eastern; its liquidation activities implement enforcement rather than a separate profit-generating mission. Many conventional security arrangements involve professional management after default. An expansive rule based on those activities alone could transform ordinary security trustees into involuntary debtors.

The petitioners' strongest response is that financing and leasing are themselves commercial activities, and a fixed contractual return is no less an investment return because the investor lacks unlimited upside. The Trust was the vehicle through which capital was raised, equipment was placed under trustee ownership and control, and the agreed return was produced and secured. Extensive leasing and disposition following default were powers embedded in the original arrangement. Calling them protective does not necessarily remove their commercial character.

Profit purpose is relevant, but the court need not decide whether all nonprofit benefit trusts fall outside the Code. Nor should it announce that the mere absence of residual profits is dispositive. The federal classification is the governing question; New York's treatment of an association operating under a trust instrument with certificate interests can inform the analysis without conclusively controlling the federal statute.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose reversal of the categorical eligibility dismissal. The statutory term should encompass this certificate-financed, trustee-operated commercial arrangement, including the substantial leasing and asset-management functions authorized from its inception. That conclusion does not require denying the parties' agreement that the sale-and-leaseback was economically a secured financing. The question is whether a financing enterprise conducted in this institutional form can be a business trust, not whether its participants must pretend that the underlying obligation was something else.

The legal error is to treat preservation of a contractually fixed return as necessarily outside business. Many businesses earn specified rather than residual returns. Nothing in the enacted inclusion of business trusts makes unlimited profit participation the decisive boundary. The predecessor statute's focus on trustee-conducted business and certificated interests reinforces that conclusion. The Trust was not a family settlement that happened to sell an asset; investors supplied capital through certificates for an organized transaction, and the trustee undertook continuing commercial responsibilities over a fleet.

The default provisions matter because they show what institution the parties created, not because subsequent distress permits the trustee to manufacture eligibility. The same indenture authorized commercial leasing, maintenance, marketing, and sales if Eastern ceased performance. The actual fleet operations make concrete an original institutional function. Stone should rely on that combined formation-and-operation account rather than pronounce that the size of the collateral pool or the number of retained professionals independently makes the Trust a business.

Appellees' concern about ordinary security trustees requires a real boundary. A trustee whose role is simply to hold a lien and distribute receipts does not automatically become a business trust because it employs counsel or sells collateral after default. Here the organized certificate investment, the integrated equipment transaction, and the trustee's substantial continuing powers together satisfy the statutory category. This is a proposed construction of the open federal term, not a universal checklist in which every characteristic must appear in all future cases.

Reversal would establish eligibility only. It would not authorize junior certificateholders to erase negotiated priorities or obtain relief merely because the equipment lost value. The bankruptcy court must decide the remaining requirements for an involuntary case, any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention, and the treatment of claims under their governing sources. The availability of those protections is not a substitute for reading the eligibility provision; it confirms that debtor classification need not pre-decide every later dispute. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8, 24; Statutory Interpretation; Markets and Private Power; Courts, Jurisdiction, and Justiciability.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** an organized investment trust conducting the integrated equipment-financing and management business shown here qualifies under § 101(9)(A)(v), notwithstanding a fixed investor return and a secured-financing purpose.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** vacate and remand for application of a definition that does not treat fixed returns, collateral preservation, or lack of surplus participation as categorical disqualifications. The court could reserve final classification if material formation or operational facts genuinely require findings under that corrected standard.

**Non-join limits:** eligibility from financial size alone; eligibility whenever a trustee performs any commercial transaction; state-law labels controlling the federal definition; a categorical exclusion of all fixed-return investment trusts; or an order granting Chapter 11 relief without the additional statutory showings.

The historical panel was Amalya L. Kearse, Frank X. Altimari, and Allen G. Schwartz, a Southern District of New York district judge sitting by designation. Stone provisionally replaces Schwartz, yielding Kearse, Altimari, and Stone. The visitor's historical eligibility is established by the opinion's designation; Stone's substitution remains conditional on the simulation's lawful roster and commission date.

Judge Altimari wrote the opinion, joined by Judge Schwartz, affirming dismissal. The majority emphasized the Trust's collateral-preservation purpose and treated its business activities as incidental, while expressly declining to make profit generation universally necessary. Judge Kearse dissented, relying on the investment structure, actual operations, and continuity with the former Bankruptcy Act. *Secured Equipment Trust*, 38 F.3d at 89-93.

Stone's proposed judgment differs from the historical result and overlaps Kearse's statutory analysis. It should not misdescribe the majority as having categorically barred every nonprofit trust. His disagreement concerns the classification of this commercial financing arrangement, not an assumed power to use bankruptcy whenever another forum is inconvenient.

# 14. DeWeerth v. Baldinger

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 93-7144 and 93-7146. **Selected version:** October 27, 1994 amended opinion, 38 F.3d 1266. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Vincent L. Broderick. **Historical argument:** November 15, 1993. The initial decision in this appeal was May 16, 1994; October 27 is the amended disposition. The simulation concerns Rule 60 relief from an already final judgment, not an unconstrained first determination of ownership or New York limitations law. [Amended opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/38/1266/635762/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Gerda Dorothea DeWeerth inherited a Monet painting in 1922. Her father had acquired it in 1908. The painting was moved for safekeeping during the war and disappeared in 1945 from a castle where American soldiers had been quartered. That chronology does not itself identify a thief or establish that a particular soldier took it.

Wildenstein handled the painting in the 1950s, and Edith Marks Baldinger purchased it in 1957 in good faith. DeWeerth located it in 1982 and demanded its return. Baldinger refused. In the ensuing diversity action, the district court found for DeWeerth, rejecting the limitations and laches defenses on the record before it. See *DeWeerth v. Baldinger*, 658 F. Supp. 688 (S.D.N.Y. 1987). Wildenstein's third-party involvement must be kept distinct from the primary ownership dispute.

The Second Circuit reversed in *DeWeerth v. Baldinger*, 836 F.2d 103 (2d Cir. 1987), predicting that New York would require reasonable diligence in locating stolen property in addition to demand and refusal. Supreme Court review was denied June 13, 1988, and the judgment became final. This earlier circuit judgment and mandate are part of the historical procedural constraint, whatever Stone thinks of their prediction.

In *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell*, 77 N.Y.2d 311, 317-21 (1991), the New York Court of Appeals rejected a due-diligence addition to the limitations rule, while preserving the relevance of diligence and prejudice to laches. DeWeerth unsuccessfully requested recall of the federal mandate. She then sought relief under Rule 60(b). Judge Broderick reopened the judgment, reasoning principally that the state's authoritative correction created extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6), and alternatively that continued possession implicated Rule 60(b)(5). See 804 F. Supp. 539 (S.D.N.Y. 1992). Baldinger and Wildenstein appealed. *DeWeerth*, 38 F.3d at 1268-71.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The order granting post-judgment relief and the resulting judgment support appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. A Rule 60 decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion, including correction of erroneous legal premises. The meaning of the rule and the effect of the prior mandate are legal questions.

The initial threshold is the district court's authority to entertain the motion after an appellate mandate. *Standard Oil Co. of California v. United States*, 429 U.S. 17, 18-19 (1976), permits a district court to consider later events under Rule 60 without first obtaining appellate leave. *Eutectic Corp. v. Metco, Inc.*, 597 F.2d 32 (2d Cir. 1979), and *Fine v. Bellefonte Underwriters Insurance Co.*, 758 F.2d 50 (2d Cir. 1985), prevent relitigation of matters actually disposed of by the appellate court; they do not make all later Rule 60 motions jurisdictionally impossible.

The unexplained denial of recall of the mandate does not necessarily establish a merits rejection of every later ground for relief. See *Doe v. New York City Department of Social Services*, 709 F.2d 782, 788 (2d Cir. 1983). That threshold authority to consider a motion must not be confused with an entitlement to reopen the judgment.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), requires federal courts adjudicating state-law rights to apply the state's substantive law. *Guaranty Trust Co. v. York*, 326 U.S. 99, 109 (1945), makes state limitations law part of that obligation in diversity. Once the New York Court of Appeals speaks, its construction governs the unresolved state-law question in pending litigation. Federal appellate confidence in an earlier prediction does not outrank the state's highest court.

But Rule 60 presents a separate federal procedural question concerning a final judgment. *Ackermann v. United States*, 340 U.S. 193, 199 (1950), and *Klapprott v. United States*, 335 U.S. 601 (1949), distinguish exceptional circumstances from the ordinary consequences of completed litigation. The Second Circuit's *Matarese v. LeFevre*, 801 F.2d 98, 106 (2d Cir. 1986), and *Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Sarkisian*, 794 F.2d 754, 757 (2d Cir. 1986), preserve exceptional equitable authority without making later legal disagreement a routine reopening mechanism.

DeWeerth's strongest argument is that *Guggenheim* did more than announce a prospective policy change. It explained the state's existing rule and expressly rejected the federal prediction. Older demand-and-refusal decisions, including *Gillet v. Roberts*, 57 N.Y. 28 (1874), and *Goodwin v. Wertheimer*, 99 N.Y. 149 (1885), support the contention that the federal court had imposed a condition the state had never adopted. Leaving the contrary federal judgment intact imposes different substantive consequences because the action happened to be in federal court. The district judge also had first-hand familiarity with the equities.

Baldinger's strongest response is that a mistaken prediction remains a legal error in an adjudicated case, not an indefinitely provisional judgment. Later state decisions will often establish that a federal prediction was wrong. If that fact alone is extraordinary, the exception consumes finality throughout diversity jurisdiction. The possible availability of certification in the earlier appeal concerns how to avoid an erroneous prediction, not a general exemption from post-judgment rules after appellate review is complete.

*Pierce v. Cook & Co.*, 518 F.2d 720, 723 (10th Cir. 1975) (en banc), is persuasive authority for extraordinary relief where federal and state judgments arising from the same accident diverged after a change in controlling state law. It does not bind the Second Circuit, and the same-transaction circumstances distinguish it from the separate litigation in *Guggenheim*. That distinction is relevant because it concerns the exceptional relationship among judgments, not merely different party names.

Rule 60(b)(5)'s prospective-application clause does not encompass every judgment with continuing consequences. An ongoing injunction or supervised equitable decree differs from a completed adjudication fixing entitlement to property. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932), illustrates the former setting. The explanation in *Twelve John Does v. District of Columbia*, 841 F.2d 1133, 1139 (D.C. Cir. 1988), is persuasive: enduring effects alone are not prospective operation of the relevant kind. Here, continued possession following a final judgment does not make that judgment an executory decree requiring continuing judicial supervision.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose reversal of the Rule 60 relief while expressly recognizing that *Guggenheim* states controlling New York substantive law. His conclusion should not defend the federal court's authority to make state policy. It should identify the different question now before the court: whether an authoritative correction of the legal premise of completed litigation, without an additional exceptional circumstance, permits reopening under the governing federal rule.

The answer should be no on this record. DeWeerth had litigated ownership and timeliness, obtained a favorable trial decision, received appellate review, and sought Supreme Court review. The alleged defect is not a denial of a hearing, fraud in obtaining the judgment, or a continuing decree operating unlawfully in changed circumstances. It is an erroneous state-law prediction. The importance of the painting and the strength of the state's later rejection do not change that category by themselves.

This conclusion should not be expressed as a waiver of correct state law through selection of a federal forum. A diversity litigant is entitled to state substantive law and does not consent to judicial invention. Nor should Stone fabricate proof that Baldinger made particular expenditures or transactions in reliance on the judgment. The finality interest is concrete in the completed adjudication of competing property claims and the legal settlement of entitlement, not in invented later conduct. Federal procedural finality can preserve a completed judgment even though a pending case would now be decided differently.

Stone should give the district court's equitable judgment its proper respect, but discretion cannot turn the ordinary occurrence of later corrective precedent into an exceptional circumstance solely by describing it as a severe legal error. *Matarese* preserves real residual authority. The opinion should leave open circumstances involving an independently extraordinary defect, a materially different relationship among judgments, or another recognized Rule 60 ground. It need not announce that a later state decision can never contribute to extraordinary circumstances.

The alternative prospective-effect theory fails independently. Baldinger's continued possession is a consequence of an adjudication of title-related claims, not an ongoing injunction requiring the court to administer future conduct. Treating that consequence as prospective would subject final property and money judgments to continuing substantive reconsideration. The remedy is reinstatement of the final judgment, not a new appellate decision on laches, the adequacy of the original search, or the identity of the wartime thief.

The earlier failure to obtain an authoritative state answer remains a legitimate institutional lesson. Stone's preference for certification on an unsettled, determinative state-law issue would guide a case still open to decision. It is not a power to reopen this one outside Rule 60. Master Jurisprudence §§ 4, 8, 11-12; Statutory Interpretation; Property, Takings, and Forfeiture; Courts, Jurisdiction, and Justiciability.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** Rule 60(b)(6) does not reopen this final diversity judgment solely because a later authoritative state decision rejects its legal prediction; Rule 60(b)(5) does not apply merely because the prevailing party continues possessing the disputed property.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** a case-specific conclusion that the combination of circumstances identified here is insufficient, while reserving whether later state authority joined with an independent extraordinary circumstance could support relief. The opinion can avoid broad pronouncements about all changes or clarifications of state law.

**Non-join limits:** federal predictions overriding a state's highest court in pending litigation; consent to erroneous substantive law inferred from choosing federal court; an absolute prohibition on all extraordinary Rule 60 relief; or treating every lasting property consequence as an ongoing equitable decree.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was Frank X. Altimari, John M. Walker, Jr., and Richard Owen, a Southern District of New York district judge sitting by designation. Stone provisionally replaces Owen, leaving Altimari, Walker, and Stone. The November 1993 argument requires lawful later participation or resubmission; the October amendment is not proof that Stone attended that argument or participated in the May disposition.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Walker wrote the amended opinion, joined by Judge Altimari, reversing the grant of Rule 60 relief. Judge Owen dissented, emphasizing the district court's discretion, New York's authoritative rejection of the federal prediction, and the injustice of retaining the contrary result. *DeWeerth*, 38 F.3d at 1271-78.

Stone's proposed judgment matches the historical disposition but rejects any suggestion that selection of a federal forum waives the right to correct state substantive law. The controlling limit is finality under the post-judgment rule, not a diminished obligation of fidelity to New York law.

# 15. Young (Ramadan) v. Selsky

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-2567. **Decision date:** November 21, 1994. **Citation:** 41 F.3d 47. **Court below:** Northern District of New York, Judge Neal P. McCurn, following a recommendation by Magistrate Judge Gustave J. DiBianco. The appeal was initially submitted April 26, 1994; appointed counsel later submitted supplemental materials. It should not be assigned an invented oral-argument date. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/41/47/564093/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Jerry Young, also known as Ramadan, challenged five disciplinary hearings conducted at Eastern Correctional Facility between February and May 1992. He alleged that he was denied requested witnesses, including thirty-one inmates and two officers, and received special-housing confinement and losses of privileges. The merits of each requested witness, the institutional reasons for exclusion, and the legality of each sanction were not established simply by his allegations.

Donald Selsky, the Department of Correctional Services' Director of Special Housing and Inmate Disciplinary Programs, acted as the commissioner's designee for administrative review of serious disciplinary determinations. He affirmed the five challenged dispositions. Young's July 1992 § 1983 complaint sought relief including damages, expungement, and restoration of affected rights. A separate action against hearing officials had its own service and procedural problems; it was not transformed into the merits of the appeal against Selsky.

The district court dismissed on absolute-immunity grounds. The immunity presentation included material from other cases describing Selsky's centralized appellate role, large caseload, and asserted insulation from institutional pressures. The record developed on appeal also included evidence that he advised hearing officers during proceedings and remained within a correctional supervisory structure whose officials had operational interests in disciplinary outcomes.

The parties' use of those outside materials is unusual and important. Appointed counsel challenged an incomplete portrayal of Selsky's function, and the state agreed to consideration of further materials bearing on that function. Agreement to consideration here does not create a general appellate power to treat testimony in unrelated lawsuits as indisputable evidence of every asserted fact. *Young*, 41 F.3d at 49-53.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The dismissal of Young's claim against Selsky is a final judgment reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The district court had jurisdiction over the federal civil-rights claim under the applicable federal-question and civil-rights grants. Whether the described function receives absolute immunity is a question of law, reviewed independently.

Selsky bears the burden of establishing entitlement to absolute immunity. The appeal does not require a finding that he is liable, that every excluded witness had to be called, or that any particular sanction warrants damages. Qualified immunity, personal participation, causation, and the merits of the due-process allegations remain separate questions.

The law at decision is the pre-*Sandin* framework. The court must identify any protected liberty interest under the law then governing and apply the procedural standards of *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), with their institutional limits. Neither later prison-liberty doctrine nor later statutory restrictions on remedies may be imported into this November 1994 appeal.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Absolute immunity is functional, not title-based. *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872), and *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), protect the judicial function under the conditions those decisions recognize. *Butz v. Economou*, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978), extends comparable protection to particular administrative adjudication because of its functional resemblance to the judicial process and its safeguards. It does not turn every official who reviews a decision into a judge.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985), is especially important. It denied absolute immunity to prison disciplinary officials whose decisionmaking lacked the independence and safeguards characteristic of courts. Relevant considerations include insulation from pressure, procedural protections, adversarial presentation, use of precedent, the availability of correction through review, and the need to protect adjudication from harassment. *Burns v. Reed*, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991), cautions against expanding absolute immunity when the official has not established its justification.

Selsky's strongest position is that his function is appellate rather than frontline discipline. He reviews a large volume of determinations across many institutions, is not ordinarily involved in the underlying incident, and must be free to decide without repeated damages actions by dissatisfied inmates. Administrative and Article 78 review provide safeguards against erroneous decisions. Judicial immunity cannot depend on whether a reviewing official ultimately decided correctly.

Young's strongest response concerns structural independence, not dissatisfaction with results. An official who advises hearing officers during the very proceedings he later reviews, remains subject to correctional management, and lacks several ordinary adjudicative protections is not necessarily comparable to an independent appellate judge. A centralized office reduces some local pressures but does not eliminate the department's institutional interests. The same functional analysis that protects genuine adjudication requires scrutiny of that distinction.

*Dorman v. Higgins*, 821 F.2d 133, 137 (2d Cir. 1987), concerning preparation of a presentence report as an arm of a court, does not dictate immunity for an official located within prison administration. *Shelly v. Johnson*, 849 F.2d 228 (6th Cir. 1988), is persuasive rather than binding and concerns a materially different state adjudicative arrangement. *Richardson v. Selsky*, 5 F.3d 616 (2d Cir. 1993), does not establish a categorical absolute immunity for Selsky's appellate role.

The distinction between correction and compensation also matters. *Walker v. Bates*, 23 F.3d 652, 658 (2d Cir. 1994), is published circuit authority by this decision date. Its treatment of punishment already served prevents the bare availability of administrative reversal from being treated as a complete answer to an earlier deprivation. This citation uses the historical governing precedent, not any unapproved Stone proposal in the separate Walker brief.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose reversal of the absolute-immunity dismissal and remand for the remaining questions. The office's name and position on an organizational chart cannot substitute for the functional comparison required by *Butz* and *Cleavinger*. Selsky's participation in advising hearing officers, his relation to correctional superiors, and the limited adversarial safeguards are directly relevant to that comparison.

The rationale should not be that inmates need someone to sue, or that a large caseload demonstrates indifference. Neither proposition establishes the absence of immunity. The narrower legal point is that absolute immunity requires the official to demonstrate a judicially comparable function with the relevant independence and safeguards. Central review of prison discipline, while important and adjudicative in a general sense, has not been shown here to carry the combination that justifies complete protection from damages.

Stone should confront the harassment concern rather than dismiss it. An official deciding thousands of appeals faces genuine litigation burdens. But qualified immunity, requirements of personal responsibility, pleading and evidentiary rules, and adjudication of meritless claims already perform protective work. The frequency of disappointed appellants cannot alone establish absolute immunity, because that reasoning would apply equally to the disciplinary bodies addressed in *Cleavinger*.

The absence of absolute immunity does not make affirmance of a disciplinary ruling automatically unconstitutional. Young must establish a protected interest, a violation of the process actually owed, Selsky's actionable involvement, causation, and an available remedy. An official may affirm a ruling after a reasonable assessment of legitimate security grounds for denying witnesses. Whether Selsky could reasonably have understood the challenged decisions as lawful must be addressed under the qualified-immunity law applicable when he acted. *Weg v. Macchiarola*, 995 F.2d 15, 18 (2d Cir. 1993), supports leaving an unresolved immunity question for appropriate further proceedings rather than deciding contested predicates without a record.

The remand should permit development of the particular five appeals and the reasons for the witness rulings. It should also distinguish damages from requests for prospective relief: a damages immunity does not automatically resolve every form of relief, and prospective relief still requires an appropriate defendant and a live, remediable injury. Young receives a forum to prove a claim, not an appellate award of $200 per day or a finding that all challenged disciplinary records must be erased. Master Jurisprudence §§ 8-9, 18, 25; Procedural Due Process; Courts, Jurisdiction, and Justiciability; Remedies.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the departmental appellate function shown here lacks the judicial independence and safeguards required for absolute immunity; qualified immunity and all remaining claim elements remain open on remand.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** Selsky has not carried his burden on the present record, requiring vacatur and focused factual development of the function before any broader classification. This alternative must not presume the truth of disputed testimony from unrelated cases beyond the parties' actual agreement.

**Non-join limits:** absolute immunity from the appellate label alone; liability from the bare act of affirmance; disregarding legitimate prison security restrictions on witnesses; treating administrative reversal as necessarily compensating confinement already served; or deciding qualified immunity through contested appellate factfinding.

The appeal's submission and supplemental briefing, rather than an assumed oral hearing, supply the relevant participation setting.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Feinberg wrote the opinion, joined by Judge Winter, reversing the absolute-immunity ruling and remanding. Judge Van Graafeiland dissented, placing greater weight on Selsky's appellate function and the practical importance of immunity from repeated inmate litigation. *Young*, 41 F.3d at 53-61.

Neither the historical judgment nor Stone's proposal establishes liability, eliminates qualified immunity, or supplies a new simulation holding until approved.

# 16. United States v. Shaoul

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 94-1093. **Decision date:** December 1, 1994. **Citation:** 41 F.3d 811. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Chief Judge Dominick L. DiCarlo of the Court of International Trade, sitting by designation. **Historical argument:** October 19, 1994. The appeal presents juror nondisclosure and jury-instruction issues following convictions for conspiracy and mail fraud. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/41/811/564137/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Jack Shaoul, an antiques dealer, submitted insurance claims arising from two alleged losses. In the first, he reported paintings and other items stolen from his car in January 1991. His employee Edward Purcell testified that a painting attributed to Albert Pinkham Ryder remained in the shop and that Shaoul directed him to conceal it. Purcell photographed it beside a current newspaper after the reported theft. Louis Liskin described a backdated purchase-interest letter intended to support an inflated valuation and testified that he had understood the painting was not authentic.

The second scheme followed a genuine water leak at the shop in February 1991. Witnesses described moving previously broken glassware into the wet area and further damaging a purported Winslow Homer watercolor that had been identified as inauthentic. The valuation documents included an invoice for a sale that the named gallery owner testified had not occurred. Purcell and Liskin testified under nonprosecution agreements, a fact relevant to credibility rather than a reason to disregard their evidence automatically. Other witnesses and documents provided corroboration.

The jury convicted Shaoul of one conspiracy count and ten substantive mail-fraud counts. Before deliberations the judge gave a general unanimity instruction, explained that the government need not prove every alleged overt act, and twice required unanimity concerning the material falsity of the relevant representations. When the jury asked whether all overt acts had to be proved, the court repeated that one was sufficient. Defense counsel did not object to the original instructions or that response.

After the verdict, the government learned that a juror was the uncle of an assistant United States attorney's wife. The prosecutor-relative worked in the same district but had no involvement in this prosecution. The juror had not recalled the relative's employment during voir dire. When the government made the juror available at sentencing, counsel expressly conceded good faith and waived examination, arguing that the relationship alone required a new trial.

The district court denied that motion and the instruction challenge. It imposed concurrent forty-month prison terms, two years of supervised release, a $50,000 fine, the costs of imprisonment and supervision, and $13,356.90 restitution. The appellate issues do not independently challenge each sentencing component. *Shaoul*, 41 F.3d at 812-15.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final criminal judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, with trial jurisdiction supplied by 18 U.S.C. § 3231. The denial of a new-trial motion is reviewed for abuse of discretion, including independent examination of the legal rule used to assess nondisclosure and implied bias.

Preservation differs between the claims. Counsel deliberately relinquished the opportunity to question the juror and expressly declined to allege dishonesty. That is not merely a failure to object. The claimed legal consequence of the disclosed relationship remains available for review on the resulting record. By contrast, the instruction objections were not made when the court could correct the charge or its response and receive plain-error review under Rules 30 and 52(b).

*United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), requires an error that is plain and affects substantial rights, followed by the appropriate discretionary assessment of its effect on the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. A preferred additional instruction is not necessarily a legally required instruction, and an unpreserved omission is not presumed prejudicial.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood*, 464 U.S. 548, 556 (1984), requires a materially dishonest voir dire answer and a showing that an accurate answer would have supported a challenge for cause. *United States v. Langford*, 990 F.2d 65, 68 (2d Cir. 1993), does not delete the first requirement. Its examination of the second followed a finding that dishonesty existed. Counsel's express concession of good faith therefore defeats this particular nondisclosure route on the record he elected to submit.

That does not establish that a genuinely biased juror may serve simply because an answer was honest. An independent bias claim must be distinguished from failure to satisfy *McDonough*. Shaoul's difficulty is evidentiary and legal: the record establishes a particular indirect relationship to a prosecutor uninvolved in his case, not actual bias or a close connection to the prosecuting team.

*United States v. Colombo*, 869 F.2d 149, 152 (2d Cir. 1989), rejected an excessively broad suggestion that prosecutorial family relationships could never justify cause challenges. Its discussion of relatives of lawyers actually involved in the investigation or prosecution does not require exclusion of every relative of every lawyer employed by the office. The distinction concerns a source of potential partiality, not an arbitrary preference for one degree of kinship. Conversely, *Garland v. United States*, 182 F.2d 801, 802 (4th Cir. 1950), is not binding and should not be read to make family ties categorically irrelevant.

The instruction issue is governed by *United States v. Schiff*, 801 F.2d 108, 114-15 (2d Cir. 1986), *United States v. Harris*, 8 F.3d 943, 945 (2d Cir. 1993), and the earlier line including *United States v. Natelli*, 527 F.2d 311, 325 (2d Cir. 1975), and *United States v. Remington*, 191 F.2d 246, 250 (2d Cir. 1951). A general unanimity charge ordinarily suffices; unusual complexity or a genuine risk that jurors will convict on incompatible factual bases can require greater specificity. *United States v. Peterson*, 768 F.2d 64, 68 (2d Cir. 1985), also constrains an attempt to turn an advisable additional charge into plain error.

Shaoul's strongest point is that several acts and alleged falsehoods could permit different jurors to select different predicates. But the actual mail-fraud charge expressly required unanimity concerning material falsity. The overt-act question asked whether every act had to be established; it did not necessarily reveal disagreement over which act occurred. The judge's accurate answer did not suspend the general unanimity command. The appeal need not decide every abstract question about whether jurors must agree on a specific overt act if the charge is adequate even assuming the proposed agreement requirement.

The remaining instruction objections do not establish a different offense from the one charged. Describing the alleged falsehoods more particularly is not necessarily a constructive broadening of the indictment. Likewise, a stipulation that mailings occurred does not remove the separate requirement that they have the required relationship to the scheme. The entire charge, rather than the stipulation sentence in isolation, supplies the legal direction.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance. The right to an impartial jury is fundamental, but the application must begin with what is established about this juror, not a speculative connection to the prosecutor's office. Counsel conceded an honest omission and elected not to develop an evidentiary record when the juror was available. Stone should enforce that choice while deciding the asserted per se legal rule on its merits.

The proposed rule should be no broader than necessary: this indirect relationship to an uninvolved prosecutor does not, standing alone, establish disqualifying implied bias. A different relationship, participation in the case, exposure to information about the prosecution, or actual evidence of partiality could require a different inquiry. Stone should not equate office-wide professional association with participation in a particular criminal case, but neither should he endorse *Garland* as eliminating family-relationship objections altogether.

The instruction challenge fails under the existing unanimity precedents and the plain-error posture. The charge addressed each count, directed unanimity, and specifically emphasized agreement on material falsehoods. The supplemental response corrected a misunderstanding about how many overt acts the government had to prove; it did not authorize a verdict assembled from mutually exclusive individual findings. There is no demonstrated complexity or contradictory submission sufficient to displace the ordinary rule on this record.

Stone should not rely on the apparent strength of the fraud case to excuse an actually biased jury. The juror claim fails because the relevant form of dishonesty or bias was not established, not because the photographs and witnesses would probably have produced the same verdict before another tribunal. The instructional claim likewise should be resolved by the charge and the governing error standard, rather than a general declaration that a defendant against whom the evidence is strong needs fewer procedural protections.

The opinion may explain why a specific unanimity instruction often provides useful clarity, but it should distinguish recommended trial practice from a new mandatory appellate rule. Affirmance leaves the criminal judgment intact. No separate evidentiary hearing is required to explore a matter counsel expressly declined to develop without alleging bad faith or another factual basis for bias. Master Jurisprudence §§ 4, 8-9; Criminal Procedure and Punishment; Evidence, Proof, and the Record.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the conceded honest nondisclosure does not satisfy *McDonough*; the particular indirect relationship to an uninvolved prosecutor does not independently establish automatic disqualification; the actual charge presents no reversible unanimity error.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** affirm on the conceded failure of the nondisclosure claim and the absence of a demonstrated independent bias basis, while limiting the kinship discussion to these facts. The court may assume without deciding the asserted specific-predicate unanimity requirement because the instructions adequately protected it here.

**Non-join limits:** family ties to a prosecutor never matter; honest nondisclosure immunizes actual bias; strength of guilt evidence cures a structurally partial tribunal; or every conspiracy case requires a newly prescribed special verdict absent governing authority.

The historical panel was J. Edward Lumbard, Frank X. Altimari, and José A. Cabranes. Stone provisionally replaces Cabranes, leaving Lumbard, Altimari, and Stone. Cabranes was serving on the circuit by the October argument; he should not be retrospectively inserted into panels earlier in 1994. The proposal preserves the actual chronology rather than selecting colleagues by their anticipated votes.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Cabranes wrote for a unanimous panel affirming the judgment. Judges Lumbard and Altimari joined; there was no separate opinion. *Shaoul*, 41 F.3d at 815-18.

Stone's proposed disposition is the same. His formulation expressly preserves independent actual-bias principles and confines the kinship conclusion to the relationship and lack of prosecutorial involvement established in this record. No simulated unanimity is assumed.

# 17. United States v. Duncan

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-1402. **Decision date:** December 2, 1994. **Citation:** 42 F.3d 97. **Court below:** District of Connecticut, Judge T. F. Gilroy Daly. **Historical argument:** April 13, 1994. The appeal concerns expert testimony, the temporal reach of bank-fraud liability, a multi-object conspiracy's timeliness, and the leadership sentencing adjustment. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/42/97/604518/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Vinal S. Duncan was a director of Security Savings & Loan Association and a member of its loan committee. In 1983, he and other committee members arranged to purchase two Meriden Road parcels through a nominee while concealing their beneficial ownership from the remaining directors. They used a bank-financed mortgage, leased property to the bank, and subsequently approved sales to the bank without disclosing their interests. The first parcel sold for $200,000 in October 1984 and the second for $135,000 in April 1985. The transactions generated $135,000 above the initial purchase amount, and the beneficial owners received distributions through checks of less than $10,000.

The government also prosecuted a separate political-corruption arrangement. Duncan, Richard Barbieri, and John Corpaci formed the Taft Group in 1986. The group provided benefits to Waterbury Mayor Joseph Santopietro and associates in return for favorable official treatment. Barbieri and Corpaci, who pleaded guilty and cooperated, described Duncan's knowledge and participation. One transaction involved $63,000 paid through a lease of uninhabitable office space. Duncan disputed guilty knowledge and intent, rather than denying that the transactions and corrupt payments occurred.

IRS Agent Thomas Mulligan testified about the financial records, the tax system, false returns, and movement of funds. Duncan objected that portions of this expert testimony supplied legal conclusions. The agent had participated in the investigation and reviewed the records; he was not simply assessing the credibility of other witnesses from their trial testimony.

The jury convicted Duncan on Count One, a conspiracy with IRS, bank-regulatory, and bank-fraud objects; Count Two, bank fraud; Count Four, conspiracy to make corrupt payments; and Count Five, corrupt payments under 18 U.S.C. § 666(a)(2). The verdict specifically identified a bank-fraud conspiratorial object and an overt act after October 12, 1984. The court imposed concurrent prison terms, the longest sixty-five months, applying a four-level leadership adjustment to the guideline counts and a separate two-level perjury adjustment. The appellate sentencing challenge concerns leadership, not every adjustment or term. *Duncan*, 42 F.3d at 99-101.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

Appellate jurisdiction lies under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; sentencing review also follows 18 U.S.C. § 3742. The district court's criminal jurisdiction arises under 18 U.S.C. § 3231. Admission of expert testimony is reviewed for abuse of discretion, with independent review of the boundary between permissible assistance and an impermissible legal conclusion. Ex post facto and statutory questions receive independent review. Supported findings about Duncan's actual role receive clear-error review.

The expert objection must be separated from harmlessness. For preserved nonconstitutional error in a criminal trial, the relevant source is Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) and *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), not the civil-trial rule. The government, as beneficiary of a preserved error, cannot avoid that inquiry merely by asserting that the defendant failed to prove prejudice.

The limitations argument attacks the IRS object of Count One. The bank-fraud object provides the independently identified basis described in the verdict. The appeal should not be expanded into an unraised dispute about every possible limitations provision, nor should a general-verdict rule be used without examining what this jury actually found.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Rules 702 and 704(a) permit expert assistance and opinions embracing factual issues the jury must decide. They do not permit an expert to instruct the jury on the governing law or announce that the accused committed the charged offense. *United States v. Bilzerian*, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991), *United States v. Scop*, 846 F.2d 135, 140-42 (2d Cir.), modified, 856 F.2d 5 (1988), and *Hygh v. Jacobs*, 961 F.2d 359, 363-65 (2d Cir. 1992), establish that boundary.

The government's strongest argument is that Mulligan organized a complicated financial record and explained why particular filings were false, drawing on his investigation and document review. He did not state that Duncan was guilty of the charged bank fraud or conspiracies. *United States v. Schwartz*, 924 F.2d 410, 425-26 (2d Cir. 1991), recognizes the significance of an investigator also testifying about the investigation itself.

Duncan's strongest response concerns substance rather than the witness's exact vocabulary. References to money laundering and to filings obstructing the IRS can carry an official legal judgment even without the sentence “the defendant is guilty.” The fact that a label describes an uncharged offense does not automatically make it admissible. Nor does personal participation in an investigation authorize a witness to assess other witnesses' credibility or apply the criminal law for the jury. The court must examine the particular testimony in context, not award law-enforcement witnesses an exemption from *Scop*.

The Ex Post Facto Clause forbids application of § 1344 to an offense completed before that statute took effect on October 12, 1984. *United States v. Torres*, 901 F.2d 205, 226 (2d Cir. 1990), and *United States v. Ferrara*, 458 F.2d 868, 874 (2d Cir. 1972), permit application to criminal activity continuing after the relevant enactment. *United States v. Fletcher*, 928 F.2d 495, 500 (2d Cir. 1991), and *United States v. Knuckles*, 581 F.2d 305, 313 (2d Cir. 1978), explain that an agreement whose object includes receipt of anticipated profits may continue until that object is realized.

Duncan characterizes the wrong as appropriation of a corporate opportunity in 1983. The prosecution's charged theory was broader in a legally material way: purchase through a concealed nominee followed by profitable resale to the bank. The 1985 sale was execution of that intended extraction of bank funds, not merely a later effort to conceal an already completed crime. That difference permits prospective application without holding that any subsequent concealment indefinitely prolongs liability.

*Griffin v. United States*, 502 U.S. 46 (1991), distinguishes an evidentially unsupported alternative from a legally impermissible basis for conviction. It should not be summarized as a rule that one good object always cures another object that is legally unavailable. Here, the verdict's express identification of bank-fraud conspiracy and its timing finding make the relevant valid basis ascertainable without merely presuming that a silent general verdict selected it. The asserted infirmity of the IRS object therefore need not invalidate the separately established basis for Count One.

Section 3B1.1(a) supplies the four-level increase for an organizer or leader of sufficiently numerous or extensive criminal activity. Duncan did not challenge the size or extensiveness predicate, but denied being a leader. *United States v. Garcia*, 936 F.2d 648, 656 (2d Cir. 1991), requires clear-error review of the role finding. Corporate title alone is insufficient; planning, authority, and actual participation in directing the criminal operation must support the conclusion.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance on the issues presented, while carefully limiting the expert-testimony rationale. Mulligan could explain the financial record, identify discrepancies in filings, and describe how the transactions affected tax administration. The testimony as described did not repeatedly instruct the jury in the statutory elements or announce that Duncan possessed the criminal intent required for conviction. That is a material distinction from the sustained legal conclusions and credibility judgments condemned in *Scop*.

Stone should not adopt a categorical rule that an investigating agent may give legal conclusions because he has personal knowledge or because the conclusion names an uncharged crime. The permitted function is factual explanation within the witness's demonstrated expertise. A statement that falsified filings interfere with tax administration can be evaluated as part of that explanation; an instruction that the accused thereby committed the charged conspiracy would cross the line. The proposed affirmance rests on the actual limited use, not on privileged status for an official witness.

If a particular phrase is treated as improperly conclusory, Stone should apply the criminal harmless-error standard. The documentary proof, direct testimony concerning the transactions, and instruction that law-enforcement witnesses receive neither special deference nor special suspicion support a finding that the isolated phrase did not substantially influence the verdict. The analysis must evaluate its role in this trial, not merely repeat that sufficient evidence remained. He should expressly avoid allocating to Duncan the civil litigant's burden of proving prejudice from a preserved evidentiary error.

The ex post facto objection fails because obtaining the premium through sale to the bank was a central part of the charged scheme. The April 1985 transaction and resulting distributions are not incidental aftereffects of a completed 1983 acquisition. The jury's post-enactment finding makes the temporal predicate explicit. Stone need not rely on the later agreement to continue concealment in 1990, and should not suggest that such concealment alone would indefinitely continue every fraud.

For Count One, the express bank-fraud finding supplies a valid conspiratorial basis independent of the challenged IRS object. The court can reserve the latter object's timeliness rather than mistakenly expand *Griffin* to every legally invalid alternative. That approach respects both the indictment's identified objectives and what the jury actually decided.

The leadership finding also survives. Duncan's presidency is only a starting fact. His partners described recurring planning discussions, his central role in real-estate ventures, and his involvement in specific benefits, including the sham office lease. The district court could credit that account over his description of passive participation. An appellate judge cannot substitute a different credibility choice merely because other participants also exercised authority. Affirmance leaves the judgment in place without resolving an unraised challenge to the perjury adjustment. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8-9; Criminal Procedure and Punishment; Sentencing; Evidence, Proof, and the Record.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the challenged testimony is permissible factual assistance in context; independently, any isolated conclusory phrase is harmless under the criminal standard; the post-enactment execution of the scheme and explicit verdict defeat the temporal objections; the leadership finding is supported.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** assume without deciding that the closest-to-conclusory phrase was improper and affirm only after applying Rule 52(a) and *Kotteakos* to its actual effect. The remaining holdings can stay confined to the 1985 sale, the express bank-fraud verdict, and the supported role findings.

**Non-join limits:** a law-enforcement exemption from the prohibition on legal conclusions; a civil harmless-error burden in a preserved criminal evidentiary claim; retroactive punishment for a completed pre-enactment offense; one valid conspiracy object automatically curing every legally invalid alternative; or a leadership increase from corporate title alone.

## Panel and proposed assignment

The historical panel was Wilfred Feinberg, Lawrence W. Pierce, and Roger J. Miner. Stone provisionally replaces Miner, yielding Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone. The April 1994 argument fits the stated service year subject to the common eligibility and conflicts qualifications.

## Historical disposition and comparison

Judge Pierce wrote for a unanimous panel affirming the convictions and sentence. Judges Feinberg and Miner joined; there was no separate opinion. The opinion held Mulligan's testimony admissible and added an alternative harmlessness discussion that invoked Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 61 and civil authorities concerning prejudice. *Duncan*, 42 F.3d at 101-07.

Stone's proposed result matches the historical judgment but would not reproduce that civil-rule formulation in a criminal appeal. He would also state the limitation on *Griffin* expressly and ground the conspiracy disposition in the actual verdict rather than a sweeping presumption about general verdicts.

# 18. American Geophysical Union v. Texaco, Inc.

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 92-9341. **Selected version:** December 23, 1994 amendment on denial of rehearing, 37 F.3d 881. **Original decision:** October 28, 1994. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Pierre N. Leval. **Historical argument:** May 20, 1993. [1994 opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/37/881/509400/).

The additional citation supplied in the request, **60 F.3d 913**, belongs to a further-amended version dated **July 17, 1995**. It identifies the same litigation, not a nineteenth case, and its later additions are not governing material for this December 1994 simulation. The chronology and version distinction are preserved in the case list.

## Material facts and proceedings below

The American Geophysical Union and eighty-two other publishers sued Texaco over unauthorized photocopying of scientific and technical articles. Texaco employed approximately 400 to 500 researchers nationally and maintained a substantial research library at Beacon, New York. Its subscriptions to the Journal of Catalysis increased from one to two in 1983 and to three in 1988.

The parties deliberately narrowed an initial trial to a written record addressing the fair-use defense. They randomly selected researcher Dr. Donald H. Chickering II and focused on eight articles copied from the Journal of Catalysis and retained in his files. Chickering was a chemical engineer conducting catalysis research. He or other Texaco employees made complete copies for his use. Six articles came to his attention through circulated journal issues; two through references in other publications.

The copies were principally retained for possible later consultation rather than used immediately in a particular experiment. Chickering did not ultimately use five of them. That fact does not mean the copying lacked a research purpose: reviewing literature, identifying possible future work, and maintaining accessible references are ordinary parts of research. It does matter to the distinct question whether the copying created convenient substitutes for additional authorized copies.

Academic Press generally owned copyrights in the individual articles through assignments from authors, who were not paid for publication. The articles were predominantly factual scientific writings. The record also addressed the Copyright Clearance Center's licensing arrangements, the feasibility of licensing this kind of institutional copying, and the limited evidence of additional subscription demand absent photocopying.

Judge Leval held that the selected copying was not fair use and certified the ruling for interlocutory appeal. *American Geophysical Union v. Texaco, Inc.*, 802 F. Supp. 1 (S.D.N.Y. 1992). The limited-issue procedure did not decide the legality of every photocopy made by every scientist, the damages for all claimed infringements, or the proper scope of an injunction. *American Geophysical Union*, 37 F.3d at 883-85.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The district court had copyright jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a); the certified interlocutory appeal proceeds under § 1292(b). The appellate issue is the fair-use conclusion on the stipulated representative record. Findings of historical fact receive clear-error review, while the legal weighing and ultimate fair-use determination are reviewed independently. *Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises*, 471 U.S. 539 (1985); *Twin Peaks Productions, Inc. v. Publications International, Ltd.*, 996 F.2d 1366, 1374 (2d Cir. 1993).

Texaco bears the burden on its affirmative defense. The parties' stipulation controls the issue's scope and does not convert the selected conduct into a legislative record authorizing categorical rules for all scientific institutions. *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), is available and must inform the analysis even though the historical argument preceded it.

The copying date, relevant market conditions, and the scope of any remedy remain significant. The existence of a workable licensing mechanism at the time examined cannot automatically establish damages for earlier conduct under materially different conditions. Those temporal and remedial questions must remain within the issues actually submitted and the proof developed.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 106 establishes the copyright owner's reproduction right, subject to the limitations that include § 107. Section 107 identifies research as a potentially fair purpose and requires consideration of purpose and character, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the actual or potential market. Those considerations must be weighed together. They are not votes on a four-item scorecard, and transformation is not an extra statutory element that every fair use must satisfy.

**Purpose and character.** Texaco's strongest argument is that these were copies for a scientist's actual research, not resale, public redistribution, or replacement publication. *Wright v. Warner Books, Inc.*, 953 F.2d 731, 736 (2d Cir. 1991), and *New Era Publications International, ApS v. Carol Publishing Group*, 904 F.2d 152, 156 (2d Cir. 1990), give genuine weight to the listed purposes. Employment by a profit-making company does not strip scientific activity of that character. *Campbell* rejects an automatic commercial-use presumption and makes the particular use important.

The publishers respond that the relevant act is complete reproduction for a personal reference collection supplied at the employer's expense. The research that may eventually produce new knowledge does not transform every reproduced input. Keeping a permanent working copy can fulfill substantially the same reading and reference function as an authorized copy. That is a legitimate consideration, although it does not itself defeat fair use: *Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.*, 464 U.S. 417 (1984), demonstrates that complete, nontransformative copying can be fair in an appropriate setting.

**Nature of the work.** Published scientific articles receive copyright protection in their expression, but their factual character favors fair use. The underlying facts and discoveries are not copyrighted merely because the article reporting them is protected. See *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991). The unpaid status of authors is relevant to the incentive argument, but does not negate their assignments or erase the publisher's legally recognized interest in producing and distributing journals.

**Amount and substantiality.** The relevant copyrighted works are the individual articles, not the journal issue as a whole. Copying eight complete articles is not rendered quantitatively small by comparing each with a two-hundred-page volume. Nevertheless, completeness must be assessed against the legitimate purpose: a scientist may need an entire methods or results discussion to understand a paper. The question is whether the amount is justified in the particular fair-use balance, not whether any complete copy automatically fails.

**Market effect.** Lost subscriptions and copying-license revenues are different markets. The evidence does not justify assuming that Texaco would buy a subscription for every researcher if photocopying were unavailable. The publishers' stronger contention is that a functioning mechanism existed for licensing this repeated institutional reproduction. A traditional, reasonable, or likely-to-develop licensing market can be cognizable under *Campbell*, but the owner cannot establish market injury merely by announcing a fee for conduct that would otherwise be fair. Otherwise, every fair use would disappear when a copyright owner offered to sell permission.

The material distinction is between the market for ordinary substitute reproductions and an asserted right to control criticism, commentary, or another use the law protects without permission. The record concerning actual institutional licensing and the nature of the archived copies supports the former characterization here. The dissenting argument remains substantial: the market itself can depend on the legal ruling, and ordinary research photocopying may produce public benefits far exceeding any demonstrated subscription loss. The court must evaluate those concerns rather than assume that a license demand proves its own validity.

*Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States*, 487 F.2d 1345 (Ct. Cl. 1973), affirmed by an equally divided Court, 420 U.S. 376 (1975), provides an important comparison involving governmental medical-library copying. The equally divided affirmance did not create controlling Supreme Court reasoning. The Court of Claims decision is persuasive, with differences concerning institutional purpose, the factual copying system, and licensing-market conditions. It cannot be dismissed merely because it is older, or treated as binding on materially different copying.

The older fair-use inquiry in *Folsom v. Marsh*, 9 F. Cas. 342, 348 (C.C.D. Mass. 1841), asks about purpose, quantity, value, and substitution. Pierre N. Leval's *Toward a Fair Use Standard*, 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1105 (1990), is useful persuasive writing on productive use, not a statute or binding precedent. Section 108's library provisions also do not displace § 107: failure to qualify for a specific library exception does not independently defeat fair use.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance of the limited fair-use ruling, with a narrower rationale than a categorical preference for licensed corporate copying. The case concerns complete articles placed in an employer-supported reference collection under conditions in which a practical reproduction-licensing market exists. It does not establish that a scientist must purchase permission for every temporary, spontaneous, critical, or otherwise justified use of scientific literature.

The analysis should credit the research purpose fully. A scientific article need not be used in an immediate experiment to assist research. Nor does an employer's profit motive make the scientist's intellectual activity legally fictitious. The first factor therefore contains considerations favoring Texaco, alongside the nontransformative creation of convenient substitutes for authorized copies. Stone should describe that mixed assessment rather than treat corporate status or the word “archival” as dispositive. A personal working file is still the researcher's file; its reproduction function, not an inflated institutional label, supplies the relevant concern.

The second factor favors Texaco because the works disseminate factual scientific knowledge. That weight is real. Yet § 107 does not make all complete copies of factual publications fair. The third factor examines the reproduction of each protected article in full. The research purpose can explain why excerpts would be less useful, but the resulting copy still performs the complete article's ordinary reference function. Necessity to the user's preferred convenience is not by itself necessity sufficient to defeat the reproduction right.

The market analysis must avoid circularity. A newly asserted price cannot make an otherwise fair use unlawful. Here, however, the claim concerns an ordinary market for additional usable copies, with evidence of an operational licensing system for institutional article reproduction. The relevant loss is not a speculative subscription for every employee, and the court need not assume that such purchases would occur. It is substitution for authorized reproductions in a demonstrated market connected to the statutory right. That gives the fourth factor substantial weight without making it supreme in every case.

On the combination of complete retained copies, their substitute function, and the workable licensing evidence, the defense should fail. The conclusion does not follow from counting factors or from finding the use unworthy. It follows from identifying a particular reproduction practice whose public research benefit, although substantial, does not on this record justify displacing the existing means of obtaining additional authorized copies.

Stone should expressly answer the concern that scientific authors often seek dissemination rather than royalties. Copyright does not protect only works written for cash payment. Authors may assign rights, and publishers incur the institutional work of assembling and disseminating scientific journals. Conversely, that observation supplies no right to monopolize facts, prevent independent experiments, or collect a fee whenever a researcher learns from an article. The existing statutory balance protects expression and permitted uses separately.

The mandate should affirm the interlocutory determination and return the remaining proceedings to the district court. It should not set damages, direct a universal licensing system, or automatically enjoin all research copying. The opinion should reserve materially different copying and any period in which the asserted licensing opportunity was not genuinely available. This is an application of ordinary copyright law to a commercial defendant, not a constitutional judgment about the relative civic worth of publishers and scientists. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8, 20, 24-25; Statutory Interpretation; Markets and Private Power; Records, Communications, and New Instruments.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the complete, retained reference copies on the stipulated record are not fair use when their substitute function and the demonstrated institutional licensing market are weighed with the genuine research purpose and factual character of the works.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** affirm only this eight-article determination, expressly reserving other research practices, copying without a workable relevant licensing market, and distinct historical periods. The first factor can be treated as mixed, or its precise direction reserved, provided the overall statutory balance is explained rather than mechanically counted.

**Non-join limits:** all corporate research copying is commercial and therefore unlawful; transformation is an indispensable element of fair use; a license offer alone establishes a legally protected market; a journal issue rather than the individually copyrighted article necessarily supplies the denominator; or a ruling about fair use automatically determines damages and injunctive scope.
