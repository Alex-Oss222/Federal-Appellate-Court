#### Issue 1: Functional absolute immunity for administrative review of prison discipline

**Preservation and reach:** Young appeals dismissal on absolute immunity. The parties supplied or accepted functional materials describing Selsky’s role; those materials do not establish the merits of all five challenged rulings.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the legal immunity classification. Unresolved conduct and injury questions remain for the proper factfinder.

**Governing test and burdens:** The official bears the burden of showing that the particular function warrants absolute rather than qualified immunity. Relevant features include protection from harassment, independence, procedural safeguards, precedent, adversarial process, and correction on appeal. Title or an appellate label is not dispositive.

Absolute immunity is functional, not title-based. *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872), and *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), protect the judicial function under the conditions those decisions recognize. *Butz v. Economou*, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978), extends comparable protection to particular administrative adjudication because of its functional resemblance to the judicial process and its safeguards. It does not turn every official who reviews a decision into a judge.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985), is especially important. It denied absolute immunity to prison disciplinary officials whose decisionmaking lacked the independence and safeguards characteristic of courts. Relevant considerations include insulation from pressure, procedural protections, adversarial presentation, use of precedent, the availability of correction through review, and the need to protect adjudication from harassment. *Burns v. Reed*, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991), cautions against expanding absolute immunity when the official has not established its justification.

Selsky's strongest position is that his function is appellate rather than frontline discipline. He reviews a large volume of determinations across many institutions, is not ordinarily involved in the underlying incident, and must be free to decide without repeated damages actions by dissatisfied inmates. Administrative and Article 78 review provide safeguards against erroneous decisions. Judicial immunity cannot depend on whether a reviewing official ultimately decided correctly.

Young's strongest response concerns structural independence, not dissatisfaction with results. An official who advises hearing officers during the very proceedings he later reviews, remains subject to correctional management, and lacks several ordinary adjudicative protections is not necessarily comparable to an independent appellate judge. A centralized office reduces some local pressures but does not eliminate the department's institutional interests. The same functional analysis that protects genuine adjudication requires scrutiny of that distinction.

*Dorman v. Higgins*, 821 F.2d 133, 137 (2d Cir. 1987), concerning preparation of a presentence report as an arm of a court, does not dictate immunity for an official located within prison administration. *Shelly v. Johnson*, 849 F.2d 228 (6th Cir. 1988), is persuasive rather than binding and concerns a materially different state adjudicative arrangement. *Richardson v. Selsky*, 5 F.3d 616 (2d Cir. 1993), does not establish a categorical absolute immunity for Selsky's appellate role.

The distinction between correction and compensation also matters. *Walker v. Bates*, 23 F.3d 652, 658 (2d Cir. 1994), is published circuit authority by this decision date. Its treatment of punishment already served prevents the bare availability of administrative reversal from being treated as a complete answer to an earlier deprivation.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Bradley v. Fisher, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872), and Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), concern judicial functions. Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978), supplies the administrative functional comparison. Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985), denies absolute immunity to the prison disciplinary body at issue. Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991), places the burden on the claimant to immunity.

**Other governing authority:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983; Dorman v. Higgins, 821 F.2d 133, 137 (2d Cir. 1987), concerning a probation officer’s court-integrated function, distinguishable; Richardson v. **Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose reversal of the absolute-immunity dismissal and remand for the remaining questions. The office's name and position on an organizational chart cannot substitute for the functional comparison required by *Butz* and *Cleavinger*. Selsky's participation in advising hearing officers, his relation to correctional superiors, and the limited adversarial safeguards are directly relevant to that comparison.

The rationale should not be that inmates need someone to sue, or that a large caseload demonstrates indifference. Neither proposition establishes the absence of immunity. The narrower legal point is that absolute immunity requires the official to demonstrate a judicially comparable function with the relevant independence and safeguards. Central review of prison discipline, while important and adjudicative in a general sense, has not been shown here to carry the combination that justifies complete protection from damages.

Stone should confront the harassment concern rather than dismiss it. An official deciding thousands of appeals faces genuine litigation burdens. But qualified immunity, requirements of personal responsibility, pleading and evidentiary rules, and adjudication of meritless claims already perform protective work. The frequency of disappointed appellants cannot alone establish absolute immunity, because that reasoning would apply equally to the disciplinary bodies addressed in *Cleavinger*.

The absence of absolute immunity does not make affirmance of a disciplinary ruling automatically unconstitutional. Young must establish a protected interest, a violation of the process actually owed, Selsky's actionable involvement, causation, and an available remedy. An official may affirm a ruling after a reasonable assessment of legitimate security grounds for denying witnesses. Whether Selsky could reasonably have understood the challenged decisions as lawful must be addressed under the qualified-immunity law applicable when he acted. *Weg v. Macchiarola*, 995 F.2d 15, 18 (2d Cir. 1993), supports leaving an unresolved immunity question for appropriate further proceedings rather than deciding contested predicates without a record.

The remand should permit development of the particular five appeals and the reasons for the witness rulings. It should also distinguish damages from requests for prospective relief: a damages immunity does not automatically resolve every form of relief, and prospective relief still requires an appropriate defendant and a live, remediable injury. Young receives a forum to prove a claim, not an appellate award of $200 per day or a finding that all challenged disciplinary records must be erased.
