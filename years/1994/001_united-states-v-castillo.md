#### Issue 1: Prior consistent statements, limiting instructions, and prosecutorial use

**Preservation and reach:** Castillo objected to the officers’ repetition of Johnson’s reports and challenged the limiting instructions and summation use. Johnson’s own initial account was admitted without objection; preservation must be assessed for the particular testimony and argument, not treated as uniform.

**Standard of review:** Abuse of discretion for admission and rehabilitation; independent review of the evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional error is assessed under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos.

**Governing test and burdens:** Under the then-current Rule 801(d)(1)(B), the government conceded that the motive to fabricate preceded the statements. Nonsubstantive rehabilitation therefore requires probative force beyond repetition, such as genuine clarification of an asserted inconsistency. A limiting instruction must be evaluated against the actual use at trial.

The government's strongest evidentiary argument is that the defense created an apparent inconsistency, not merely a general credibility attack. *United States v. Pierre*, 781 F.2d 329, 331-33 (2d Cir. 1986), permits a consistent statement with rehabilitative force beyond repetition to explain a claimed inconsistency. *United States v. Brennan*, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986), likewise recognizes that limited use. But *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), does not permit a party simply to multiply its witness's accusations through additional mouths.

The prior statements did not qualify for substantive admission under Rule 801(d)(1)(B): the asserted motive to explain Johnson's cocaine ingestion already existed when he made them. The difficult question is consequently whether repeating the waistband account actually clarifies the difference between that account and the gunpoint description. Repetition can show consistency, but that alone is not the independent rehabilitative function required by the precedents. Its practical effect also matters where the prosecutor relies on the repeated accusation as corroboration that a firearm existed.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946): a preserved evidentiary error requires assessment of its substantial influence on the actual verdict; binding. No directly controlling Supreme Court holding deciding this particular rehabilitation use has been identified.

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B), 105; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). United States v. Pierre, 781 F.2d 329, 331-34 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Brennan, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Quinto, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), binding circuit limits.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** The defense manufactured an apparent contradiction. The statements explained that “gunpoint” described the same threatened display, and repeated instructions confined the jury to credibility rather than substantive use.

#### Issue 2: Personal right to testify and alleged interference by counsel

**Preservation and reach:** Raised through Castillo’s post-trial affidavit and on appeal. The affidavit’s disputed account is not a trial-court finding; the separate Rule 33 timing limit does not eliminate the need to distinguish direct-appeal reach from collateral litigation.

**Standard of review:** The allocation of the right is a legal question. Any ineffective-assistance claim requires the applicable constitutional standard and an adequate factual record; the appellate court may not find the attorney-client conversation itself.

**Governing test and burdens:** The defendant holds the personal choice to testify. Prior advice can establish awareness of the right, but disputed interference by counsel and a knowing decision not to testify are different inquiries. A conventional ineffective-assistance claim requires deficient performance and prejudice, not assumption of either.

The right to testify is personal. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987), identifies its constitutional importance. Prior advice that the right exists is relevant, but is not logically identical to a finding that Castillo understood he could override counsel at the later trial. Conversely, his later affidavit does not prove unconstitutional interference without attention to the trial record and counsel's account. The court should not decide a contested attorney-client episode through appellate factfinding.

**Component:** Prior consistent statements, limiting instructions, and prosecutorial use

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Castillo objected to the officers’ repetition of Johnson’s reports and challenged the limiting instructions and summation use. Johnson’s own initial account was admitted without objection; preservation must be assessed for the particular testimony and argument, not treated as uniform. **Proposed disposition:** Reverse the firearm conviction and remand for a new trial, not acquittal.

**Governing law:** Abuse of discretion for admission and rehabilitation; independent review of the evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional error is assessed under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos. Under the then-current Rule 801(d)(1)(B), the government conceded that the motive to fabricate preceded the statements. Nonsubstantive rehabilitation therefore requires probative force beyond repetition, such as genuine clarification of an asserted inconsistency. A limiting instruction must be evaluated against the actual use at trial.

Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946): a preserved evidentiary error requires assessment of its substantial influence on the actual verdict; binding. No directly controlling Supreme Court holding deciding this particular rehabilitation use has been identified. Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B), 105; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). United States v. Pierre, 781 F.2d 329, 331-34 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Brennan, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Quinto, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), binding circuit limits.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** A statement outside Rule 801(d)(1)(B) cannot be admitted as rehabilitation merely because it repeats the trial accusation; on this record the repetition and use had a prejudicial corroborative effect.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose reversal of the firearm conviction for a new trial because the repeated reports lacked a sufficiently distinct rehabilitative purpose and the error cannot confidently be treated as harmless. His reason is not distrust of police testimony as a category. It is the evidentiary structure of this prosecution: one eyewitness supplied the disputed firearm evidence, the weapon was not recovered, the initial report omitted it, and other officers' repetition risked supplying the corroboration the investigation itself lacked.

*Pierre* should be applied, not repudiated. A prior statement may resolve ambiguity when its context explains why two accounts are compatible. Here the central defect is that additional witnesses repeated the same substantive accusation. Calling that use rehabilitation does not explain how it resolves the asserted discrepancy. Nor does a limiting instruction necessarily neutralize a presentation that invites the jury to count multiple official witnesses as corroboration of a single source. The earlier *Castillo* decision makes particular care appropriate because it already identified the danger of bolstering the contested gun evidence; it does not conclusively decide harmlessness at a different trial.

Under *Kotteakos*, Stone should examine the actual prosecutorial use of the statements and their importance to this verdict. The government cannot prevail merely by identifying sufficient admissible evidence. Where the disputed element is supplied principally by the witness being bolstered, repetition by fellow officers can materially affect the jury's evaluation. A new trial, rather than acquittal, follows because the proposed error concerns the manner of proof, not a determination that no rational jury could find the firearm element.

**Independent or alternative considerations:** No additional rule is proposed beyond the interpretation and application stated here.
