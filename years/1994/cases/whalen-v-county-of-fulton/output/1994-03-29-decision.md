## Decision summary

### The case and the result

The individual officials named as defendants appealed from an interlocutory order of the United States District Court for the Northern District of New York (Frederick J. Scullin, Jr., District Judge). That order denied, without prejudice to renewal after discovery, their motion for summary judgment on the ground of qualified immunity. The record does not establish the date on which the order was entered. The officials asked for immediate review and for immunity from suit. The Whalens asked that the appeal be dismissed, or that the order permitting development of the record be left in place.

George and Elizabeth Whalen are the adoptive parents of Michael. They challenged child-welfare authorities' placement of Michael's biological sister, Elizabeth, with another family. They sued the two counties, their departments of social services, and the individual officials involved, alleging interference with association, denial of access to the courts, and violations of substantive and procedural due process, together with state-law claims. The record describes no established custodial relationship between the Whalens and the sister comparable to an existing parent-child household. No final judgment has been entered. The claims against the counties and departments and the state-law claims remain pending in the district court, and an immunity ruling for an individual official would not dispose of them. The court does not decide whether the notice of appeal was timely.

The court of appeals decided only whether it could hear the appeal now. It held that it could not. The order reserved the immunity question for decision on a developed record, so it does not conclusively determine the officials' entitlement to qualified immunity and is not an appealable collateral order. The court therefore dismissed the appeal for want of appellate jurisdiction and did not decide whether the right the Whalens assert was clearly established or whether the individual defendants are entitled to immunity.

Issues and standards of review:

1. Whether the court has appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the collateral-order doctrine over an order denying a qualified-immunity summary judgment motion without prejudice to renewal after discovery. This question is decided first and independently. The legal character of the order, and whether disputed facts are material to the defense, are reviewed de novo. The officials, who seek review before final judgment, must show that the order falls within the exception to the final-judgment rule.
2. If jurisdiction existed, whether the right the Whalens assert was clearly established, a question of law, with factual disputes governed by the rules of summary judgment. This issue is not reached by the court.
3. Whether any basis exists to review, with the officials' appeal, matters affecting the counties, the departments or the state-law claims. No basis is presented, and the court does not reach the question.

Judgment: The appeal is dismissed for want of appellate jurisdiction.  
Vote: Two to one.  
Opinion and joins: Opinion of the Court by Judge Meskill, joined in full by Judge Stone. Dissenting opinion by Judge Walker, joined by no other judge. Judge Stone writes no separate opinion.

Component results:

- **Appellate jurisdiction over the order.** The order does not conclusively determine the officials' entitlement to qualified immunity and is not an appealable collateral order. For this holding: Judge Meskill and Judge Stone. Against: Judge Walker, who would exercise limited jurisdiction over the legal immunity question.
- **Disposition of the appeal.** Dismissed. For: Judge Meskill and Judge Stone. Against: Judge Walker, who would reverse in the reviewable part and remand for entry of summary judgment for the individual defendants on the federal claims resting on the asserted right to the sister's placement.
- **Qualified immunity on the merits.** The court does not decide whether the asserted right was clearly established or whether the individual defendants are entitled to immunity. Judge Walker, in dissent, would hold that no clearly established right existed. No majority reaches the question.
- **Protected interest in sibling association.** No member of the panel decides whether adoptive parents or siblings hold a constitutionally protected interest in the placement of a sibling or in sibling association generally.
- **Counties, departments and state-law claims.** Unaffected by the judgment. All three judges would leave them to the district court.

Judges Meskill and Stone agree in the judgment and in the reasoning of the controlling proposition. Judge Walker disagrees with both.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

MESKILL, Senior Circuit Judge, joined by STONE, Circuit Judge:

The individual officials named as defendants in this civil rights action appeal from an order of the United States District Court for the Northern District of New York (Frederick J. Scullin, Jr., Judge) denying, without prejudice to renewal after discovery, their motion for summary judgment on the ground of qualified immunity. Whether we may hear that appeal now is the only question before us. We hold that the order does not conclusively determine the officials' entitlement to immunity and is therefore not an appealable collateral order. We dismiss the appeal for want of appellate jurisdiction.

**I. Background**

George and Elizabeth Whalen are the adoptive parents of Michael. They challenge the decision of child-welfare authorities to place Michael's biological sister, Elizabeth, with another family. They sued Fulton County and Montgomery County, their departments of social services, and the individual officials involved under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, alleging interference with association, denial of access to the courts, and violations of substantive and procedural due process. They also plead claims under state law. The record describes no established custodial relationship between the Whalens and the sister comparable to an existing parent-child household.

The individual officials moved for summary judgment on the ground of qualified immunity. The district court denied the motion without prejudice to its renewal after discovery. The claims against the counties and departments, and the state-law claims, remain pending below, and an immunity ruling in favor of an individual official would not dispose of them. This appeal concerns only the interlocutory order. No final judgment has been entered, and none has found a constitutional right to the joint placement of siblings.

**II. Appellate Jurisdiction**

*A. The governing rule.* Our jurisdiction extends to appeals from "final decisions" of the district courts. 28 U.S.C. § 1291. An order denying summary judgment is ordinarily not final, because it leaves the case pending and may be reviewed with the final judgment. The Supreme Court has recognized a "small class" of decisions that finally determine claims of right separable from, and collateral to, the rights asserted in the action, and that may be appealed at once. Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949). To come within that class, the order "must conclusively determine the disputed question, resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits of the action, and be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment." Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978). Cohen itself explained that the finality requirement excludes any decision that is "tentative, informal or incomplete." 337 U.S. at 546.

Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985), applied these requirements to qualified immunity. Immunity is an "entitlement not to stand trial or face the other burdens of litigation," id. at 526, and that entitlement is lost if a case is erroneously allowed to proceed. A district court's denial of a claim of qualified immunity, "to the extent that it turns on an issue of law," is therefore an appealable final decision. Id. at 530. The question the court of appeals then decides is a legal one: whether the facts the plaintiff alleges or can show describe a violation of clearly established law. Id. at 528. We have applied Mitchell with attention to its limits. Our jurisdiction is "limited to circumstances where the qualified immunity defense may be established as a matter of law," and when a factual determination is a necessary predicate to deciding whether immunity bars the suit, "review is postponed." Cartier v. Lussier, 955 F.2d 841, 844 (2d Cir. 1992); see Golino v. City of New Haven, 950 F.2d 864, 868 (2d Cir. 1991). Whether disputed facts are material to the defense is a legal question that we review de novo. Cartier, 955 F.2d at 844.

The officials ask us to review a non-final order, and so it is for them to show that it satisfies each of these requirements. We decide that question independently, and before we consider anything about the merits of the defense. The first requirement, a conclusive determination, disposes of the matter. Because the order fails it, we do not reach the requirements of separateness and effective unreviewability.

*B. What the order decided.* Whether an order conclusively determines a question depends on what the order did, not on what it is called. The words "without prejudice" settle nothing in either direction. An order that rejects an immunity defense as a matter of law does not become tentative because the district court describes the rejection as provisional, and an order that truly withholds decision does not become final because a party characterizes it as a rejection. We look to the substance.

The order before us denied the motion without prejudice and permitted the officials to renew it after discovery. By that provision the district court did not answer the question the officials put to it, namely whether, on the claims as pleaded and the facts as they stand, the individual defendants are entitled to judgment because the right the Whalens assert was not clearly established. It postponed that question until the record is fuller, and it expressly preserved the officials' ability to press the defense at that time. The immunity defense therefore remains alive and undecided in the district court. A decision that by its own terms invites renewal on a developed record is the kind of tentative and incomplete ruling that the finality requirement excludes. There is no settled legal determination for us to review. If we decided the immunity question now, we would not be reviewing the district court's answer to it. We would be supplying an answer the district court has not given.

That conclusion is consistent with Mitchell. The entitlement Mitchell protects is the right to have the legal question of immunity decided correctly before the burdens of litigation are imposed, and Mitchell permits an immediate appeal because a conclusive ruling against the defense cannot be undone if review waits. Where the district court has not ruled against the defense, and has preserved it for decision, the entitlement has not been denied. It remains to be adjudicated in the forum in which it was raised. Cartier states the point from the other direction: where a factual determination is a necessary predicate to resolving whether immunity bars the suit, review is postponed. The order here contemplates just that sequence, development of the record followed by decision. We do not decide whether the district court was right that further record is needed. That question is not before us in a posture that permits review, because the order did not conclusively decide it.

*C. Musso and Dube.* The officials rely on Musso v. Hourigan, 836 F.2d 736 (2d Cir. 1988), and Dube v. State University of New York, 900 F.2d 587 (2d Cir. 1990), in which we exercised jurisdiction although the district court had not clearly ruled on the immunity defense. In Musso, the district court denied summary judgment on a false arrest claim without expressly addressing the defendant's qualified immunity defense. We held that review was proper because declining it would risk the loss of whatever entitlement the defendant had not to stand trial, and we remanded the immunity question to the district court. Id. at 741-42. In Dube, the district court denied summary judgment in a one-sentence statement that we could not read as having addressed the defense on either constitutional claim, and we held the denial immediately appealable. 900 F.2d at 596. In each case the district court had denied the motion, and the case was to proceed toward trial with the immunity defense unaddressed. The order here differs in kind. It does not deny the defense and send the case toward trial. It withholds decision and invites the same motion to be made again on a fuller record. Neither Musso nor Dube, as we read them, involved an order that expressly preserved the defense for later decision, and neither holds that an immediate appeal lies from such an order. Nothing we say today limits the rule those cases apply to unqualified denials.

*D. The officials' functional argument.* The officials' strongest argument is a functional one. Qualified immunity protects against the burdens of discovery as well as trial. Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982) ("Until this threshold immunity question is resolved, discovery should not be allowed."); Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226, 231-32 (1991). They contend that if "without prejudice" is treated as controlling, a district court could reject the defense on a pure question of law, call the rejection provisional, and require officials to bear the very burdens the doctrine is meant to prevent. They add that, if the pleaded facts taken as true identify no clearly established right, further discovery is unnecessary and the defense should be sustained now.

We accept the premise of the first point and have framed the question with it in mind. A label cannot save an order from appeal if, in substance, the order finally rejects a legal entitlement to immunity. Our holding is that this order does not do so. The second point is an argument about the merits of the defense. It may be a substantial argument, and the officials may present it to the district court on renewal. But the strength of a defense cannot supply appellate jurisdiction that the character of the order withholds. If it could, the jurisdictional inquiry would be circular: whenever an immunity claim appeared strong enough, we would assume the authority to adjudicate it. Cohen's requirement of a conclusive determination operates independently of the apparent merits of the defense.

Nor are the officials without recourse. Nothing in our dismissal forecloses them from asking the district court to decide at once the purely legal question whether the right the Whalens assert was clearly established, to confine any discovery to facts bearing on that question, or to seek protection under Fed. R. Civ. P. 26(c). If the district court, on renewal or otherwise, rejects the defense as a matter of law, whether in terms or in substance, Mitchell and Cartier describe when an appeal lies. We decide nothing about any such ruling.

*E. The dissent.* Our dissenting colleague would take limited jurisdiction on the view that the defense can be established as a matter of law on the facts alleged. The limits our cases place on jurisdiction, that review lies where the defense may be established as a matter of law, restrict the cases that Mitchell reaches. They do not enlarge the class of orders that qualify as collateral orders, and they presuppose a district court decision against the defense that is conclusive. Whether the defense is decidable as a matter of law on the pleaded facts is precisely the question the district court left open. To answer it, we would have to decide a motion the district court has not decided. That the right the Whalens assert may prove unestablished does not turn this order into a conclusive rejection of the defense.

**III. Limits of the Holding**

First, the holding rests on the order before us. We do not hold that the words "without prejudice" invariably defeat immediate review. An order that in function finally rejects a purely legal immunity defense is reviewable under Mitchell whatever the district court calls it, and we decide nothing about such an order.

Second, we decide nothing about the merits. We do not decide whether the Whalens' allegations state a violation of any right, whether adoptive parents or siblings hold a constitutionally protected interest in the placement of a sibling or in sibling association, whether any such right was clearly established when the officials acted, or whether the individual defendants are entitled to immunity. Those questions are for the district court in the first instance and for any later review that is properly available.

Third, our decision does not endorse open-ended discovery, and it does not suggest that a district court may defer a purely legal immunity question until trial as a matter of course. Harlow and Siegert direct that the threshold question be resolved before discovery goes forward. Where facts are genuinely needed to determine what the officials did and knew, discovery may be limited to those facts. Where the question is purely legal, a plaintiff cannot overcome qualified immunity merely by predicting that discovery may persuade a court to recognize a right not previously established, because the inquiry is whether the right was clearly established when the officials acted. Harlow, 457 U.S. at 818. These observations explain the boundary of our holding. We do not apply them to the discovery that has taken or will take place in this case, and we do not review the sequencing of discovery as such.

Fourth, this is an appeal by the individual officials. We express no view on, and exercise no pendent appellate jurisdiction over, the claims against the counties and their departments or the state-law claims. They remain pending in the district court and are unaffected by this decision.

**IV. Disposition**

The appeal is dismissed for want of appellate jurisdiction. The order of the district court remains in effect, and the action continues in the district court, including discovery and renewed consideration of the immunity defense, consistent with this opinion.

### Dissenting opinion

Author: Judge Walker. Joined by: no other judge.

Judge Walker dissents. No other judge joins him. His grounds, as established, are these. He would exercise limited jurisdiction over the legal immunity question. Under Soares v. Connecticut, 8 F.3d 917, 920 (2d Cir. 1993), review is proper where the qualified immunity defense may be established as a matter of law on the facts viewed in the Whalens' favor, or where the district court has committed fundamental error in applying the law, a rule he draws also from Cartier and Mitchell. He would not take jurisdiction unless the defense is first found decidable as a matter of law, and he would rely on no unalleged facts and resolve no disputed facts.

On the pleaded facts, which allege no custodial relationship, he would hold that no clearly established right of adoptive parents to the placement of the sister existed. He would not dismiss on the ground that the order is a provisional deferral requiring factual development.

He would reverse in the reviewable part and remand for entry of summary judgment for the individual defendants on the federal claims resting on the asserted sibling-placement right, leaving the remainder of the case, the claims against the counties and the state-law claims to the district court. His dissent is not circuit law.

## What the decision does

- **Controlling holding (Part II of the opinion; Judge Meskill and Judge Stone).** An interlocutory denial of a qualified-immunity summary judgment motion is immediately appealable under the collateral-order doctrine only to the extent that the order conclusively determines, as a matter of law, the officials' entitlement to the defense. The rule applies to an appeal by officials from an order denying the motion before final judgment. The test is that the order must conclusively determine the disputed question, resolve an important issue completely separate from the merits, and be effectively unreviewable after final judgment (Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546 (1949); Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978)), applied to immunity through Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 526-30 (1985), Cartier v. Lussier, 955 F.2d 841, 844 (2d Cir. 1992), and Golino v. City of New Haven, 950 F.2d 864, 868 (2d Cir. 1991). The burden is on the officials as appellants to bring the order within the exception. The standard of review is de novo. Applied here, the order denied the motion without prejudice and permitted renewal after discovery; it reserved the immunity question for decision on a developed record, so it is tentative and not conclusive, and the court looks to what the order did and not to its label.
- **Qualification.** The holding is confined to this order. Orders that in function finally reject a purely legal immunity defense remain reviewable whatever they are called, and the court decides nothing about them.
- **Dicta.** Part III's observations on discovery and on the officials' recourse restate Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), and Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226, 231-32 (1991), explain the limits of the holding, and are not necessary to the judgment.
- **Status and treatment of precedent.** Published opinion. The Opinion of the Court, with the joins of Judge Meskill and Judge Stone, is precedential in this circuit on the controlling proposition stated in Part II, from its release on March 29, 1994, subject to rehearing, en banc review, and controlling Supreme Court authority. The dissent is not precedent. The decision follows and applies Cohen, Coopers & Lybrand, Mitchell, Cartier and Golino. It distinguishes Musso and Dube as concerning unqualified denials of summary judgment, and it neither overrules nor questions them. It exercises no authority to depart from any controlling decision.
- **Not decided and not precedent.** The merits of the immunity defense, the existence of any protected interest in sibling placement or association, the timeliness of the notice of appeal, pendent appellate jurisdiction over the counties and state-law claims, and whether any later ruling on renewal will be appealable. The dismissal decides only that this court lacks jurisdiction over this appeal. It carries no law-of-the-case or preclusive effect on the merits of the immunity defense, which the district court remains free to decide on renewal.
- **Effect on the parties and relief.** The appeal is dismissed. There is no remand order, because the case never left the district court. The district court's order stands, and discovery and renewed consideration of the individual defendants' immunity defense proceed there. The decision directs no entry of summary judgment, sets no scope or limit for discovery, and does not affect the claims against the counties and departments or the state-law claims.
- **Mandate status.** No mandate has issued. Under the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure as in force on the decision date, a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order or by local rule (Fed. R. App. P. 40(a)). The mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order (Fed. R. App. P. 41(a)). If a petition for rehearing is timely filed, the mandate does not issue until 7 days after entry of an order denying it, unless that time is shortened or enlarged. The date on which judgment was entered is not established in the record, and no petition or motion is known to have been filed.
