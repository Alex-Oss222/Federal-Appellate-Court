### Issue 1: Collateral-order jurisdiction and the reach of the immunity appeal

**Preservation and reach:** The officials appeal a denial without prejudice before final judgment. Appellate jurisdiction must be established independently; merits strength cannot supply it.

**Standard of review:** De novo review of collateral-order jurisdiction and the legal character of the order. Any reached immunity-law question is also legal, but factual disputes remain governed by summary-judgment rules.

**Governing test and burdens:** Cohen requires conclusive determination, separateness, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Mitchell permits an immunity appeal to the extent the order conclusively decides a legal entitlement to avoid trial.

The officials' strongest position is functional. Qualified immunity protects against trial and burdensome discovery, not merely damages. A court could defeat *Mitchell* by labeling every legal rejection “without prejudice” while requiring officials to bear the very litigation burdens immunity is meant to avoid. If the pleaded facts, taken as true, identify no clearly established right, additional discovery may be unnecessary.

The opposing jurisdictional argument is that the district court has not conclusively rejected immunity. It deferred resolution, preserved renewal before trial, and did not enter an order finally deciding the legal defense. Under *Cohen* and *Coopers & Lybrand*, provisional treatment is not necessarily an appealable collateral order. Immediate review of every scheduling decision involving an immunity motion would expand a narrow exception to finality.

The underlying merits illustrate why the officials' concern is substantial, but do not themselves confer appellate jurisdiction. *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), requires clearly established law, not the possibility that a court might establish a right in this litigation. *Golino v. City of New Haven*, 950 F.2d 864, 870 (2d Cir. 1991), likewise demands attention to what a reasonable official could understand. *Smith v. Organization of Foster Families for Equality & Reform*, 431 U.S. 816 (1977), does not establish a categorical right of adoptive parents to obtain placement of another biological sibling. *Rivera v. Marcus*, 696 F.2d 1016 (2d Cir. 1982), involved a materially different, existing family relationship. Those distinctions make immunity a serious defense, not an appellate jurisdictional shortcut.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546-47 (1949); Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978); Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 526-30 (1985). Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), supplies the clearly-established-law defense if lawfully reached.

**Other governing authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 1291; Cartier v. Lussier, 955 F.2d 841, 844 (2d Cir. 1992); Golino v. City of New Haven, 950 F.2d 864, 870 (2d Cir. 1991). Smith v. Organization of Foster Families, 431 U.S. 816 (1977), and Rivera v. Marcus, 696 F.2d 1016 (2d Cir. 1982), do not automatically establish a right to a desired adoption placement.
