## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Collateral-order jurisdiction and the reach of the immunity appeal

**Jurisdiction and reach:** The officials appeal a denial without prejudice before final judgment. Appellate jurisdiction must be established independently; merits strength cannot supply it. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Dismiss the appeal for lack of collateral-order jurisdiction.

**Governing law:** De novo review of collateral-order jurisdiction and the legal character of the order. Any reached immunity-law question is also legal, but factual disputes remain governed by summary-judgment rules. Cohen requires conclusive determination, separateness, and effective unreviewability after final judgment. Mitchell permits an immunity appeal to the extent the order conclusively decides a legal entitlement to avoid trial.

Cohen v. Beneficial Industrial Loan Corp., 337 U.S. 541, 546-47 (1949); Coopers & Lybrand v. Livesay, 437 U.S. 463, 468 (1978); Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511, 526-30 (1985). Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982), supplies the clearly-established-law defense if lawfully reached. 28 U.S.C. § 1291; Cartier v. Lussier, 955 F.2d 841, 844 (2d Cir. 1992); Golino v. City of New Haven, 950 F.2d 864, 870 (2d Cir. 1991). Smith v. Organization of Foster Families, 431 U.S. 816 (1977), and Rivera v. Marcus, 696 F.2d 1016 (2d Cir. 1982), do not automatically establish a right to a desired adoption placement.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** This provisional denial without prejudice does not conclusively determine immunity; labels are not dispositive in a materially different case where deferral functionally rejects a legal defense.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose dismissal of this appeal because the particular order did not conclusively resolve the immunity defense. He should make the holding narrow and acknowledge that labels cannot be decisive in a future case where a supposed deferral in fact finally rejects a purely legal entitlement to immunity.

The starting point is the order actually entered. It left renewal available after development of the record. The court of appeals should not convert that provisional ruling into a final rejection merely because it can identify a powerful merits argument for the officials. Otherwise the jurisdictional inquiry becomes circular: whenever an immunity claim appears strong enough, the court would assume authority to adjudicate it. *Cohen* requires conclusiveness independently of the apparent merits.

At the same time, the opinion should not endorse unlimited discovery or suggest that the district court can postpone legal immunity questions until trial as a matter of course. *Mitchell* and *Harlow* give immunity a procedural function. The district court should distinguish facts genuinely necessary to determine what the officials did and knew from a purely legal question about the right asserted. A plaintiff cannot overcome qualified immunity merely by predicting that discovery may persuade a court to recognize a previously unestablished right.

Because Stone's proposed disposition is jurisdictional, those observations should explain the boundary of the holding, not become a merits mandate granting or denying immunity. The opinion should not adjudicate the Whalens' asserted family right, reject all sibling-association claims, or dismiss municipal and state-law counts. Each would exceed the reason the court may act at this stage.
