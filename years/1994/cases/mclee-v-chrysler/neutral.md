## Record and issues

The district judge read employment-discrimination precedents as prohibiting summary judgment where intent was at issue. Chrysler argued that those decisions instead required careful application of Rule 56. After the court of appeals sought a response, the judge maintained the essential position and attempted to distinguish recent circuit authority. The available record concerns refusal to perform the Rule 56 inquiry, not a completed determination that McLee’s evidence created a genuine factual dispute. ([OpenJurist](https://openjurist.org/38/f3d/67/mclee-v-chrysler-corporation))

**Jurisdiction:** Section 1292(b) governs permission for the certified appeal; the All Writs Act, § 1651, supplies the mandamus channel in aid of appellate jurisdiction. The panel must distinguish correcting ordinary error from requiring a judge to exercise the authority entrusted to the court. **Preservation:** The categorical procedural refusal and the requested extraordinary remedy are squarely presented. **Missing material:** Full summary-judgment papers and underlying employment record; those omissions prevent deciding Chrysler’s motion itself.

### Issue 1: Mandatory consideration under Rule 56

**Review and governing law:** The legal availability of summary judgment is reviewed independently. *Dister v. Continental Group*, 859 F.2d 1108 (2d Cir. 1988), *Gallo v. Prudential Residential Services*, 22 F.3d 1219 (2d Cir. 1994), and *Woroski v. Nashua*, 31 F.3d 105 (2d Cir. 1994), require caution about intent and credibility but do not create a categorical exemption. *Poller v. Columbia Broadcasting*, 368 U.S. 464 (1962), likewise does not authorize refusal to apply the rule. ([OpenJurist](https://openjurist.org/38/f3d/67/mclee-v-chrysler-corporation))

**Positions:** The district judge emphasizes the jury’s role in resolving intent. Chrysler emphasizes that a jury resolves genuine factual disputes, not claims unsupported by sufficient evidence. Both principles can coexist.

**Paths:** Require consideration under the ordinary standard without directing a particular result. The appellate court should not infer that Chrysler wins because the judge used the wrong procedural premise.

### Issue 2: Mandamus and reassignment

**Governing law and burden:** Mandamus is extraordinary, not an alternative to ordinary appeal. *La Buy v. Howes Leather*, 352 U.S. 249 (1957), recognizes supervisory intervention where a court abdicates a required judicial function. Chrysler must establish more than disagreement with a discretionary ruling. ([OpenJurist](https://openjurist.org/38/f3d/67/mclee-v-chrysler-corporation))

**Positions and remedial choice:** Chrysler argues that the judge’s continued categorical refusal makes ordinary correction ineffective. The countervailing concern is preserving normal trial-court responsibility and avoiding extraordinary review of every summary-judgment denial.

**Lawful paths:** Deny mandamus if the judge merely applied Rule 56 incorrectly in an ordinary case. Grant narrowly if the judge refuses to conduct the inquiry at all. Reassignment requires a case-specific reason tied to reliable performance of that inquiry; it must not be punishment for rhetoric. Any reassignment should follow normal district procedures.
