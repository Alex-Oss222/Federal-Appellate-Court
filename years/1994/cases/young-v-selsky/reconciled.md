## Historical comparison

The historical panel (41 F.3d 47, decided 1994-11-21, submitted on the papers 1994-04-26) reversed the absolute-immunity dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky and remanded. Feinberg wrote the opinion and Winter joined it. Van Graafeiland dissented, giving greater weight to the adjudicative appellate function and to practical protection against repeated prisoner litigation. The majority held that Selsky's administrative appellate position did not establish the judicial independence and safeguards required for absolute immunity. It left qualified immunity, the merits, personal responsibility, and relief for further proceedings, with no automatic damages award, expungement, or revival of a different action.

Premises that survive: the same parties, record, submission on the papers, question, and pre-cutoff authorities. Premises that changed:

1. The third seat is held by a different person. Winter's historical join is not inherited, and history supplies no evidence about that seat. This premise does not alter the legal or factual record that either retained judge confronts.
2. The packet admits Walker v. Bates as binding within a cure-only scope (administrative reversal after punitive confinement began is no defense to a section 1983 claim for punishment already served). history.md was written without it and does not say whether the historical panel considered it. Walker does not address absolute immunity of a reviewing official. Selsky affirmed the five dispositions, so no reversal and no cure question arises here. Its only bearing is indirect: it lessens the weight that administrative correction can bear as a substitute for damages review.

## Wilfred Feinberg

**Reconciled disposition:** Reverse the absolute-immunity dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky; only qualified immunity is available at this stage. No view on liability, damages, or any sanction. This matches both the commitment and history.

**Ground:** The immunity classification is reviewed independently and functionally. Selsky bears the burden under Butz, Burns, and Antoine. Applying the Cleavinger factors, the state has not shown insulation from departmental pressure, adversary presentation, or precedent at the review stage; centralization and caseload bear mainly on harassment, which qualified immunity addresses. The result rests on the failure of the burden, not on a finding that the advisory-role evidence is true. Dorman, Shelly, and Richardson are distinguished; Walker is confined to the cure question.

**Remedy:** Reverse and remand. Qualified immunity, personal involvement, causation, the Wolff witness claims, any protected-interest question, and relief are left to the district court. No damages directed. Nothing in the reversal bars non-damages relief.

**Join limits:** Unchanged from the commitment. He can join a narrow burden-and-factors rationale. He cannot join (a) appellate fact-finding from testimony in other lawsuits, (b) a categorical rule that no reviewing official in any prison appeals system can have absolute immunity, (c) first-instance appellate decision of qualified immunity or the merits on this record, or (d) a reading of Walker beyond its cure scope.

**Basis for any departure from the commitment or from history:** None on disposition, ground, or remedy; the commitment and history agree. History shows him as author, which is evidence only; authorship is for assembly. History's second join came from the replaced member and is not carried over. Walker does not change his position: it reinforces a point his commitment already makes and decides nothing about absolute immunity.

## Ellsworth A. Van Graafeiland

**Reconciled disposition:** Affirm the absolute-immunity dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky (dissent from reversal). This departs from the commitment (reverse) and follows history.

**Ground:** As far as history.md reports it, Selsky's statewide review of disciplinary determinations is sufficiently adjudicative-appellate in character, and absolute immunity is warranted to protect that function from repeated damages suits by dissatisfied prisoners. How the dissent treated Selsky's burden, the advisory-role evidence, and Cleavinger is not reported and is not supplied here. No such reasoning is added.

**Remedy:** Affirmance as to the damages claims, so no remand on immunity. The component under review is the damages dismissal only. Heimbach, which he joined, leaves non-damages relief unbarred, and nothing here directs any such relief.

**Join limits:** He joins no part of a reversal of the absolute-immunity ruling, on any rationale. The commitment's barriers still apply to any opinion he would join, in whole or part: appellate fact-finding from other lawsuits' testimony, a categorical rule for all reviewing officials, decision of personal involvement or qualified immunity on this record, and extension of Walker past the cure question.

**Basis for any departure from the commitment or from history:** Departure from the commitment. No changed premise explains the difference. The replaced third seat does not change the law or record that determines his own reading of the function. Walker is cure-only. If anything it would push toward reversal, the commitment's result, so it cannot explain a move to dissent. Keeping the commitment would therefore be the default. History is nevertheless the stronger evidence here, for four specific reasons.

1. It is this judge's actual vote on the identical question and record, with premises intact.
2. The commitment's basis is generic law (Butz, Burns, Cleavinger) plus inferences from opinions he joined. The commitment itself records that no Butz or Cleavinger writing by him was found and that the search was incomplete, so little judge-specific content supports it.
3. Its one judge-specific point, Marino and the adequacy of Article 78 correction, is the factor the commitment called the state's best and then discounted. That points toward the dissent's emphasis.
4. The commitment itself named affirmance as a plausible lawful path, and the dissent's stated weights (appellate function, protection against repeated litigation) are the packet's account of Selsky's strongest position. Walker leaves them untouched. At most it could lead him to rest less on correctability as a substitute for damages.

This predicts his vote. It does not say affirmance is the better legal answer. The burden analysis in the commitment remains a serious argument for the panel.

## Agreement between the two modeled judges (by component)

- **Disposition:** They disagree. Feinberg reverses and Van Graafeiland affirms. Without the third panel member, no two-vote position exists on the judgment.
- **Sufficient ground:** They disagree on application. They share the framework: function over title, the burden on the proponent, the Cleavinger factors, Dorman, Shelly, and Richardson not controlling, and Walker limited to cure. They weigh the factors oppositely on whether this review function earns absolute immunity. No joint rationale for either disposition exists.
- **Remedy:** They disagree because remedy follows disposition (remand versus none). They coincide only on negatives: no damages directed, no decision of qualified immunity, personal involvement, or merits now, and no extension of Walker.
- **Join limits:** Compatible. Their barriers overlap on appellate fact-finding, categorical rules, deciding unresolved issues on this record, and Walker. Overlapping barriers do not produce a shared join on the judgment. A partial join by Van Graafeiland in a reversal opinion is not shown by history.md and is not assumed.

Consequence: had the commitments stood, both judges would reverse and two votes would exist without the third. After reconciliation, the third panel member's vote decides the disposition of the damages claims. That sensitivity follows from the one departure above and is not a reason for it.
