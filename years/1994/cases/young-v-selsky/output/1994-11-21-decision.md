## Decision summary

### The case and the result

Jerry Young, also known as Ramadan, was an inmate at Eastern Correctional Facility when the events at issue occurred. Between February and May 1992, five disciplinary hearings involving him were held at that facility. Young alleges that he was denied requested witnesses, including thirty-one inmates and two correctional officers, and that he received confinement in special housing and losses of privileges. Whether any witness should have been called, why any request was refused, and whether any sanction was lawful have not been established, and this decision does not decide them.

Donald Selsky is the Director of Special Housing and Inmate Disciplinary Programs of the New York Department of Correctional Services. Acting as the commissioner's designee, he reviewed on administrative appeal the serious disciplinary determinations at issue and affirmed all five dispositions. In July 1992 Young filed this action under 42 U.S.C. 1983, seeking damages, expungement of the disciplinary records, and restoration of the affected rights. A separate action against officials who conducted the hearings is not before the court.

The district court dismissed, on the ground of absolute immunity, the damages claims under section 1983 against Selsky. The papers before the panel do not include the notice of appeal or the docket entry fixing the date on which judgment was entered, and no entry date is stated. The material on which the district court relied came largely from other lawsuits. It described Selsky's centralized review role, his large caseload, and asserted insulation from institutional pressures. The record on appeal also includes evidence that Selsky advised hearing officers during disciplinary proceedings and that he works within a correctional supervisory structure whose officials have operational interests in disciplinary outcomes. Appointed counsel for Young contended that the district court had an incomplete picture of Selsky's function, and the State agreed that further materials bearing on that function might be considered.

One issue is presented: whether the function Selsky performs in reviewing New York prison disciplinary determinations entitles him to absolute immunity from damages under section 1983. The court held that, on this record, Selsky, who bears the burden as the proponent of the immunity, has not shown that his review function warrants absolute rather than qualified immunity. The dismissal on absolute-immunity grounds is reversed and the case is remanded to the district court. Qualified immunity remains available to Selsky on its own terms, and the decision does not decide qualified immunity, his personal involvement, causation, the merits of the due process allegations, or any protected liberty interest.

Jurisdiction rests on 28 U.S.C. 1291, because the dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky is a final judgment. Whether a function warrants absolute immunity is a question of law reviewed independently, and unresolved questions of conduct and injury remain for the proper factfinder. The law applied is the law in force on the date of decision.

### The principal contrary argument and the answer

The principal contrary argument, pressed by Selsky and reflected in the dissent, is that a statewide appellate reviewer who is distant from the underlying incidents, who decides a very large number of appeals, and whose decisions can be corrected in the state courts performs a function so like that of an appellate judge that he must be protected from repeated damages suits by dissatisfied prisoners. The opinion of the court answers that the immunity belongs to the function and must be established by the official who claims it; that centralization and volume speak mainly to harassment, which qualified immunity, the personal-involvement requirement and the ordinary limits on claims already address; that *Cleavinger* rejected the same reasoning for prison disciplinary decisionmakers; and that this record does not show insulation from the Department's institutional interests, adversary presentation, or a body of precedent at the review stage.

## Opinion of the court

**Opinion of the court by Judge Feinberg, joined by Judge Stone.**

Jerry Young, also known as Ramadan, who was an inmate at Eastern Correctional Facility when the events at issue occurred, appeals from a judgment of the United States District Court for the Northern District of New York (Neal P. McCurn, District Judge) that dismissed, on the ground of absolute immunity, his claims for damages under 42 U.S.C. 1983 against Donald Selsky. Selsky is the Director of Special Housing and Inmate Disciplinary Programs of the New York Department of Correctional Services and, as the commissioner's designee, reviewed on administrative appeal the five disciplinary determinations that Young challenges. The district court acted on the recommendation of Magistrate Judge Gustave J. DiBianco. The question is whether the function Selsky performs in reviewing serious prison disciplinary determinations is one for which the law grants absolute, rather than qualified, immunity from damages. We hold that Selsky, on whom the burden of establishing the immunity rests, has not shown on this record that it is. We therefore reverse and remand.

### I. Background

Between February and May 1992, five disciplinary hearings involving Young were held at Eastern Correctional Facility. Young alleges that at those hearings he was denied witnesses he had requested, including thirty-one inmates and two correctional officers, and that he received confinement in special housing and losses of privileges. We have no findings on whether any requested witness should have been called, on why any request was refused, or on whether any sanction was lawful, and nothing in this opinion decides those matters. Selsky affirmed all five dispositions. In July 1992 Young filed this action under section 1983, seeking damages, expungement of the disciplinary records, and restoration of the rights he lost. A separate action against officials involved in the hearings is not before us and plays no part in our decision.

The district court dismissed the damages claims against Selsky on the ground that his review function is protected by absolute immunity. The materials on which that ruling rested came largely from other lawsuits. They describe Selsky's centralized, statewide role in reviewing disciplinary determinations, the large number of determinations he reviews, and what was asserted to be his insulation from institutional pressures. The record on appeal contains more. It includes evidence that Selsky advised hearing officers during disciplinary proceedings and that he works within a correctional supervisory structure whose officials have operational interests in how disciplinary proceedings come out. Counsel appointed to represent Young on appeal contended that the district court had been given an incomplete picture of Selsky's function, and the State agreed that the court might consider further materials bearing on it. The appeal was submitted on the papers on April 26, 1994, without oral argument, and appointed counsel later submitted supplemental materials.

The State's agreement has a limit. It allows us to consider the materials for what they show about the nature of Selsky's function. It does not make testimony in unrelated lawsuits indisputable proof of every fact asserted in it, and an appellate court does not find facts. We do not find how often, or in what manner, Selsky advised hearing officers. As will appear, our conclusion does not depend on such a finding.

### II. Jurisdiction and Standard of Review

The dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky is a final judgment, and we have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1291. Whether the function an official performs warrants absolute immunity is a question of law, and our review is independent. We do not resolve any question of conduct or injury that must be decided by a factfinder. The official who asserts absolute immunity bears the burden of showing that it is justified for the function in question. *Burns v. Reed*, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991). As the Supreme Court has put it, "[t]he proponent of a claim to absolute immunity bears the burden of establishing the justification for such immunity." *Antoine v. Byers & Anderson, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 429 (1993).

### III. Discussion

#### A. The governing principles

Section 1983 contains no express immunity. The Supreme Court has treated qualified immunity as the ordinary protection of officials who exercise discretion, and it has reserved absolute immunity for functions that warrant it. Absolute immunity attaches to a function, not to an office or a title. The immunity of judges, *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872); *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), protects the exercise of judicial judgment under conditions that make damages actions an unnecessary and harmful way of controlling error. In *Butz v. Economou*, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978), the Court extended comparable protection to officials who perform adjudicative functions within federal agencies, because their proceedings closely resemble judicial proceedings and are surrounded by comparable safeguards. *Butz* did not make a judge of every official who reviews another's decision, and the Court has cautioned against extending absolute immunity further than its justification requires. *Burns*, 500 U.S. at 486-87.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985), is the decision closest to this one. It concerned the members of a prison disciplinary committee. The Court, drawing on *Butz*, looked to the considerations characteristic of the judicial process: the need to assure that the decisionmaker can perform his functions without harassment or intimidation; the presence of safeguards that reduce the need for private damages actions as a means of controlling unconstitutional conduct; insulation from political influence; the importance of precedent; the adversary nature of the process; and the correctability of error on appeal. Concluding that the committee members lacked the independence and the procedural safeguards that characterize courts, the Court held that they were entitled to qualified immunity only. *Cleavinger* teaches that neither the label attached to a prison disciplinary decision nor its importance to institutional order decides the question. What decides it is whether the person claiming the immunity has shown that he performs a function that is judicial in the respects the immunity exists to protect.

#### B. Application to Selsky's review function

Selsky's position has force. He reviews, for the whole state system, determinations made at many institutions. He is not ordinarily involved in the incidents that give rise to them. He decides a very large number of appeals, and an official so situated is exposed to suit by those who do not prevail. Administrative review and review in the state courts under Article 78 are available to correct error. And absolute immunity cannot depend on whether the official decided correctly. Our conclusion does not suggest that any of the five dispositions was wrong. The question is whether, on this record, Selsky has carried the burden of showing that his function has the characteristics that justify absolute immunity. We conclude that he has not.

First, insulation from institutional pressure. Independence is the premise on which the immunity of judges and agency adjudicators rests: a decisionmaker who need not fear the consequences of an unwelcome ruling may decide without regard to them. Selsky is an official of the Department that operates the facilities in which the determinations he reviews are made. He works within a correctional supervisory structure, and the officials of that structure have operational interests in how disciplinary proceedings come out. The record includes evidence that he advises hearing officers in the course of disciplinary proceedings. Centralizing review in an office removed from any single facility relieves some of the local pressures that concerned the Court in *Cleavinger*, but it does not show that the reviewer is insulated from the Department's own institutional interests, which a statewide office shares with the facilities it oversees. An official who advises the tribunals whose decisions he later reviews, to whatever extent he does so, is not shown to stand apart from the hearing process in the way an appellate court stands apart from the trial court. We do not find that Selsky gave such advice in any of Young's five cases, and we do not decide the extent of any advisory role. It is enough that the evidence is directly relevant to the comparison with a court, and that the party who bears the burden of establishing insulation has not established it.

Second, adversary presentation, precedent, and the other safeguards of adjudication. The materials on which Selsky relies describe the centralization of review, the volume of determinations, and the asserted insulation of his office. The State has not shown that review proceeds through an adversary presentation in which opposing positions are put to a neutral decisionmaker, that Selsky's determinations are issued in a form and with an effect that makes them precedent constraining later reviewers, or that the process contains safeguards of the kind that reduce the need for private damages actions as a means of controlling unconstitutional conduct. Those are the features that make the work of a court something other than the work of an administrator, and showing them is the proponent's burden.

Third, correctability. That error in a disciplinary determination may be corrected by administrative review and by proceedings in the state courts is a consideration to be weighed, and we weigh it. It is not sufficient by itself. If the availability of review were enough, absolute immunity would become the rule for every official whose decisions can be reviewed, which is the opposite of the caution the Supreme Court has urged. *Burns*, 500 U.S. at 486-87. Nor does later correction undo a deprivation already suffered. In *Walker v. Bates*, No. 93-2107 (2d Cir. Apr. 29, 1994), Part II, this court held that administrative reversal of a disciplinary determination after punitive confinement has begun is not, as a matter of law, a defense to a section 1983 claim for punishment already served. *Walker* did not concern absolute immunity, no reversal occurred here, and we do not extend its holding beyond its terms. We note it only to explain why the possibility of later correction cannot bear the weight Selsky would place on it as a substitute for ordinary damages review.

Fourth, harassment. The concern that an official who decides many appeals will be sued by many disappointed inmates is genuine, and the State properly presses it. It is the consideration to which Selsky's centralized role and large caseload mainly relate. But it cannot alone establish absolute immunity. Qualified immunity protects an official who could reasonably have believed his conduct lawful and permits the early disposal of insubstantial claims. A plaintiff must also show the defendant's personal involvement in the alleged violation and causation, and the ordinary rules of pleading and evidence, together with the court's authority to dismiss meritless claims, remain in force. The frequency with which unsuccessful appellants may sue cannot suffice, for the same could be said of the disciplinary committee members in *Cleavinger*, whom the Court nonetheless held to qualified immunity. We add what is not our reasoning. It is not that inmates must have someone to sue, and it is not that the size of Selsky's caseload shows indifference to the merits of the appeals he decides.

The authorities on which Selsky relies do not alter this analysis. *Dorman v. Higgins*, 821 F.2d 133, 137 (2d Cir. 1987), granted absolute immunity to a probation officer for preparing a presentence report because in doing so the officer acts as an arm of the court, in work integral to the judicial process. Selsky is an official of a correctional department, and his office is not part of any court. *Shelly v. Johnson*, 849 F.2d 228 (6th Cir. 1988), does not bind us and concerns a materially different state arrangement for adjudicating prison discipline; Selsky has not shown that the features that mattered there are present in New York's system. *Richardson v. Selsky*, 5 F.3d 616 (2d Cir. 1993), resolved the claims against Selsky and others on the ground of qualified immunity and did not decide whether his review function is absolutely immune. It cannot be read to establish a categorical immunity for that function.

#### C. Holding and its limits

We hold that, on the record before us, Selsky has not carried his burden of showing that his review of prison disciplinary determinations has the independence from institutional pressure and the safeguards that justify absolute immunity. Administrative review by Selsky does not acquire absolute judicial immunity merely because it is called an appeal. Qualified immunity remains available to him on its own terms. The dismissal of the damages claims on the ground of absolute immunity must therefore be reversed.

The holding is narrow. It rests on the failure of the proponent's showing and not on any finding of fact that this court could not properly make on appeal. It is confined to the structure of review shown on this record and states no rule about facts not presented. It does not hold that no official who reviews prison discipline, in New York or elsewhere, can have absolute immunity. It does not hold that Selsky's affirmance of a disciplinary determination was itself a violation. Whether Young can establish a protected liberty interest under the law then governing, a violation of the process actually owed, Selsky's personal involvement, and causation are questions for the district court. In deciding them the district court will apply *Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), with the limits on the right to call witnesses that *Wolff* recognizes where institutional safety or correctional goals require them. An official may affirm a disciplinary determination after a reasonable assessment that legitimate security grounds justified the refusal of witnesses. Whether Selsky could reasonably have understood the challenged determinations to be lawful, under the law as it stood when he acted, is a question of qualified immunity that we do not decide on this record, for it would require us to resolve contested questions about what he did and why.

#### D. Remand

On remand the district court should permit development of the record concerning the five disciplinary appeals, the reasons for the rulings on requested witnesses, Selsky's personal involvement, qualified immunity, causation, and the relief Young seeks. We direct no award of damages in any amount and no expungement of any disciplinary record. Young is entitled to a forum in which to try to prove his claim, not to an award from this court. The district court's dismissal addressed the claims for damages. Our reversal does not bar any claim to relief other than damages against Selsky, but neither do we decide that such relief is available. An immunity from damages does not by itself resolve whether other relief may be had, and relief directed to the future requires an appropriate defendant and an injury that is live and capable of remedy. Those questions are for the district court.

The judgment of the district court dismissing the damages claims against Selsky on the ground of absolute immunity is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

## Separate opinions

### Dissent of Judge Van Graafeiland (joined by no other judge)

Judge Van Graafeiland dissents and would affirm the dismissal of the damages claims against Selsky. In his view, Selsky's statewide review of disciplinary determinations is sufficiently adjudicative and appellate in character that absolute immunity is warranted to protect that function from repeated damages suits by prisoners dissatisfied with the outcome of their appeals. He joins no part of the opinion of the court. He would order no remand on immunity.

## What the decision does

- **Controlling holding.** Absolute immunity under section 1983 turns on the function performed, not the title of the office or the label "appeal." The official who claims it bears the burden of showing that the particular function has the independence from institutional pressure and the procedural safeguards that characterize adjudication. The test is the set of considerations identified in *Butz* and applied in *Cleavinger*: the need to protect the decisionmaker from harassment or intimidation; safeguards that reduce the need for private damages actions; insulation from political or institutional influence; the importance of precedent; the adversary nature of the process; and the correctability of error on appeal. The burden is on the proponent of the immunity. The authorities for the rule are *Bradley v. Fisher*, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872); *Pierson v. Ray*, 386 U.S. 547 (1967); *Butz v. Economou*, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978); *Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985); *Burns v. Reed*, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991); and *Antoine v. Byers & Anderson, Inc.*, 508 U.S. 429 (1993). On the record presented, Selsky has not carried that burden as to his review of prison disciplinary determinations. Administrative review by Selsky does not acquire absolute judicial immunity merely because it is called an appeal; qualified immunity remains available to him on its own terms. In application, the record does not show insulation from the Department's institutional interests, adversary presentation, or a body of precedent at the review stage; the centralization and volume of his review bear mainly on harassment, which qualified immunity addresses; and the availability of later correction does not carry the immunity.
- **Limits of the holding.** The holding rests on failure of the proponent's showing, not on any finding that the advisory-role evidence is true. It is confined to the structure of review shown on this record and states no rule about facts not presented. It is not a rule that no official reviewing prison discipline in any system can have absolute immunity. It does not decide qualified immunity or the merits on this record. It does not extend *Walker v. Bates* beyond its own question.
- **Binding and non-binding parts.** The opinion is published and is precedential as to the holding stated in the opinion of the court, on the joins of Judge Feinberg and Judge Stone. The dissent has no precedential force and states Judge Van Graafeiland's position only. The reversal and remand of this judgment, and the direction that no damages or expungement is awarded now, are party-specific. The observations about the *Wolff* limits on witnesses, about relief other than damages, and about the character of *Walker* describe matters left to the district court and are not holdings. On remand, the ruling that Selsky has not shown absolute immunity on the record presented governs the further proceedings in this case, and the opinion states no rule about facts not presented.
- **Treatment of prior authority.** The opinion applies, as binding Supreme Court authority, *Butz*, *Cleavinger*, *Burns* and *Antoine*, and follows *Bradley* and *Pierson* on the judicial function. It distinguishes *Dorman v. Higgins* (a probation officer acting as an arm of the court), treats *Shelly v. Johnson* as persuasive authority from another circuit that concerns a materially different arrangement, and reads *Richardson v. Selsky* as a qualified-immunity decision that did not decide absolute immunity. It refers to *Walker v. Bates*, Part II, only for its own holding on the cure question and does not extend it. No earlier circuit holding is overruled or displaced.
- **What the circuit's law now is.** The circuit now holds that the official who reviews New York prison disciplinary determinations bears the burden of showing a function with the independence and safeguards of adjudication, and that Selsky's statewide review has not been shown on this record to carry absolute immunity.
- **Effect on the parties.** The judgment dismissing the damages claims against Selsky on the ground of absolute immunity is reversed. Young may go forward in the district court on those claims. No damages, expungement or restoration is directed. A claim to relief other than damages is neither barred nor sustained by this decision. The opinion makes no direction as to costs.
- **Matters left open.** The opinion of the court does not decide: whether Selsky is entitled to qualified immunity; whether he was personally involved in, or caused, any violation; whether any sanction implicated a protected liberty interest under the law then governing; whether the refusal of any requested witness violated the process owed under *Wolff*; whether Young may recover damages in any amount; whether and against whom any relief other than damages is available; the extent and character of any advisory role Selsky played with hearing officers; any question about the structure of review on facts not presented on this record; and whether any official who reviews prison discipline in another system has absolute immunity.
- **Mandate and remand.** The case is remanded to the United States District Court for the Northern District of New York for further proceedings consistent with the opinion of the court. The scope of remand is the development and decision of the five disciplinary appeals, the reasons for the rulings on requested witnesses, Selsky's personal involvement, qualified immunity, causation, any protected-interest question, and the relief Young seeks. The mandate has not issued, and the date of the opinion is not the date of the mandate. Under Fed. R. App. P. 40(a), a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment, the United States not being a party, unless that time is shortened or enlarged by order. Under Fed. R. App. P. 41(a), the mandate issues only after the time for seeking rehearing has expired, and a timely petition for rehearing stays it until the petition is disposed of. No filing after the decision is recorded.
