## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Functional absolute immunity for administrative review of prison discipline

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Young appeals dismissal on absolute immunity. The parties supplied or accepted functional materials describing Selsky’s role; those materials do not establish the merits of all five challenged rulings. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Propose reversal of the absolute-immunity dismissal and remand.

**Governing law:** Independent review of the legal immunity classification. Unresolved conduct and injury questions remain for the proper factfinder. The official bears the burden of showing that the particular function warrants absolute rather than qualified immunity. Relevant features include protection from harassment, independence, procedural safeguards, precedent, adversarial process, and correction on appeal. Title or an appellate label is not dispositive.

Bradley v. Fisher, 80 U.S. (13 Wall.) 335 (1872), and Pierson v. Ray, 386 U.S. 547 (1967), concern judicial functions. Butz v. Economou, 438 U.S. 478, 512-14 (1978), supplies the administrative functional comparison. Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193, 202-06 (1985), denies absolute immunity to the prison disciplinary body at issue. Burns v. Reed, 500 U.S. 478, 486-87 (1991), places the burden on the claimant to immunity. 42 U.S.C. § 1983; Dorman v. Higgins, 821 F.2d 133, 137 (2d Cir. 1987), concerning a probation officer’s court-integrated function, distinguishable; Richardson v. Selsky, 5 F.3d 616 (2d Cir. 1993), does not establish categorical absolute immunity; Shelly v. Johnson, 849 F.2d 228 (6th Cir. 1988), persuasive only. Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), governs the distinct process claim with its limits.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding functional-immunity law to an unresolved institutional role.

**Proposed holding:** On the demonstrated institutional structure, administrative review by Selsky does not acquire absolute judicial immunity merely because it is called an appeal; qualified immunity remains available on its own terms.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose reversal of the absolute-immunity dismissal and remand for the remaining questions. The office's name and position on an organizational chart cannot substitute for the functional comparison required by *Butz* and *Cleavinger*. Selsky's participation in advising hearing officers, his relation to correctional superiors, and the limited adversarial safeguards are directly relevant to that comparison.

The rationale should not be that inmates need someone to sue, or that a large caseload demonstrates indifference. Neither proposition establishes the absence of immunity. The narrower legal point is that absolute immunity requires the official to demonstrate a judicially comparable function with the relevant independence and safeguards. Central review of prison discipline, while important and adjudicative in a general sense, has not been shown here to carry the combination that justifies complete protection from damages.

Stone should confront the harassment concern rather than dismiss it. An official deciding thousands of appeals faces genuine litigation burdens. But qualified immunity, requirements of personal responsibility, pleading and evidentiary rules, and adjudication of meritless claims already perform protective work. The frequency of disappointed appellants cannot alone establish absolute immunity, because that reasoning would apply equally to the disciplinary bodies addressed in *Cleavinger*.

The absence of absolute immunity does not make affirmance of a disciplinary ruling automatically unconstitutional. Young must establish a protected interest, a violation of the process actually owed, Selsky's actionable involvement, causation, and an available remedy. An official may affirm a ruling after a reasonable assessment of legitimate security grounds for denying witnesses. Whether Selsky could reasonably have understood the challenged decisions as lawful must be addressed under the qualified-immunity law applicable when he acted. *Weg v. Macchiarola*, 995 F.2d 15, 18 (2d Cir. 1993), supports leaving an unresolved immunity question for appropriate further proceedings rather than deciding contested predicates without a record.

The remand should permit development of the particular five appeals and the reasons for the witness rulings. It should also distinguish damages from requests for prospective relief: a damages immunity does not automatically resolve every form of relief, and prospective relief still requires an appropriate defendant and a live, remediable injury. Young receives a forum to prove a claim, not an appellate award of $200 per day or a finding that all challenged disciplinary records must be erased.

**Independent or alternative considerations:** No additional rule is proposed beyond the interpretation and application stated here.

**Preferred scope:** On the demonstrated institutional structure, administrative review by Selsky does not acquire absolute judicial immunity merely because it is called an appeal; qualified immunity remains available on its own terms. The lawful outer boundary is the issue and remedial limit stated here, not a broader rule about unpresented facts.

Case-level proposed scope: the departmental appellate function shown here lacks the judicial independence and safeguards required for absolute immunity; qualified immunity and all remaining claim elements remain open on remand.

**Fallback position:** Proposed: vacate dismissal because this record does not establish the safeguards and independence required for absolute immunity, reserving any materially different institutional showing.

Case-level nonjoin limits, also proposed: absolute immunity from the appellate label alone; liability from the bare act of affirmance; disregarding legitimate prison security restrictions on witnesses; treating administrative reversal as necessarily compensating confinement already served; or deciding qualified immunity through contested appellate factfinding.

**Remedy and remand:** Allow development of the five disciplinary appeals, witness rulings, personal involvement, qualified immunity, causation, and requested relief. Do not award daily damages or automatic expungement.

**Reserved questions:** No merits ruling that every witness denial was unlawful; no general denial of immunity to all administrative appellate officers.
