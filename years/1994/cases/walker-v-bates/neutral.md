# Walker v. Bates

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-2107. **Decision date:** April 29, 1994. **Citation:** 23 F.3d 652. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Judge David G. Larimer. **Historical argument:** December 15, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/23/652/482392/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Southport inmate Robert Walker was charged in December 1990 with possessing a metal rod and excess bedding. He maintained that the property had been left by a prior occupant of the cell. At a Tier III disciplinary hearing, hearing officer Bates refused Walker's requests to call three correction officers as witnesses and imposed 120 days in the special housing unit. Punishment began immediately.

Donald Selsky reversed the disciplinary decision in February 1991 and authorized a prompt rehearing. No rehearing occurred. Walker nevertheless remained in special housing until February 22. He then sought damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The district court dismissed the claim against Bates on the theory that administrative reversal cured the earlier procedural defect. Other named defendants had been dismissed and are not automatically restored by resolving this appeal. *Walker*, 23 F.3d at 653-55.

The published account contains an internally inconsistent year for one district-court order relative to the magistrate's recommendation. The substantive sequence is clear, but this brief does not silently invent a corrected docket date.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The federal civil-rights action falls within federal-question jurisdiction, and dismissal with prejudice is a final decision under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. Review of the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is de novo under the pleading law existing in 1994, including *Conley v. Gibson*, 355 U.S. 41 (1957). The court takes the properly pleaded account favorably to Walker; it does not find that every alleged deprivation occurred or that damages are established.

The question is whether administrative reversal after punishment has begun defeats the constitutional claim as a matter of law. Whether the witnesses could lawfully have been excluded, whether Bates is qualifiedly immune, and what injury he caused are separate questions that the asserted administrative-cure rule does not resolve.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), recognizes a qualified right to call witnesses at prison disciplinary hearings, subject to institutional safety and other legitimate limits. *Sher v. Coughlin*, 739 F.2d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 1984), and *McCann v. Coughlin*, 698 F.2d 112, 121-22 (2d Cir. 1983), supply the relevant pre-1995 framework for protected interests in punitive confinement. Later changes to prison-liberty doctrine cannot be used here.

Bates's strongest argument treats the hearing and administrative appeal as one integrated process. A functioning correction procedure identified and removed the error, and imposing damages despite correction may discourage prompt administrative review. *Young v. Hoffman*, 970 F.2d 1154, 1155-56 (2d Cir. 1992), supports the absence of a completed deprivation when administrative reversal occurs before the prisoner serves the disciplinary sentence.

That temporal qualification matters. Walker alleges that punishment began before review and continued for a substantial period. *Patterson v. Coughlin*, 761 F.2d 886, 893 (2d Cir. 1985), rejects the proposition that later correction necessarily erases a completed deprivation. *Russell v. Scully*, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 1994), must be read for the immunity and detention questions it actually addressed, not as a universal administrative-cure rule.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193 (1985), establishes that prison disciplinary adjudicators receive qualified, not absolute, immunity. Its procedural facts are relevant by analogy, but its holding does not itself conclusively decide every administrative-reversal question. *Ponte v. Real*, 471 U.S. 491 (1985), permits officials to justify witness exclusions in subsequent litigation; a federal requirement of contemporaneous written reasons should not be invented. Finally, *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), distinguishes the denial of process from proof of compensable injury.
