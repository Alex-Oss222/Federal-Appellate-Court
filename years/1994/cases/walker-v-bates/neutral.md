## Record and issues

**Material record:** Southport inmate Robert Walker was charged in December 1990 with possessing a metal rod and excess bedding. He maintained that the property had been left by a prior occupant of the cell. At a Tier III disciplinary hearing, hearing officer Bates refused Walker's requests to call three correction officers as witnesses and imposed 120 days in the special housing unit. Punishment began immediately.

Donald Selsky reversed the disciplinary decision in February 1991 and authorized a prompt rehearing. No rehearing occurred. Walker nevertheless remained in special housing until February 22. He then sought damages under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The district court dismissed the claim against Bates on the theory that administrative reversal cured the earlier procedural defect. Other named defendants had been dismissed and are not automatically restored by resolving this appeal. *Walker*, 23 F.3d at 653-55.

### Issue 1: Administrative reversal after punishment and the surviving due-process claim

**Preservation and reach:** The dismissal rests on administrative cure, not a factual determination that the witnesses could properly be excluded. Other defendants’ prior dismissals are outside this appeal’s requested restoration.

**Standard of review:** De novo review of Rule 12(b)(6), taking the properly pleaded facts and inferences favorably to Walker under the 1994 pleading standard.

**Governing test and burdens:** A protected liberty interest and denial of constitutionally required process are necessary. The qualified witness right permits legitimate institutional restrictions. Reversal before punishment may prevent a deprivation; reversal after punishment can mitigate without necessarily erasing one. Liability, qualified immunity, causation, and damages remain distinct.

*Wolff v. McDonnell*, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), recognizes a qualified right to call witnesses at prison disciplinary hearings, subject to institutional safety and other legitimate limits. *Sher v. Coughlin*, 739 F.2d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 1984), and *McCann v. Coughlin*, 698 F.2d 112, 121-22 (2d Cir. 1983), supply the applicable 1994 framework for protected interests in punitive confinement. Later changes to prison-liberty doctrine cannot be used here.

Bates's strongest argument treats the hearing and administrative appeal as one integrated process. A functioning correction procedure identified and removed the error, and imposing damages despite correction may discourage prompt administrative review. *Young v. Hoffman*, 970 F.2d 1154, 1155-56 (2d Cir. 1992), supports the absence of a completed deprivation when administrative reversal occurs before the prisoner serves the disciplinary sentence.

That temporal qualification matters. Walker alleges that punishment began before review and continued for a substantial period. *Patterson v. Coughlin*, 761 F.2d 886, 893 (2d Cir. 1985), rejects the proposition that later correction necessarily erases a completed deprivation. *Russell v. Scully*, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 1994), must be read for the immunity and detention questions it actually addressed, not as a universal administrative-cure rule.

*Cleavinger v. Saxner*, 474 U.S. 193 (1985), establishes that prison disciplinary adjudicators receive qualified, not absolute, immunity. Its procedural facts are relevant by analogy, but its holding does not itself conclusively decide every administrative-reversal question. *Ponte v. Real*, 471 U.S. 491 (1985), permits officials to justify witness exclusions in subsequent litigation; a federal requirement of contemporaneous written reasons should not be invented. Finally, *Carey v. Piphus*, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), distinguishes the denial of process from proof of compensable injury.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974), qualified witness right; Ponte v. Real, 471 U.S. 491 (1985), permits later explanation of exclusions; Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193 (1985), qualified rather than absolute immunity for prison hearing officials; Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), separates a process violation from compensatory proof; Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41 (1957), supplies the then-existing pleading framework.

**Other governing authority:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983; Patterson v. Coughlin, 761 F.2d 886, 893 (2d Cir. 1985); Young v. Hoffman, 970 F.2d 1154, 1155-56 (2d Cir. 1992); Russell v. Scully, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 1994); Sher and McCann on the period’s liberty-interest framework.
