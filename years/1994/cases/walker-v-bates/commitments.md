# Commitments: Walker v. Bates (No. 93-2107, 2d Cir.)

**Stage:** neutral modeling. **Legal cutoff:** 1994-04-29. **Inputs:** neutral.md, ENGINE.md, and the public sources cited below.

Shared frame (both modeled judges). Review is de novo of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, taking the pleaded facts favorably to Walker under Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 45-46 (1957) (the "no set of facts" test, as quoted in Gagliardi below). Jurisdiction rests on 28 U.S.C. § 1291 (final dismissal of the claim against Bates), not on any collateral-order theory. The issue decided below is only the categorical administrative-cure rule. Qualified immunity, the justification for excluding the witnesses, causation, and damages were not decided below.

## Wilfred Feinberg (Senior Circuit Judge)

**Disposition:** Reverse the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of the due process damages claim against Bates to the extent it rests on administrative cure, and remand. Do not restore the previously dismissed defendants. Do not decide immunity, justification for the exclusions, causation, or damages.

**Ground:** An administrative reversal that comes after punitive segregation has begun does not, as a matter of law, erase a deprivation already suffered. The complaint alleges a Tier III hearing, refusal of three requested witnesses, and 120 days in special housing imposed immediately and served before Selsky's February 1991 reversal, with further detention to February 22 and no rehearing. McCann v. Coughlin, 698 F.2d 112 (2d Cir. 1983), holds that an inmate who is or may be sentenced to special housing has the Due Process Clause's protections, including the Wolff witness right (Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539, 566 (1974), subject to institutional safety limits). Young v. Hoffman, 970 F.2d 1154 (2d Cir. 1992) (per curiam), treats reversal as part of due process and as a cure, but on a record where the inmate "was never penalized"; it did not decide whether the hearing itself violated due process. Patterson v. Coughlin, 761 F.2d 886 (2d Cir. 1985) (Timbers, J.), says that once a constitutional claim accrues "nothing that the state does subsequently can cut off the § 1983 claim." Russell v. Scully, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir.) (Winter, J.), as modified on rehearing (decided January 3, 1994), repeats that an appeal can cure hearing defects but allows damages for punitive loss of privileges during the appeal and limits recovery for confinement that would have occurred anyway. Read together, cure and mitigation bear on liability and damages for time not yet served, not on a categorical bar for time served. A panel reconciles Young and Russell this way and does not need to overrule either. Whalen v. County of Fulton (1994-03-29), Part II, is limited to appellate jurisdiction over qualified-immunity denials made without prejudice. It does not reach this appeal's § 1291 basis or the merits; its premise that a reserved or undecided immunity defense is not a conclusive ruling is consistent with leaving immunity to the district court.

**Remedy:** Reverse and remand for the district court to address, on a developed record: Bates's reasons for refusing the witnesses (Ponte v. Real, 471 U.S. 491 (1985), allows officials to justify exclusions later, including in court); qualified immunity (Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193 (1985), qualified immunity only; Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800 (1982)); causation and whether the same result would have followed with the witnesses (Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), nominal damages for a process denial, no compensatory damages for a deprivation that would have occurred anyway); Bates's personal responsibility for retention after the reversal; and damages. No instruction on any of these outcomes.

**Sources:** No Feinberg-authored opinion directly on prison discipline, administrative cure, or Rule 12(b)(6) practice was located within the permitted research; the record of his own law is therefore thin and this position rests on circuit law and the statutes. Located and read: Sostre v. McGinnis, 442 F.2d 178 (2d Cir. 1971) (en banc) (Feinberg, J., opinion dissenting in part and concurring, on the Eighth Amendment limits of prolonged punitive segregation; it does not address hearing procedure); Warren v. Dwyer, 906 F.2d 70 (2d Cir. 1990) (panel Kaufman, Feinberg, Winter; Kaufman writing, Winter dissenting in part; Feinberg joined the majority, which said the better rule is for the court to decide qualified immunity as a matter of law). Neither is weighed heavily. Authorities relied on are those named above. Pinpoints for McCann, Sher, Patterson, and Young are the packet's; the source texts I read carry no page markers, so I did not verify them.

**Join limits:** Would join a narrow reversal on the cure question. Would not join an opinion that overrules, disparages, or declines to follow Young or Russell, or one that decides immunity, justification for the exclusions, or causation on this record. Would not join a requirement that officials give contemporaneous written reasons (Ponte). Would not join a holding that Bates is liable for post-reversal detention.

## Roger J. Miner (Active Circuit Judge)

**Disposition:** Reverse the dismissal of the claim against Bates insofar as it rests on administrative cure and remand for further proceedings. Express no view on qualified immunity. Do not restore other defendants.

**Ground:** Same result by the same frame. His own opinions apply de novo review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal with Conley's "no set of facts" language and reverse when the pleaded chronology supports the inference the claim needs (Gagliardi). He reverses judgments that rest on an undeveloped record (Salahuddin) and in Gittens v. LeFevre, 891 F.2d 38 (2d Cir. 1989), found a liberty interest in New York's mandatory confinement rules under Hewitt v. Helms, 459 U.S. 460 (1983), and a process violation, while holding the officials immune because no case had marked the line. That shows he separates the existence of a violation from immunity and damages, which is the separation the cure ruling collapses. Walker's allegations fit that pattern: special housing began at once and was served before reversal, so Young's premise (no penalty served) is absent, and Patterson (1985) and Russell (as modified) show later correction does not erase accrued liability. Whalen has no bearing on the merits and confirms that an undecided immunity defense stays with the district court.

**Remedy:** Reverse and remand on the same open items as Judge Feinberg: Ponte justification, qualified immunity (in Salahuddin he declined to opine on an immunity defense not yet asserted below), causation under Carey and McCann, post-reversal responsibility, and damages. Reversal does not award damages.

**Sources:** Gagliardi v. Village of Pawling, 18 F.3d 188 (2d Cir. 1994) (Miner, J.) (de novo Rule 12(b)(6) review; dismissal of due process claim affirmed for want of a protected interest, retaliation claim reinstated on pleaded chronology). Salahuddin v. Coughlin, 993 F.2d 306 (2d Cir. 1993) (Miner, J.) (reversing summary judgment on an undeveloped record; "We express no opinion on the qualified immunity defense" not yet asserted below). Gittens v. LeFevre, 891 F.2d 38 (2d Cir. 1989) (Miner, J.). Freeman v. Rideout, 808 F.2d 949 (2d Cir. 1986) (Re, C.J., Ct. Int'l Trade, by designation, writing; Miner joined; excluded witness permissible where reason is "logically related" to institutional safety or correctional goals; joined, not authored). Authorities as in the Feinberg section; Sher v. Coughlin, 739 F.2d 77 (2d Cir. 1984) (Newman, J.), and Williams v. Smith, 781 F.2d 319 (2d Cir. 1986) (Mansfield, J.; the witness right was clearly established by 1983), noted as context for the later immunity question only.

**Join limits:** Would not join a rationale that decides immunity or that the right was clearly established, since the district court did not pass on it. Would not join a holding that the witness exclusion violated Wolff as a matter of law, because Bates's reasons are absent and Ponte lets him supply them. Would not join an opinion treating Young as wrongly decided; a panel cannot do so, and Young is distinguishable on its own terms. Would not join a requirement of contemporaneous written reasons, or language that makes the administrative reversal irrelevant to damages; it may mitigate. Routine agreement with a narrow cure holding is otherwise expected.

## Open points and source gaps

- CourtListener's search API returned HTTP 429 on both attempts and a different endpoint returned 401, so no keyword search of either judge's opinions could be run. Authorship was found by fetching candidate citations from static.case.law; the survey is not exhaustive, and the absence of a Feinberg-authored on-point opinion is a limit of this research, not a finding that none exists.
- The record does not give the hearing date, the date of Selsky's reversal, or days served before and after it. The split between pre-reversal punishment and the post-reversal stay to February 22 is therefore unquantified.
- Patterson (1985): the packet says page 893 rejects the view that later correction necessarily erases a completed deprivation. What I verified is the passage in the context of an Article 78 proceeding and expungement; its application to a reversal during the sentence is an inference from that language.
- Russell v. Scully: the reporter citation (15 F.3d 219) and the packet's 1994 year sit with a June 1993 opinion modified on rehearing on January 3, 1994; both dates precede the cutoff. Its administrative-cure statement was made alongside the rehearing ruling allowing damages for loss of privileges, which I read as limiting it.
- Pinpoints for Wolff (566), Ponte (about 497-99), Carey (about 260-67), Hewitt, and Harlow rest on cross-references in Cornell's texts, not star paging. No citator check was run.
- Appellate-filing timeliness remains unverified, as the packet states. The Whalen extract was the only text of that decision available.
