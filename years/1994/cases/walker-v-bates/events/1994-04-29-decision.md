## Public decision

### Case and court

**Caption:** Robert Walker, Plaintiff-Appellant, v. L. Bates, Hearing Officer, Defendant-Appellee.

**Docket:** No. 93-2107.

**Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit, on appeal from the United States District Court for the Western District of New York (David G. Larimer, District Judge).

**Argument and submission:** Oral argument was held on December 15, 1993. The appeal was resubmitted for decision on the papers on April 29, 1994. Judge Stone-Khoury did not attend the oral argument and decides the appeal on the papers.

**Decision date:** April 29, 1994.

**Panel:** Wilfred Feinberg, Senior Circuit Judge; Roger J. Miner, Circuit Judge; Alex-Lamar Stone-Khoury, Circuit Judge.

**Publication status:** Published opinion; precedential. Cite as Walker v. Bates, No. 93-2107 (2d Cir. Apr. 29, 1994).

### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** Walker appeals from a judgment of the district court dismissing, under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), his claim against Bates in an action under 42 U.S.C. § 1983. The court dismissed on one ground: that administrative reversal of the disciplinary determination cured the procedural defect Walker alleges. The dismissal rests on that cure theory alone, not on any finding that the witnesses Walker sought could properly be excluded. Other named defendants had been dismissed earlier, and their dismissals are not before us.

**Material facts.** We take the properly pleaded facts, and the inferences from them, in Walker's favor. Walker, an inmate at Southport, was charged in December 1990 with possessing a metal rod and excess bedding. He maintained that the property had been left in the cell by a prior occupant. At a Tier III disciplinary hearing, Bates, the hearing officer, refused Walker's requests to call three correction officers as witnesses and imposed 120 days in the special housing unit. The punishment began immediately. In February 1991, Donald Selsky reversed the disciplinary determination on administrative review and authorized a prompt rehearing. No rehearing occurred. Walker nevertheless remained in special housing until February 22. He then sued for damages. The record before us does not state Bates's reasons for refusing the witnesses, does not fix how many days Walker spent in special housing before and after the reversal, and does not say who was responsible for his retention after it.

**Jurisdiction.** The action arises under federal law. The dismissal with prejudice of the claim against Bates, the other named defendants having been dismissed earlier, is a final decision under 28 U.S.C. § 1291.

**Issue and standard of review.** The question is whether administrative reversal of a disciplinary determination, after the punishment it imposed has begun, defeats as a matter of law a prisoner's claim for damages for the denial of procedural due process at the hearing. Review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is de novo. A complaint may not be dismissed unless it appears beyond doubt that the plaintiff can prove no set of facts that would entitle him to relief. Conley v. Gibson, 355 U.S. 41, 45-46 (1957). On this posture the cure theory prevails only if it defeats the claim even on Walker's account of events. Whether the refusal of the witnesses was justified, whether Bates is immune, and what injury he caused are separate questions that the cure theory does not resolve.

**Positions and dates.** Walker seeks reversal and further proceedings on his witness-denial claim. Bates seeks affirmance on the ground that administrative review cured the hearing defect. The relevant dates are the December 1990 charge, the February 1991 reversal, February 22 (the end of Walker's time in special housing), oral argument on December 15, 1993, and resubmission and decision on April 29, 1994.

### Judgment

The judgment of the district court dismissing Walker's claim against Bates is REVERSED insofar as it rests on administrative cure, and the case is REMANDED for further proceedings consistent with the opinion of the court. By component:

1. Reversal of the dismissal insofar as it rests on administrative cure: Judges Feinberg, Miner, and Stone-Khoury in favor; none opposed.
2. Remand for further proceedings on the questions the cure theory did not resolve, with no direction as to outcome, no award of damages, and no determination that Bates is liable: Judges Feinberg, Miner, and Stone-Khoury in favor; none opposed.
3. The earlier dismissals of the other defendants are neither disturbed nor restored: Judges Feinberg, Miner, and Stone-Khoury in favor; none opposed.

The judgment is unanimous, 3-0.

### Opinions and holdings

**Writings.** There is one writing: the opinion of the court by Judge Miner, in which Judge Feinberg and Judge Stone-Khoury join in full. No concurring or dissenting opinion was filed. Because all three judges join the whole opinion, agreement in the judgment and agreement in the reasoning coincide.

**Controlling proposition.** When a disciplinary determination imposing punitive special-housing confinement is reversed on administrative review after the punishment has begun, the reversal is not, as a matter of law, a defense to the prisoner's section 1983 claim that the hearing denied him required process, insofar as the claim is for punishment already served. The reversal may mitigate injury. The claim itself requires a protected liberty interest and the denial of the process the Constitution requires, including the qualified right to call witnesses under Wolff, with officials bearing the burden under Ponte of justifying a refusal. Actual damages require proof of causation under Carey. Review is de novo under Conley. Judges Feinberg, Miner, and Stone-Khoury support the proposition.

**Opinion of the court.**

MINER, Circuit Judge:

Robert Walker alleges that a prison hearing officer, L. Bates, refused to let him call three correction officers as witnesses at a disciplinary hearing and then imposed 120 days in the special housing unit, that the punishment began at once, and that it was running when administrative review reversed the determination in February 1991. The district court dismissed his section 1983 claim against Bates on the ground that the reversal cured whatever was wrong with the hearing. We hold that the reversal does not defeat, as a matter of law, a claim for the punishment Walker had already undergone. We therefore reverse and remand. We decide that and nothing more, and we leave to the district court the questions the cure theory was thought to make unnecessary.

I.

A prisoner punished with segregated confinement is entitled to the process the Constitution requires before the liberty at stake is taken. To state a claim under section 1983 for denial of that process, Walker must show a protected liberty interest and the denial of constitutionally required procedure. Where New York's disciplinary system imposes special housing confinement as punishment, this circuit has recognized a protected interest and has required the procedures of Wolff v. McDonnell, 418 U.S. 539 (1974). Sher v. Coughlin, 739 F.2d 77, 81 (2d Cir. 1984); McCann v. Coughlin, 698 F.2d 112, 121-22 (2d Cir. 1983). One of those procedures is a qualified right to call witnesses and present evidence, which gives way when permitting it would be unduly hazardous to institutional safety or correctional goals. Wolff, 418 U.S. at 566. Because the right is qualified, officials must be allowed to keep hearings within reasonable limits. The Supreme Court has held that officials must at some point state their reasons for refusing a witness, that they may do so in later litigation rather than in the hearing record, and that the burden of justifying the refusal rests on them. Ponte v. Real, 471 U.S. 491, 497-500 (1985). We therefore do not read the Constitution to require contemporaneous written reasons, and nothing in this opinion imposes such a requirement.

None of these questions was decided below. The district court did not hold that Walker lacked a protected interest or that Bates had good reason to refuse the three officers, and the record does not disclose Bates's reasons. We will not supply grounds merely to sustain a dismissal that rests on a different one, and we will not foreclose grounds that Wolff and Ponte permit Bates to establish. The only ruling before us is that administrative reversal cured the defect.

II.

Bates's argument deserves a fair statement. The hearing and the administrative appeal, he says, are stages of a single process, and the process worked: the error was found, the determination was reversed, and a rehearing was authorized. Damages liability, he adds, would discourage the prompt administrative correction that prison systems should be encouraged to make. The argument has force, and Young v. Hoffman, 970 F.2d 1154, 1155-56 (2d Cir. 1992), supplies its foundation. In Young the administrative reversal came before the prisoner had served any part of the penalty. We held that the reversal was part of the process he received and cured any defect in the hearing, and that because he was never penalized he suffered no interference with a liberty interest and had no claim. Russell v. Scully, 15 F.3d 219 (2d Cir. 1994) (as modified on denial of rehearing), relied on Young in stating that an administrative appeal may correct a hearing's procedural defects.

We follow both decisions, but each rests on a premise that is absent here. In Young the sentence never operated. Walker alleges the opposite: the punishment was imposed and began at once, and it continued for a substantial period before the reversal. In that circumstance the integrated-process argument confronts a different question. The question is no longer whether a deprivation ever occurred, but whether a later correction can undo one that has been suffered. Our decisions point to the answer that it cannot, at least not automatically. In Patterson v. Coughlin, 761 F.2d 886, 893 (2d Cir. 1985), a prisoner was confined in special housing as discipline after a hearing at which he was not permitted to call witnesses, and he later obtained a stipulated order in state court under which the state agreed to restore his good time and expunge the record. We held that his section 1983 claim was not defeated by that later relief. The claim arises when the deprivation occurs without the process required beforehand, and once a cause of action for a constitutional violation has accrued, nothing the state does afterward cuts it off. Patterson involved a state-court proceeding rather than an administrative appeal, and Bates is correct that the two differ. But the reasoning on which we rely does not depend on which body acts afterward. It depends on timing: a deprivation already suffered is not undone by a later decision that it should not have been imposed.

Russell, read for what it decided, does not hold otherwise. The prisoner there had been held in special housing before and during an administrative appeal that ended in reversal. We held that the confinement itself, which the state could have imposed on administrative grounds whatever the flaws in the hearing, caused him no constitutional harm, but that the loss of privileges imposed as punishment during the appeal period could support a claim for damages. We expressly did not reach whether imposing punishment pending an administrative appeal that later corrects an error violates due process, and we resolved the case by holding that the defendant was entitled to qualified immunity. Russell therefore does not hold that a later reversal bars a claim for punishment already imposed, it left that question open, and our decision today is consistent with it.

Taking Walker's allegations as true, then, the cure theory does not defeat his claim for the confinement under Bates's order, and the dismissal cannot stand on that ground. The reversal matters, but not in the way Bates urges. It ended the operation of the order, and it may mitigate the injury Walker can attribute to the hearing. It bears on whether Bates, rather than someone else, answers for what followed it, and it bears on damages. What it cannot do is make time already spent under an allegedly unlawful punitive order disappear. Bates's concern for prompt administrative correction is met by the same distinction. The sooner a mistaken penalty is lifted, the less there is to compensate, so the rule we apply preserves the incentive. It does not support a rule under which a reversal, however late, bars the claim whatever the prisoner has already endured. We hold that administrative reversal after punitive confinement has begun is not an automatic defense to an accrued procedural due process claim for punishment already served.

III.

The holding is narrow, and we state what it does not decide. We do not decide that Walker was denied due process. He must establish a protected interest and the denial of required process, and Bates may offer safety, relevance, duplication, or other permissible grounds for refusing the officers, which the district court must consider under Wolff and Ponte. We do not hold that the refusal violated Wolff, and we do not hold that Bates is liable for any period of confinement. The complaint also places in issue the time after the reversal when Walker remained in special housing without a rehearing. Who was responsible for his retention then is left to the district court.

We do not decide that Bates lacks qualified immunity. Cleavinger v. Saxner, 474 U.S. 193, 206-07 (1985), holds that members of a prison disciplinary committee have qualified, not absolute, immunity. It does not itself decide how an administrative reversal affects liability, and we do not treat it as having done so. Whether Bates may claim the defense turns on the law clearly established when he acted. This opinion neither supplies nor assumes any clarity that the law did not already have, and the district court will decide the question on a developed record.

Finally, causation and damages are for the district court. Under Carey v. Piphus, 435 U.S. 247 (1978), the denial of process and proof of compensable injury are distinct. Actual damages require proof that the injury was caused by the denial of required process rather than by a deprivation that was justified, id. at 263-64, and the district court must distinguish detention caused by Bates's order from any independent lawful basis for confinement. A denial of procedural due process without proof of actual injury is actionable for nominal damages. Id. at 266-67. The reversal is relevant to each of these inquiries. Nothing in this opinion directs an award for every day Walker spent in special housing, and nothing makes the reversal irrelevant to damages. We apply the framework in force on the date of this decision. The dismissals of the other defendants are not before us and are not restored.

The judgment is reversed insofar as it rests on administrative cure, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

### Precedent and legal effect

This is a published opinion and is precedential. Its holding, that administrative reversal after punitive confinement has begun is not, as a matter of law, a defense to a claim for punishment already served, commands three joins: Judges Feinberg, Miner, and Stone-Khoury. The court follows Young v. Hoffman and distinguishes it on its premise that no penalty was served; it does not overrule, question, or limit Young. It follows Patterson v. Coughlin on the principle that an accrued claim is not cut off by later state action. It reads Russell v. Scully for what it decided and does not disturb it; Russell reserved the question of punishment imposed pending an administrative appeal. It applies Sher, McCann, Wolff, Ponte, Cleavinger, Carey, and Conley as those decisions stand.

The opinion changes circuit law only to the extent of the stated proposition and decides none of the questions left open in Part III. The passages restating Ponte, Cleavinger, and Carey, and the observation that reversal may mitigate injury, state settled law and are not independent holdings. Whalen v. County of Fulton, No. 93-7457 (2d Cir. Mar. 29, 1994), Part II, concerns appellate jurisdiction over orders denying qualified-immunity summary judgment without prejudice to renewal. It is not implicated here, because the order under review is a final dismissal on a cure theory and no immunity ruling is before the court.

The decision grants party-specific relief, reversal and remand. The ruling that the cure theory does not bar the claim for punishment already served is law of the case on remand; the unresolved questions have no preclusive effect. There is no concurrence or dissent, so no separate position stands apart from the opinion of the court. The decision applies the law in force on April 29, 1994.

### Remedy and next stage

**Remedy.** The dismissal of the claim against Bates is reversed insofar as it rests on administrative cure, and the case is remanded to the district court for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The court gives no direction as to outcome on any remaining question. Those questions are: whether Walker had a protected liberty interest; whether he was denied required process, including whether Bates can establish a legitimate ground for refusing the three correction officers under Wolff and Ponte; Bates's personal responsibility, including for Walker's retention in special housing after the reversal; whether Bates is entitled to qualified immunity, judged by the law clearly established when he acted; and causation and damages under Carey, including the separation of detention caused by Bates's order from any independent lawful basis for confinement, and whether any relief is nominal or compensatory. No damages are awarded and no finding of liability is made. The other defendants are not restored.

Under Fed. R. App. P. 41(a) as then in force, the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the court shortens or enlarges the time, and a timely petition for rehearing stays the mandate until the petition is disposed of. The district court takes up the remand after the mandate issues. No other filing or next step is recorded.
