## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Administrative reversal after punishment and the surviving due-process claim

**Jurisdiction and reach:** The dismissal rests on administrative cure, not a factual determination that the witnesses could properly be excluded. Other defendants’ prior dismissals are outside this appeal’s requested restoration. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Reverse and remand without directing damages or finding Bates liable.

**Governing law:** De novo review of Rule 12(b)(6), taking the properly pleaded facts and inferences favorably to Walker under the 1994 pleading standard. A protected liberty interest and denial of constitutionally required process are necessary. The qualified witness right permits legitimate institutional restrictions. Reversal before punishment may prevent a deprivation; reversal after punishment can mitigate without necessarily erasing one. Liability, qualified immunity, causation, and damages remain distinct.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** Administrative reversal after punitive confinement has begun is not an automatic defense to an accrued procedural-due-process claim.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose reversal of the dismissal and remand. A disciplinary appeal can prevent a deprivation if it corrects the error before punishment. It can also terminate an ongoing deprivation and mitigate injury. It cannot make time already spent under an allegedly unlawful punitive order disappear.

That conclusion does not constitutionalize every violation of a prison rule. Walker must establish a protected interest and a denial of the process the Constitution requires. The witness right is qualified. Bates may offer legitimate safety, relevance, duplication, or other permissible grounds, and the district court must consider them under *Wolff* and *Ponte*. The appellate court should not infer such grounds merely to rescue dismissal, but neither should it foreclose grounds the governing doctrine permits the official to establish.

The proposal rests on the character and timing of the deprivation. Once the hearing imposed and immediately activated punitive confinement, the question was whether adequate procedure supported that confinement. An administrative remedy capable only of later reversal does not necessarily furnish the predeprivation protection required for an established disciplinary process. The integrated-process argument has force in *Young*, where the sentence never operated; it loses that force where the prisoner has already borne the punishment.

The remedy must remain disciplined. Reversal does not direct a damages award for every day in special housing. The court must distinguish detention caused by Bates's order from any independent lawful basis for confinement and determine who was responsible for continued confinement after reversal. Under *Carey*, actual damages require causal proof; an established procedural violation without proven compensatory injury may support nominal damages. Qualified immunity remains governed by the law clearly established when Bates acted, not by the clarity supplied by this appeal.

Stone should expressly identify *Cleavinger*'s limited holding, rather than improve the argument by overstating Supreme Court authority. His broader proposed circuit rule is that reversal after disciplinary punishment begins is not an automatic defense to an accrued procedural-due-process claim.
