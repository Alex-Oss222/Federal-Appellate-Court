## Decision summary

Cynthia Yvette Anderson pleaded guilty to credit-card fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1029(a)(2). She was sentenced to six months' imprisonment and three years of supervised release, which began in August 1991. Her conditions restricted controlled substances and firearms and required participation in substance-abuse treatment. The probation officer sought revocation in October 1992. In January 1993 the United States District Court for the District of Connecticut (T. F. Gilroy Daly, District Judge) found violations, and on January 26, 1993 it revoked her supervised release and sentenced her to seventeen months' imprisonment. The Chapter Seven policy statements of the Sentencing Guidelines recommended six to twelve months, and the statutory ceiling was twenty-four months. The district court expressly emphasized the need for structured drug and psychological treatment and access to an intensive institutional program. The record also reflects poor cooperation with supervision.

Anderson appealed and sought resentencing. The United States sought affirmance. She raised two questions:

1. Whether a district court revoking supervised release may select or lengthen the term of imprisonment in order to promote the defendant's rehabilitation.
2. Whether the Chapter Seven recommended range of six to twelve months binds the sentencing court.

The court answered the first question no, by a vote of two to one. When a district court revokes supervised release under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e) and requires the defendant to serve time in prison, it may not select the length of that imprisonment, or lengthen it, to promote the defendant's rehabilitation or to obtain treatment or a program for the defendant. Because the district court relied on that objective in choosing seventeen months, and the record does not show that the error was harmless, the term of imprisonment is vacated and the case is remanded for resentencing. The court unanimously answered the second question no: the Chapter Seven policy statements must be considered but do not bind, so an otherwise lawful term need not fall within six to twelve months.

The finding of violations and the order revoking supervised release are not disturbed. The court imposes no twelve-month ceiling, no direction to release Anderson, and no appellate finding of fact, and its instructions presuppose no further term of supervised release. Judge Oakes would have affirmed.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

KEARSE, Circuit Judge:

**I. Background and threshold matters** (joined by Oakes, Kearse and Stone-Khoury)

Cynthia Yvette Anderson appeals from the sentence imposed on January 26, 1993, by the United States District Court for the District of Connecticut (T. F. Gilroy Daly, Judge), upon revoking her supervised release. Having pleaded guilty to credit-card fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1029(a)(2), she began a three-year term of supervised release in August 1991, on conditions that included restrictions on controlled substances and firearms and participation in substance-abuse treatment. The district court found violations in January 1993. The Chapter Seven policy statements recommended six to twelve months' imprisonment; the statutory ceiling was twenty-four months. The court imposed seventeen months, emphasizing Anderson's need for structured drug and psychological treatment and for access to an intensive institutional program. The record also reflects poor cooperation with supervision.

Anderson does not contest the violations or the court's power to imprison her. She argues that the court could not lawfully rely on rehabilitation to fix the length of the term and that the Chapter Seven range capped her sentence at twelve months. The United States asks us to affirm. We have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and 18 U.S.C. § 3742. The meaning of the statutes and the force of the policy statements are legal questions we decide independently. We defer to supported findings of fact, but a legally impermissible sentencing objective is not insulated from review because the court adopted it in the exercise of discretion.

The papers before us do not disclose Anderson's present custody status or how much of the term she has served. The questions presented do not include mootness, timeliness or forfeiture, and we decide the appeal on the reasons the district court itself gave. The term under review has not been shown to have expired, and a sentence that may still be corrected presents a live controversy. We therefore reach the merits and leave to the district court on remand any effect of intervening events.

**II. Rehabilitation and the length of imprisonment on revocation** (joined by Stone-Khoury)

*A. The sentencing choice.* The question is narrower than a general debate about treatment. Anderson was not merely directed to a program while serving a term chosen for independent reasons. The district court identified the need for structured treatment and an institutional program as a reason for the seventeen months it selected. The issue is whether a court that revokes supervised release may fix the length of the imprisonment it requires in order to obtain rehabilitative services for the defendant. That treatment is desirable, or that the treatment factor in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)(2)(D) bears on some decisions in a revocation proceeding, does not answer it. We must ask what Congress permitted a court to do with the instrument of imprisonment.

*B. The statutory text.* Section 3582(a) directs a court that is deciding whether to imprison, and for how long, to consider the applicable factors in § 3553(a), "recognizing that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting correction and rehabilitation." Title 28, section 994(k), expresses the same judgment by directing the Sentencing Commission to ensure that the guidelines reflect "the inappropriateness of imposing a sentence to a term of imprisonment for the purpose of rehabilitating the defendant." The Supreme Court has described the Sentencing Reform Act as one that "rejects imprisonment as a means of promoting rehabilitation." Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989). Mistretta describes the design of the Act and does not decide the question before us, so we cite it only as confirmation of what the text says. Our own decisions recognize the line the statute draws. In United States v. Maier, 975 F.2d 944, 947 (2d Cir. 1992), we observed that rehabilitation "may not be a basis for incarceration but must be considered as a basis for a sentence." Maier arose from a departure to probation, and we do not extend its holding beyond that noncustodial setting. Neither Maier nor Bermudez decides whether § 3582(a) governs the length of a revocation term. But Maier's reading of the statutory design is the one we apply.

Nothing in these provisions confines the limitation to an initial sentence. Section 3582(a) speaks of a term of imprisonment and of the determination of its length, and § 994(k) speaks of imprisonment imposed for the purpose of rehabilitating the defendant. A prison term that a court requires a defendant to serve after revocation is a term of imprisonment, and choosing how long it will be is a determination of its length. The policy Congress attached to those decisions is stated in terms of the means employed, not of the procedural label under which the court acts.

*C. The Government's reading.* The United States responds that revocation is governed by § 3583(e). That section authorizes a court, after considering specified § 3553(a) factors that include the need to provide the defendant with "needed educational or vocational training, medical care, or other correctional treatment," § 3553(a)(2)(D), to revoke supervised release and require service in prison of all or part of the term. The Government stresses that § 3583(e) incorporates the treatment factor yet does not cross-reference § 3582(a), and that it speaks of requiring imprisonment rather than imposing it. Because rehabilitation properly informs the choice and length of supervision in the first place, the Government reasons, it remains relevant when supervision fails. It adds that a seventeen-month term below the statutory maximum is lawful and also reflects Anderson's failure to cooperate. The argument is a serious one, but we are not persuaded, for three reasons.

First, the absence of a cross-reference is not a disclaimer. Section 3582(a) is a general direction about how a court is to decide whether and for how long to imprison, and § 3583(e) says nothing exempting revocation terms from it. The difference between requiring and imposing a term is a weak basis for withdrawing a policy Congress attached to decisions about imprisonment itself. A defendant returned to prison after revocation serves a prison term, and the judge who sets its length makes the same decision whatever the label.

Second, reading the two sections together gives the treatment factor in § 3583(e) real work without turning prison into a rehabilitative tool. The factor is not peculiar to revocation. It is part of § 3553(a), and § 3582(a) directs the court to consider the applicable § 3553(a) factors in the very sentence that tells it to recognize that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting rehabilitation. Incorporating § 3553(a)(2)(D) into § 3583(e) therefore cannot be read as a license to pursue that factor by the means § 3582(a) disclaims. Nor is the incorporation idle on our reading. Section 3583(e) lets the court extend a term of supervised release and modify its conditions, and treatment conditions like those in Anderson's own terms remain available in that process. The treatment factor properly guides those choices, as Maier's recognition of rehabilitation's role in nonprison sentencing confirms. What it does not do is authorize the addition of months of imprisonment so that a program may be obtained.

Third, the statutory ceiling answers a different question. That seventeen months is under twenty-four shows the term is not too long, not that the reason for choosing that length was a permissible one. A legally forbidden objective does not become lawful because the sentence it produced lies below the maximum. Nor can we affirm on the Government's alternative ground. Poor cooperation with supervision, protection of the public, deterrence and other lawful considerations that § 3583(e) incorporates may well support imprisonment, and even a term above the Chapter Seven range. But the district court expressly relied on treatment, and we may not uphold the sentence by supposing it would have reached the same length for other reasons while setting aside the reason it gave.

*D. Holding.* We hold that when a district court revokes a term of supervised release under § 3583(e) and requires the defendant to serve time in prison, it may not select the length of that imprisonment, or lengthen it, for the purpose of promoting the defendant's rehabilitation or of obtaining treatment or a program for the defendant. The treatment consideration in § 3583(e) does not authorize adding imprisonment for rehabilitation. It remains relevant to the treatment and supervision choices that are lawfully open to the court, including the conditions, term and structure of supervision, and recommendations for available care when custody rests on other grounds. We do not hold that treatment is irrelevant to revocation, we announce no constitutional right to treatment outside prison, and we direct no particular term. Applied here, the district court's stated reliance on Anderson's need for structured treatment and an institutional program in selecting seventeen months was an impermissible basis for the length of her imprisonment, and the sentence was imposed in violation of law.

**III. The Chapter Seven range** (joined by Oakes, Kearse and Stone-Khoury)

Anderson also contends that the recommended range of six to twelve months capped her sentence. We addressed the force of these statements in United States v. Bermudez, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992), where we described them as "advisory rather than mandatory in nature," while adding that "[c]ourts should nonetheless take the policy statements into account when sentencing for a violation of supervised release." The statements must therefore be considered, but they do not bind, and an otherwise lawful term need not fall within six to twelve months.

Anderson invokes the Supreme Court's treatment of Commission materials in Williams v. United States, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), and Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36 (1993). Those decisions concern policy statements and commentary that operate with a governing guideline. Williams treats a policy statement that prohibits a specified action as "an authoritative guide to the meaning of the applicable Guideline," 503 U.S. at 201, and Stinson concerns commentary that interprets or explains a guideline. Neither converts every policy statement into a mandatory guideline, and neither addresses the stand-alone revocation recommendations of Chapter Seven, as to which Bermudez is controlling circuit authority that a panel must follow.

Our disposition of the first issue does not change that conclusion. Correcting an unlawful sentencing purpose does not make the recommended range mandatory. On resentencing the district court must consider the Chapter Seven statements, and it may select a term within the statutory maximum on lawful grounds, whether inside the recommended range or outside it. The claim that the sentence is limited to twelve months is rejected.

**IV. Remedy** (joined by Stone-Khoury)

When a sentence has been imposed in violation of law, 18 U.S.C. § 3742(f)(1) directs a remand for further sentencing proceedings with such instructions as the court considers appropriate. Where a sentencing court relied on an impermissible consideration, remand is appropriate unless the reviewing court concludes, on the record as a whole, that the error was harmless. Williams, 503 U.S. at 203. Nothing in the record suggests that the district court would have chosen seventeen months without regard to the treatment objective, and its express emphasis on that objective points the other way. We have no basis to treat the error as harmless, and we may neither decide disputed sentencing facts nor choose the sentence ourselves.

We therefore vacate the term of imprisonment and remand for resentencing. We do not disturb the finding of violations or the court's power to revoke and imprison. On remand the district court is to select a term justified by lawful considerations, so that the permissible grounds for whatever term it chooses are apparent, and it may not select additional months because a particular program requires them. It must consider the Chapter Seven policy statements but is not confined to the recommended range. We impose no twelve-month ceiling and no direction to release Anderson. These instructions do not bar consideration of treatment in setting conditions of supervision or recommending care, and they presuppose no further term of supervised release, which this court has held § 3583(e) does not authorize after revocation and imprisonment. Bermudez, 974 F.2d at 13; United States v. Koehler, 973 F.2d 132, 133 (2d Cir. 1992).

The judgment vacating the term of imprisonment is entered, and the case is remanded.

## Separate opinions

### Opinion of Judge Oakes, concurring in part and dissenting in part

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.* Joined by no other judge. The final record establishes this writing in the following extent.

Judge Oakes joins Parts I and III of the court's opinion and agrees that the Chapter Seven statements are advisory and must be considered, though they do not bind. He dissents from Part II, from Part IV, and from the vacatur. In his view the district court's choice of term should be affirmed. Revocation under § 3583(e) is a distinct statutory framework. That section expressly directs the court to consider the treatment factor in § 3553(a)(2)(D) and contains no cross-reference to § 3582(a). The recognition in § 3582(a) that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting rehabilitation addresses the initial decision to imprison, and it does not bar treatment considerations in choosing the length of a revocation term. Because the seventeen-month term lies within the twenty-four month ceiling and the Chapter Seven statements are advisory, he would hold that the sentence is lawful and affirm. His opinion does not suggest that the recommended range may be disregarded, and it advances no ground beyond those stated here.

## What the decision does

- Controlling holding (Proposition A, Part II; Kearse and Stone-Khoury). When a district court revokes supervised release under 18 U.S.C. § 3583(e) and requires the defendant to serve time in prison, it may not select the length of that imprisonment, or lengthen it, to promote the defendant's rehabilitation or to obtain treatment or a program for the defendant. Authority: 18 U.S.C. §§ 3582(a), 3583(e), 3553(a)(2)(D); 28 U.S.C. § 994(k); Maier, 975 F.2d at 946-47; Mistretta, 488 U.S. at 367 (confirming the statutory design only). Test: § 3582(a)'s limitation on imprisonment is read together with § 3583(e)'s incorporation of § 3553(a)(2)(D). Burden: Anderson shows the impermissible basis from the district court's own stated reasons; the term then stands only if the record as a whole shows the error did not affect the term selected (Williams, 503 U.S. at 203). Qualification: treatment remains relevant to the conditions and structure of supervision and to recommendations of care when custody rests on other grounds. Application: the express reliance on structured treatment and an institutional program in choosing seventeen months was error, and the record does not show it harmless.
- Controlling holding (Proposition B, Part III; unanimous). Chapter Seven's revocation policy statements must be considered but do not bind, so an otherwise lawful term need not fall within six to twelve months. Authority: Bermudez, 974 F.2d at 14; 18 U.S.C. §§ 3583(e), 3742; Williams and Stinson distinguished. Standard: independent review. Application: the claimed twelve-month ceiling fails.
- Remedy (Proposition C, Part IV; Kearse and Stone-Khoury). Vacate the term only, with no twelve-month or release directive, no appellate fact-finding, and no presupposition of a further term of supervised release.
- Principal contrary argument and the court's answer. The contrary argument, adopted by the dissent, is that § 3583(e) is a self-contained revocation provision that incorporates the treatment factor but not § 3582(a), so rehabilitation may properly influence how long the revocation term lasts. The court's answer is that § 3582(a) speaks to imprisonment however it is reached, that § 3583(e)'s incorporation of the treatment factor does independent work in conditions and supervision and cannot license the means § 3582(a) disclaims, and that a term under the statutory maximum does not validate an impermissible reason for its length.
- Precedential effect. This is a published opinion of a three-judge panel and is precedential under the circuit's settled practice, binding later panels unless overruled in banc or displaced by intervening Supreme Court authority. The rule in Part II (Proposition A) is circuit law on the votes of Kearse and Stone-Khoury, notwithstanding Judge Oakes's dissent; the dissent is not circuit law. Part III (Proposition B) is a unanimous application of Bermudez and changes nothing in that decision. Maier is followed for its statement of the statutory design and is not extended as a holding beyond its noncustodial setting. Mistretta is cited as description only. Williams is applied for the harmless-error standard and, with Stinson, distinguished as to Chapter Seven. Bermudez and Koehler are followed on the unavailability of a further supervised-release term. What changes within the circuit is that a court revoking supervised release may no longer lengthen imprisonment to promote rehabilitation. The observations that other lawful considerations may support a term above the recommended range, and that treatment may be recommended or addressed through conditions, are qualifications on the holding and are not applied to this record. The vacatur of Anderson's seventeen-month term and the remand instructions in Part IV are relief specific to this case, not circuit-wide rules; the remand rulings bind the district court as law of the case in this proceeding.
- Left open. The court does not decide the appropriate length of the term on remand under permissible objectives; whether any constitutional right to treatment outside prison exists; the effect of Anderson's custody status or any time already served on resentencing; whether the district court considered the Chapter Seven range at the first sentencing; or any question about the Guidelines system beyond Bermudez.
- Effect on the parties. The seventeen-month term is vacated and the case is remanded to the United States District Court for the District of Connecticut for resentencing consistent with this opinion, in the manner described in Part IV. No twelve-month ceiling, release directive or appellate finding of fact is imposed, and nothing presupposes a further term of supervised release. Violation findings and the revocation order stand. Anderson's claim that the Chapter Seven range capped her sentence at twelve months is rejected.
- Mandate and remand. The mandate has not issued. Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 41(a) as in force on this date, the mandate issues twenty-one days after entry of judgment unless that time is shortened or enlarged by order, and the timely filing of a petition for rehearing stays it pending disposition. Under Rule 40(a), a petition for rehearing in a criminal case may be filed within fourteen days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order or local rule. No petition, motion for a stay, or other filing is recorded. Resentencing in the district court awaits issuance of the mandate.
