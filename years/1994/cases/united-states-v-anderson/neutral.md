# United States v. Anderson

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 93-1097. **Decision date:** February 17, 1994. **Citation:** 15 F.3d 278. **Court below:** District of Connecticut, Judge T. F. Gilroy Daly. **Historical argument:** November 1, 1993. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/15/278/536351/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Anderson pleaded guilty to credit-card fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1029(a)(2), received six months' imprisonment and three years of supervised release, and began supervised release in August 1991. His conditions included restrictions on controlled substances and firearms and participation in substance-abuse treatment. The probation officer sought revocation in October 1992. The district court found violations in January 1993.

Chapter Seven's policy statements recommended six to twelve months' imprisonment. The statutory ceiling was twenty-four months. The district court imposed seventeen months, expressly emphasizing the need for structured drug and psychological treatment and access to an intensive institutional program. The record also reflected poor cooperation with supervision. The appeal therefore requires separation of a potentially proper custodial response to violations from an impermissible reason for selecting its duration. *Anderson*, 15 F.3d at 279-81.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The court reviews a final revocation sentence under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the applicable provisions of 18 U.S.C. § 3742. The district court's authority to revoke comes from § 3583(e). The principal legal questions are whether imprisonment following revocation may be lengthened to provide rehabilitation and whether Chapter Seven's recommended range is binding.

Statutory meaning and the legal force of the policy statements are reviewed independently. Supported factual findings receive deference, but a legally impermissible sentencing objective cannot be insulated as discretion. Review must use the statutory sentencing regime existing in February 1994, not later advisory-Guidelines or rehabilitation decisions.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 3582(a) directs the sentencing court, when determining whether and for how long to impose imprisonment, to recognize that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting correction and rehabilitation. Section 994(k) of Title 28 contains a related direction to the Sentencing Commission. Conversely, § 3583(e) incorporates several § 3553(a) considerations, including the defendant's need for medical care or other correctional treatment under § 3553(a)(2)(D).

The government's strongest reading treats revocation as a distinct statutory operation. Section 3583(e) permits the court to require imprisonment for all or part of the authorized period and expressly incorporates treatment considerations. It does not expressly incorporate § 3582(a). Rehabilitation can legitimately affect the original choice and length of supervision, so the government argues that the same consideration remains relevant when supervision fails.

Anderson's stronger structural response is that treatment remains relevant to many choices under § 3583(e), including modification or extension of conditions and nonprison supervision. Giving those words effect does not require construing them as authority to lengthen incarceration for treatment. The difference between “impose” and “require” is a weak basis for withdrawing a policy Congress attached to decisions about imprisonment. *United States v. Maier*, 975 F.2d 944, 946-48 (2d Cir. 1992), recognized rehabilitation's relevance to a noncustodial disposition while emphasizing the statutory rejection of imprisonment as its instrument. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989), describes that reform in the sentencing system's design.

Chapter Seven presents a separate question. *United States v. Bermudez*, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992), treats its revocation recommendations as advisory. *Williams v. United States*, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), addresses policy statements that operate with governing guidelines; it does not convert every Commission policy statement into a mandatory guideline. The distinction is compatible with *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), concerning authoritative guideline commentary. Consequently, an otherwise lawful sentence need not fall within six to twelve months merely because that is the Chapter Seven recommendation.
