### Issue 1: Rehabilitation as a reason to lengthen revocation imprisonment

**Preservation and reach:** Anderson’s challenge concerns the expressly stated basis of the revocation term. The existence of violations and power to impose some imprisonment are distinct from the reason for its length.

**Standard of review:** Independent statutory interpretation; factual findings receive the deference their nature requires. A legally forbidden objective is not insulated as sentencing discretion.

**Governing test and burdens:** Read § 3582(a)’s limitation on imprisonment together with § 3583(e)’s incorporation of § 3553(a)(2)(D), without treating treatment as categorically irrelevant to nonprison supervision.

Section 3582(a) directs the sentencing court, when determining whether and for how long to impose imprisonment, to recognize that imprisonment is not an appropriate means of promoting correction and rehabilitation. Section 994(k) of Title 28 contains a related direction to the Sentencing Commission. Conversely, § 3583(e) incorporates several § 3553(a) considerations, including the defendant's need for medical care or other correctional treatment under § 3553(a)(2)(D).

The government's strongest reading treats revocation as a distinct statutory operation. Section 3583(e) permits the court to require imprisonment for all or part of the authorized period and expressly incorporates treatment considerations. It does not expressly incorporate § 3582(a). Rehabilitation can legitimately affect the original choice and length of supervision, so the government argues that the same consideration remains relevant when supervision fails.

Anderson's stronger structural response is that treatment remains relevant to many choices under § 3583(e), including modification or extension of conditions and nonprison supervision. Giving those words effect does not require construing them as authority to lengthen incarceration for treatment. The difference between “impose” and “require” is a weak basis for withdrawing a policy Congress attached to decisions about imprisonment. *United States v. Maier*, 975 F.2d 944, 946-48 (2d Cir. 1992), recognized rehabilitation's relevance to a noncustodial disposition while emphasizing the statutory rejection of imprisonment as its instrument. *Mistretta v. United States*, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989), describes that reform in the sentencing system's design.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989), describes Congress’s rejection of imprisonment as a rehabilitative instrument; it does not itself decide this specific revocation construction.

**Other governing authority:** 18 U.S.C. §§ 3553(a)(2)(D), 3582(a), 3583(e); 28 U.S.C. § 994(k); United States v. Maier, 975 F.2d 944, 946-48 (2d Cir. 1992), binding within its noncustodial sentencing setting.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** Whether the imprisonment limitation governs the prison-duration choice on revocation despite the cross-reference structure.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The judge selected additional months to obtain an institutional treatment program, the use of imprisonment Congress rejected. Treatment retains work in supervision without increasing incarceration.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Revocation is governed by § 3583(e), which specifically incorporates treatment needs but not § 3582(a); the sentence also reflects failed supervision and lies below the statutory maximum.

### Issue 2: Legal force of Chapter Seven’s policy statements

**Preservation and reach:** Anderson also contends the recommended 6-to-12-month range constrains the revocation sentence.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the legal force of the policy statements.

**Governing test and burdens:** Chapter Seven’s revocation policy statements must be considered but, under Bermudez, do not bind as an applicable mandatory sentencing guideline.

Chapter Seven presents a separate question. *United States v. Bermudez*, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992), treats its revocation recommendations as advisory. *Williams v. United States*, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), addresses policy statements that operate with governing guidelines; it does not convert every Commission policy statement into a mandatory guideline. The distinction is compatible with *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), concerning authoritative guideline commentary. Consequently, an otherwise lawful sentence need not fall within six to twelve months merely because that is the Chapter Seven recommendation.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Williams v. United States, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), addresses policy statements implementing governing guidelines; Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), addresses authoritative commentary. Neither automatically makes every policy statement a mandatory guideline.

**Other governing authority:** United States v. Bermudez, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992); U.S.S.G. Chapter Seven; 18 U.S.C. §§ 3583(e), 3742.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** No panel-level authority to disregard Bermudez merely because a different Commission document is binding.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The sentencing framework and Supreme Court treatment of Commission statements require closer adherence to the recommended range.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Bermudez directly treats these revocation recommendations as advisory, and the statutory maximum rather than the policy range controls the outer limit.
