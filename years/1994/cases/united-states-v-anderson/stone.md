## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Rehabilitation as a reason to lengthen revocation imprisonment

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Anderson’s challenge concerns the expressly stated basis of the revocation term. The existence of violations and power to impose some imprisonment are distinct from the reason for its length. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Vacate the 17-month term and remand for selection of a term without increasing imprisonment to obtain rehabilitation.

**Governing law:** Independent statutory interpretation; factual findings receive the deference their nature requires. A legally forbidden objective is not insulated as sentencing discretion. Read § 3582(a)’s limitation on imprisonment together with § 3583(e)’s incorporation of § 3553(a)(2)(D), without treating treatment as categorically irrelevant to nonprison supervision.

Mistretta v. United States, 488 U.S. 361, 367 (1989), describes Congress’s rejection of imprisonment as a rehabilitative instrument; it does not itself decide this specific revocation construction. 18 U.S.C. §§ 3553(a)(2)(D), 3582(a), 3583(e); 28 U.S.C. § 994(k); United States v. Maier, 975 F.2d 944, 946-48 (2d Cir. 1992), binding within its noncustodial sentencing setting.

**Legal relationship:** Resolving an open question; applying binding statutory and circuit authority within its scope.

**Proposed holding:** The treatment consideration in § 3583(e) does not authorize adding imprisonment for rehabilitation; it remains relevant to lawful treatment and supervision choices.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose vacatur of the seventeen-month sentence and remand for resentencing without using additional incarceration to obtain rehabilitation. He should accept the advisory character of Chapter Seven. These conclusions are complementary: the court has genuine discretion outside the policy-statement range, but not discretion to use an objective Congress has excluded from the decision to imprison.

The opinion should begin with the actual sentencing choice. Anderson was not merely directed toward treatment while otherwise serving an independently selected term. The judge used the need for a structured program as a reason for choosing seventeen months. The inquiry therefore cannot be resolved by observing that treatment is desirable or that § 3553(a)(2)(D) is relevant somewhere in the proceeding. It is relevant to the available measures Congress authorized for that purpose.

Section 3583(e)'s treatment cross-reference has substantial work to do even if prison duration cannot be increased for rehabilitation. Conditions can require treatment; the period and structure of supervision can reflect treatment needs; a court selecting lawful custody for other reasons can recommend available care. The construction Stone proposes preserves all of those functions. The government's alternative, in contrast, makes a change in the label of the sentencing proceeding sufficient to restore imprisonment's rehabilitative rationale, despite § 3582(a)'s direct instruction about imprisonment itself.

Stone should not announce a constitutional right to treatment outside prison or hold that rehabilitation is irrelevant to revocation as a whole. Nor should he direct a twelve-month sentence. Poor compliance, protection of the public, deterrence, and other incorporated lawful considerations may support a term above the Chapter Seven recommendation. The proper mandate requires the district court to exercise that discretion again, explain the permissible reasons, and refrain from selecting extra months because a particular program requires them.

This is an interpretation of the statutes available in 1994, informed by *Maier* and the distinction between imprisonment and other sentencing measures. It does not depend on the later Supreme Court treatment of rehabilitation. The

### Component 2

**Component:** Legal force of Chapter Seven’s policy statements

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Anderson also contends the recommended 6-to-12-month range constrains the revocation sentence. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Reject the argument that Chapter Seven caps the sentence at 12 months.

**Governing law:** Independent review of the legal force of the policy statements. Chapter Seven’s revocation policy statements must be considered but, under Bermudez, do not bind as an applicable mandatory sentencing guideline.

Williams v. United States, 503 U.S. 193 (1992), addresses policy statements implementing governing guidelines; Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), addresses authoritative commentary. Neither automatically makes every policy statement a mandatory guideline. United States v. Bermudez, 974 F.2d 12, 14 (2d Cir. 1992); U.S.S.G. Chapter Seven; 18 U.S.C. §§ 3583(e), 3742.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** Chapter Seven remains advisory in this revocation setting, and correction of an unlawful sentencing purpose does not make its recommendation mandatory.

**Legal reasoning:** Bermudez supplies the circuit rule. Williams and Stinson distinguish the effect of materials connected to binding guidelines; their reasoning does not erase that rule for stand-alone revocation recommendations. The remand in the separate rehabilitation component therefore leaves authority to select a different lawful term within the statute.
