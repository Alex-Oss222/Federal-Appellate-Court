## Decision summary

### The dispute

Cities, states, organizations, and individual residents challenged the refusal of the Secretary of Commerce to adjust the 1990 census. Their interests include representation and the distribution of public funds. Wisconsin and Oklahoma intervened as defendants and support the Secretary, emphasizing distributive accuracy and the allocation of seats and funds. The controversy concerns whether the enumeration, supplemented by a post-enumeration survey (PES), yields a better measure of population. It does not concern whether every person could be counted without error.

The Bureau of the Census surveyed approximately 170,000 households, compared the results with the enumeration, and developed adjustment factors for 1,392 population categories. Its estimates indicated an overall undercount of approximately 5.3 million people, substantially higher for several racial and ethnic minorities than for non-Hispanic whites. These are survey estimates in the record, not findings of an independently known exact population. The Secretary acknowledged that adjusted figures would probably improve numerical accuracy nationally and in jurisdictions holding about two-thirds of the population. He nevertheless declined to adjust, stressing distributive accuracy, meaning the proportions assigned to different places, together with uncertainty in local estimates, possible changes in research findings, and the implications for later censuses. His decision was made on July 15, 1991.

### How the case reached this court

After a thirteen-day bench trial, the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York (Joseph M. McLaughlin, Circuit Judge, sitting by designation) dismissed the complaint. 822 F. Supp. 906 (E.D.N.Y. 1993). The district court found that adjustment produced a more accurate count for most purposes and that the plaintiffs had mounted a compelling attack on several aspects of the decision. It also found the Secretary's choice and application of his eight guidelines not arbitrary or capricious, regarded a focus on distributive accuracy as compatible with the census's representative function, and stated that disagreement over the better exercise of discretion was not enough to overturn the decision. The plaintiffs appealed and ask for reversal and the use, or further consideration, of adjusted figures for the uses challenged. The federal defendants and the intervenors ask for affirmance.

### The questions and the result

The appeal presents two questions. The first is whether the district court correctly resolved the constitutional challenge, under Article I, § 2 and the Fourteenth Amendment, § 2, to the refusal to adjust. The second is whether 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195 limit or require the use of sampling and adjustment, and what remedy, if any, may lawfully follow.

The court vacates the judgment of dismissal and remands. Judges Kearse and Stone vote to vacate and remand; Judge Timbers would affirm. The vacatur rests on a narrow ground. The district court asked whether the Secretary's decision was arbitrary or capricious, which is a question about administrative rationality, and did not state a constitutional standard and apply it to the reasons the Secretary actually gave. The court does not hold that adjustment was required or that the Secretary's choice was unlawful, and it does not weigh the experts or choose a statistical method. On the statutory question, Judges Kearse and Timbers hold that 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195 do not command the Secretary to adopt adjusted figures for any census use; Judge Stone does not join that holding. All three judges agree on the limits of any relief: this court orders no adjustment, reapportionment, or reallocation, and no order directed to the President.

## Opinion of the court

Opinion by Judge Kearse, which is the opinion of the court in the Parts and with the joins stated for each Part. Part III.C of Judge Kearse's opinion states her own view and is not part of the opinion of the court; it appears under "Separate writings" below.

### Part I. Background (Judges Kearse and Stone)

**The decision under review.** This appeal is from a judgment of the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York (Joseph M. McLaughlin, Circuit Judge, sitting by designation), entered after a thirteen-day bench trial, dismissing the complaint that challenged the Secretary's refusal to adjust the 1990 census. The opinion below is reported at 822 F. Supp. 906 (E.D.N.Y. 1993). The materials before this court do not show the date on which judgment was entered. The Secretary's decision under challenge was made on July 15, 1991.

**The material record.** The plaintiffs are cities, states, organizations, and individual residents; their interests include representation and the distribution of public funds. Wisconsin and Oklahoma intervened as defendants and support the Secretary, emphasizing distributive accuracy and the allocation of seats and funds. The controversy concerns whether the enumeration, supplemented by a post-enumeration survey (PES), yields a better measure of population. It does not concern whether every person could be counted without error. A 1989 stipulation required a fresh consideration of adjustment, the development of review guidelines, a survey capable of producing adjusted figures, and an explained decision. The Bureau of the Census surveyed approximately 170,000 households, compared the results with the enumeration, and developed adjustment factors for 1,392 population categories. Its estimates indicated an overall undercount of approximately 5.3 million people, substantially higher for several racial and ethnic minorities than for non-Hispanic whites. These are survey estimates in the record, not findings of an independently known exact population.

The Secretary acknowledged that adjusted figures would probably improve numerical accuracy nationally and in jurisdictions holding about two-thirds of the population. He nevertheless stressed distributive accuracy, meaning the proportions assigned to different places, together with uncertainty in local estimates, possible changes in research findings, and the implications for later censuses. The advisory panel divided evenly. The Bureau's director favored adjustment; other senior advisers opposed it. The Secretary expressly disclaimed any belief that the survey had been manipulated. The district court found that adjustment produced a more accurate count for most purposes and that the plaintiffs had mounted a compelling attack on several aspects of the decision. It also found the Secretary's choice and application of his eight guidelines not arbitrary or capricious, regarded a focus on distributive accuracy as compatible with the census's representative function, and stated that disagreement over the better exercise of discretion was not enough to overturn the decision. 822 F. Supp. at 916, 920-29.

**Relief sought.** The plaintiffs seek reversal and the use, or further consideration, of adjusted figures for the uses challenged. The federal defendants and the intervenors seek affirmance.

**Jurisdiction.** The judgment is a final judgment reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; the claims invoke 28 U.S.C. § 1331. The notice of appeal and the entry showing the date of judgment are not among the materials before the court. Jurisdiction is exercised on the premise, which the materials neither confirm nor contradict, that the notice of appeal was timely under Fed. R. App. P. 4(a).

**Issues and standards of review.** The appeal presents two issues.

1. Whether the district court correctly resolved the constitutional challenge, under Article I, § 2 and the Fourteenth Amendment, § 2, to the refusal to adjust. The governing constitutional standard is a question of law reviewed de novo. Findings of fact made after the bench trial are reviewed for clear error under Fed. R. Civ. P. 52(a). Neither standard permits this court to choose the statistical estimator it prefers. The level of deference owed to the Secretary's good-faith census judgment must be identified, not assumed.
2. Whether 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195 limit or require the use of sampling and adjustment, and what remedy, if any, may lawfully follow. Statutory construction and jurisdiction are reviewed de novo; equitable relief is available only within an established remedial power.

### Part II. Jurisdiction, threshold matters, and standards of review (Judges Kearse, Stone, and Timbers)

The final judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, and the claims arise under 28 U.S.C. § 1331. The plaintiffs must establish standing for the injuries they assert and for each form of relief they seek. The breadth of the coalition neither creates standing nor turns an individual representational injury into a generalized grievance. The City and State of New York and the other governmental plaintiffs assert concrete injuries to representation and to distributions of public funds that follow from the Secretary's decision. This court has held that the City, the State, and individual voters had standing to challenge census conduct on allegations of reduced federal funds and vote dilution, that such a claim is justiciable, and that it is not committed to agency discretion by law. *Carey v. Klutznick*, 637 F.2d 834 (2d Cir. 1980) (per curiam). A later decision treated that holding as settled. *Carey v. Klutznick*, 653 F.2d 732 (2d Cir. 1981). Following those decisions, the governmental plaintiffs' funding and representation injuries suffice to reach the merits, and the claim is not barred as nonjusticiable. The record before the court does not establish the standing of every other plaintiff for every form of relief; those questions are decided on the record made below, plaintiff by plaintiff and use by use, and are not decided here.

The constitutional claim must be kept apart from any claim under the Administrative Procedure Act. *Franklin v. Massachusetts*, 505 U.S. 788, 800-06 (1992), holds that the President is not an agency within the Act and that the Secretary's apportionment report is not the President's final apportionment decision. A court therefore may not use the Act to direct the President. *Franklin* does not, however, dispose of a constitutional challenge to the responsible officials' census decision, which does not depend on an APA route. Whether the Secretary's decision is final agency action reviewable under the Act as to any census use other than apportionment requires its own analysis; that question is not before the court on this record and is not decided.

The governing constitutional standard is reviewed de novo, and the district court's findings are reviewed for clear error. The Secretary's good-faith technical judgments receive respect, and a court of appeals does not find facts, weigh experts, or select the better estimator. How far administrative-law deference defines the constitutional inquiry is the question addressed in Part III.

### Part III.A. What the claim does not establish (Judges Kearse, Stone, and Timbers)

Differential undercount among racial and ethnic groups is shown by the Bureau's estimates. That disparity does not by itself establish discriminatory purpose, and unequal effects alone do not call for racial strict scrutiny. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976); *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-68 (1977). The Secretary disclaimed any belief that the survey was manipulated, and nothing in the record permits an inference of racial or partisan hostility from the fact that particular States would gain or lose seats. This court makes no such finding. It does not hold that national numerical accuracy is invariably superior to distributive accuracy, or the reverse. It does not direct the use of the PES, adopt the plaintiffs' statistics, or count expert opinions. Mathematical perfection and identical accuracy in every locality are impossible, and the Constitution does not demand them.

### Part III.B. The constitutional inquiry the district court must make (Judges Kearse and Stone; Judge Timbers dissents)

**Rule.** Where plaintiffs claim that the Secretary's refusal to adjust a census count impaired equal representation, a determination that the refusal was not arbitrary or capricious under administrative-law review does not dispose of the claim. The district court must state the governing constitutional standard and apply it independently to the reasons the Secretary actually gave. At a minimum the Secretary's choice must rest on reasons that are legitimate, genuinely held, supported by the record, and directed to the constitutional task of measuring the population for purposes of representation rather than displacing that task. The standard is a question of law reviewed de novo. The findings the district court has made remain findings and stand unless clearly erroneous.

**Authority.** The Constitution makes population the measure of representation. Article I, § 2 provides for an "actual Enumeration" made "in such Manner as they shall by Law direct," and the Fourteenth Amendment, § 2, apportions Representatives "according to their respective numbers, counting the whole number of persons in each State." Equal representation has been enforced against the drawing of districts. *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964); *Kirkpatrick v. Preisler*, 394 U.S. 526 (1969); *Karcher v. Daggett*, 462 U.S. 725, 730-31, 740 (1983). Those decisions concern the use of accepted population data and do not decide how the federal government must choose between disputed nationwide estimates of the population. This opinion therefore does not carry the burden structure of *Karcher* into the enumeration, and whether and in what form it applies is left open. But *Department of Commerce v. Montana*, 503 U.S. 442, 459-66 (1992), which recognizes both the constitutional constraints on apportionment and its unavoidable compromises, and *Franklin*, which tested a census judgment against the constitutional language and the goal of equal representation, show that a census judgment is measured against the Constitution and not merely against administrative regularity. Neither decision gives the Secretary unreviewable discretion. The arbitrary-and-capricious standard, 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A), asks whether an agency acted rationally within the discretion Congress conferred. It does not define what the Constitution requires of a census decision. Nor does *Carey* hold otherwise. It spoke of review for reasonableness in deciding that census methods could be reviewed at all, and it did not decide what the Constitution demands of the Secretary's choice among competing nationwide estimates after a full survey.

**Application.** The district court framed the dispositive question as whether the Secretary's decision was arbitrary or capricious. It found the eight guidelines rationally chosen and applied, regarded the focus on distributive accuracy as compatible with the census's representative function, and treated disagreement over the better exercise of discretion as insufficient. Those conclusions answer a question about administrative rationality. The opinion below does not state a constitutional standard and apply it to the reasons the Secretary actually gave. The same court found that adjustment yields a more accurate count for most purposes and that the plaintiffs mounted a compelling attack on several aspects of the decision. Those findings cannot be set aside as immaterial because the Secretary has discretion, and the opinion does not explain whether, taken with the court's other findings, they show anything more than disagreement about the wiser exercise of discretion once the decision is measured against the representational aim of the census.

**The principal contrary argument.** The Secretary, the intervenors, and the dissent contend that choosing among imperfect methods is a matter of technical judgment, that the advisory panel divided evenly, that the experts disagreed, that numerical and distributive accuracy are different things, and that the district court, after thirteen days of trial, found the guidelines rational. The court accepts that genuine technical uncertainty and the difference between numerical and distributive accuracy are real. It does not hold that adjustment was required or that the Secretary's choice was unlawful, and it does not weigh the experts. The vacatur rests on the absence of the constitutional determination, not on a view that adjustment would have been wiser.

A second objection is that this inquiry only renames stricter administrative review. It does not. Administrative review asks whether the decision is within the bounds of rational agency action. The constitutional inquiry asks whether the choice, as the Secretary actually made it, serves the constitutional task of measuring population for representation. It directs attention to the Secretary's real reasons and does not authorize a court to select or prescribe a statistical method.

**Direction on remand.** The district court should determine whether the demand that adjustment improve accuracy at every geographic level was an actual decision criterion and, if so, whether the record supports it as a technical matter; whether the competing statistical analyses support the stated rationale of distributive accuracy; whether the weight given to speculative risks to future censuses, including the possibility of manipulation that the Secretary did not believe had occurred in this survey, served the present constitutional task rather than displaced it; and whether the court's findings establish more than disagreement over the wisdom of the choice.

**Matters not decided.** The court does not decide how much justification the Constitution requires, or which party bears the burden of persuasion on the constitutional question. Judge Kearse and Judge Stone differ on both points (see Part III.C and the statement of Judge Stone's vote below). The district court should state its findings on each of the matters above so that a reviewing court can apply whatever measure of justification is held to govern.

### Part III.C. [Judge Kearse's own view; not the opinion of the court]

Part III.C of Judge Kearse's opinion is joined by no other judge. It is set out under "Separate writings" below.

### Part IV. The statutes (Judges Kearse and Timbers; Judge Stone does not join)

**Rule.** Sections 141(a) and 195 of Title 13 do not command the Secretary to adopt adjusted figures for any census use. The question whether the exception in § 195 forbids the use of adjusted figures for congressional apportionment is not decided.

**Authority and reasoning.** Section 141(a) directs the Secretary to take the decennial census "in such form and content as he may determine, including the use of sampling procedures and special surveys." That language, added in 1976, authorizes sampling and surveys and commits the form and content of the census to the Secretary's determination. It grants authority; it commands nothing. Section 195 provides that, "[e]xcept for the determination of population for purposes of apportionment of Representatives in Congress among the several States, the Secretary shall, if he considers it feasible, authorize the use of the statistical method known as 'sampling' in carrying out the provisions of this title." The direction concerns the authorization of sampling in carrying out the title, and it is conditioned on the Secretary's own consideration of feasibility. For apportionment it is withdrawn. Neither provision requires the Secretary to adopt a particular set of adjusted figures. The 1976 amendments reflect a purpose to permit broader use of sampling, but a broad purpose does not turn a grant of authority into a mandate. The 1989 stipulation required a fresh consideration of adjustment and an explained decision; it could not enlarge the Secretary's statutory authority or this court's remedial power.

**Application and the contrary argument.** The plaintiffs read the apportionment exception as limiting a mandate, not as prohibiting supplementation that § 141(a) permits. The intervenors read it as preserving a special restriction on apportionment. The court need not choose. Even on the plaintiffs' reading, the statutes permit the Secretary to supplement the enumeration; they do not require him to do so. A claim to an order compelling adjustment on a statutory ground therefore fails. The constitutional claim remains for the district court. Judge Stone does not join this Part.

### Part V. Remand and limits on relief

**Part V.A. Limits on relief (Judges Kearse, Stone, and Timbers).** A defect in the reasoning of a census decision does not by itself authorize a court to reallocate seats or to disregard § 195. Each census use and each form of relief requires its own legal basis. A remedy requires an identified violation, a claimant with a record-proven injury, a lawful defendant, and an administrable correction. *Franklin* forecloses an order against the President under the Administrative Procedure Act. This court may not select adjusted totals or find facts. An adjustment whose effects would shift seats or funds among jurisdictions cannot be shaped without regard to the interests of States and localities that are not before the court. *Carey v. Klutznick*, 653 F.2d 732 (2d Cir. 1981) (other States have interests affected by an adjustment limited to some jurisdictions, and alternatives such as notice to the affected States should be considered). No immediate reapportionment is ordered.

**Part V.B. Remand (Judges Kearse and Stone; Judge Timbers dissents).** The judgment of dismissal is vacated and the case is remanded to the district court. The district court is to decide the constitutional question first, under Part III.B and on its existing findings, which remain findings, and in the manner it considers appropriate. This court directs no new census, no adoption of the plaintiffs' statistics, and no predetermined adjustment. Only if the district court finds a constitutional defect may it reach the questions of standing of other claimants and for particular relief, the proper defendants, the finality of the Secretary's decision as to uses other than apportionment, the application of §§ 141 and 195 to each affected use, and the limits stated in Part V.A. The district court is to address each of those questions before ordering any particular correction.

## Separate writings

### Part III.C of Judge Kearse's opinion: the measure of justification (hers alone; not the opinion of the court)

Author: Judge Kearse. Joins: none; no other judge joins this Part. Judge Stone does not join it, and Judge Timbers rejects it. It is not the holding of the court and is not circuit law.

Judge Kearse would hold that the Article I, § 2 claim requires a more searching justification than ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review. Where the Bureau's own estimates, together with the district court's finding that adjustment yields a more accurate count for most purposes, show a known undercount that falls unequally on identifiable groups and places, the government must justify its refusal to correct it as essential to a legitimate governmental objective. A finding that the Secretary's choices were rational is therefore not sufficient.

She would hold that this requirement applies to enumeration, and not only to districting, because the Constitution makes the enumeration the instrument by which equal representation is carried out. If the census figures from which districts are drawn could be left knowingly skewed without a comparable justification, the requirement that districting variances be shown necessary to achieve some legitimate goal, *Karcher*, 462 U.S. at 730-31, would be hollow. *Montana* and *Franklin* allow for the unavoidable compromises of apportionment, but they treat those compromises as bounded by the constitutional task and not as discretion without a constitutional measure.

She would apply the test of legitimate, genuinely held, record-supported reasons that engage the constitutional task at this higher level, and she would send its application to the district court. She would make no appellate finding that the Secretary's reasons fail it, would not choose an estimator, and would not infer racial purpose from disparity.

### Dissenting opinion of Judge Timbers

Author: Judge Timbers. Dissents from the judgment and from Parts III.B, III.C, and V.B of Judge Kearse's opinion. No other member of the panel is stated to join the dissent.

Judge Timbers would affirm. He agrees that the constitutional standard is a question of law reviewed de novo and that the district court's findings are reviewed for clear error. In his view the Secretary's good-faith technical judgment is entitled to substantial but reviewable deference. Distributive accuracy, uncertainty in the local estimates, and an advisory panel that divided evenly sustain the decision, and the district court's finding, after a thirteen-day trial, that the Secretary's guidelines were rational is not clearly erroneous. A court of appeals may not choose the better estimator.

He would not import into the enumeration the burden of justification that applies to the drawing of districts, and he would not infer racial purpose from the disparity in undercount. He agrees with Part IV that the statutes contain no command to adjust, and he would not decide whether § 195 forbids adjustment for apportionment.

Had he found a defect, he would have remanded to the Secretary rather than allow judicial substitution of figures, and he would have confined any relief to an identified census use, a lawful defendant, and a claimant with proven injury, with no order against the President and with attention to the absent States and localities.

### Judge Stone's vote (no separate opinion is filed)

Judge Stone files no separate opinion. He joins the judgment and Parts I, II, III.A, III.B, V.A, and V.B of Judge Kearse's opinion. He does not join Part III.C or Part IV.

His vote to vacate rests on the proposition in Part III.B: the constitutional review of the census cannot end with the conclusion that the Secretary's decision survives ordinary administrative review, and the district court must determine whether the choice reflects a good-faith performance of the representative task, respecting genuine technical uncertainty and the difference between numerical and distributive accuracy. He would keep on the plaintiffs the burden of establishing a constitutional defect, would not add a least-restrictive-means requirement or an across-the-board burden of persuasion on the government, and would not treat unequal undercount alone as automatically shifting any burden. For those reasons he does not join Part III.C. He would reserve the construction of §§ 141 and 195 beyond what the remand requires, and for that reason he does not join Part IV.

Judge Stone's stated grounds, given without a separate opinion, are not circuit law.

## What the decision does

- **Controlling holding.** This is a published opinion of a three-judge panel, and a proposition is the holding of the court only if at least two members of the panel join it. The holdings of the court are as follows.
  - Part II (threshold, justiciability, reach of the merits, the line between the constitutional claim and the APA, and the standards of review), joined by all three judges.
  - Part III.A (what the claim does not establish), joined by all three judges.
  - Part III.B, joined by Judges Kearse and Stone: where plaintiffs claim that the Secretary's refusal to adjust a census count impaired equal representation, a determination that the refusal was not arbitrary or capricious under administrative-law review does not dispose of the claim; the district court must state the governing constitutional standard and apply it independently to the reasons the Secretary actually gave; and at a minimum the choice must rest on reasons that are legitimate, genuinely held, supported by the record, and directed to the constitutional task of measuring the population for purposes of representation rather than displacing that task.
  - Part IV, joined by Judges Kearse and Timbers: 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195 do not command the Secretary to adopt adjusted figures for any census use. Section 141(a) grants authority and commands nothing, and § 195 directs the authorization of sampling only if the Secretary considers it feasible and withdraws that direction for apportionment. Whether the exception in § 195 forbids the use of adjusted figures for congressional apportionment is not decided.
  - Part V.A (limits on relief), joined by all three judges.
- **The ground for the judgment.** The judgment vacating the dismissal rests on two votes, and the ground they share is the narrow one stated in Part III.B. Where the judges who vote for a judgment rest on different measures of the justification required, the holding is limited to the ground they share. Cf. *Marks v. United States*, 430 U.S. 188 (1977). The stricter formulation in Part III.C has one vote and is not circuit law. The dissent is not circuit law. Judge Stone's stated grounds, given without a separate opinion, are not circuit law. Part I is background and is not a holding.
- **Treatment of precedent.** The court follows *Carey v. Klutznick*, 637 F.2d 834 (2d Cir. 1980) (per curiam), and *Carey v. Klutznick*, 653 F.2d 732 (2d Cir. 1981), on standing, justiciability, and the interests of absent States. It applies *Franklin v. Massachusetts* to the limits on relief against the President. It applies *Washington v. Davis* and *Arlington Heights* to the inference of racial purpose. It treats *Wesberry*, *Kirkpatrick*, and *Karcher* as districting decisions that it does not extend to the enumeration, and it treats *Montana* and *Franklin* as showing that a census judgment is measured against the Constitution. The district court's opinion at 822 F. Supp. 906 states record rulings and is not circuit precedent. The court adopts no change to the Supreme Court's law and does not overrule any decision of this court.
- **Law of the case and party-specific relief.** The holdings of the court bind the district court on remand. The vacatur runs to the parties to this judgment, and nothing in it grants any plaintiff an adjustment or any other form of relief. The district court's existing findings of fact are not disturbed.
- **Matters left open.** The measure of justification required of the Secretary and the allocation of the burden of persuasion on the constitutional claim; whether and in what form the burden structure of *Karcher* applies to the enumeration; whether § 195's exception forbids the use of adjusted figures for congressional apportionment; whether the Secretary's decision is final agency action reviewable under the Administrative Procedure Act as to any use other than apportionment; the standing of plaintiffs other than the governmental plaintiffs and standing for any particular form of relief; and any remedy.
- **Effect on the parties.** The judgment of the district court dismissing the complaint is vacated. On remand the district court is to state and apply the constitutional standard to the Secretary's actual reasons, as described in Part III.B, on its existing findings and in the manner it considers appropriate. It may reach the questions of standing for particular relief, proper defendants, the application of 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a) and 195 to each affected use, and the limits in Part V.A only if it finds a constitutional defect. This court orders no adjusted figures, no new census, no reapportionment or reallocation, and no relief against the President. No plaintiff is granted an adjustment, and the question whether any relief is warranted remains for the district court.
- **Mandate and remand.** The case is remanded to the United States District Court for the Eastern District of New York for further proceedings consistent with this opinion. The mandate has not issued. Under the rules in force on the decision date, a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment unless the court shortens or enlarges the time by order or by local rule, Fed. R. App. P. 40(a), and the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the court shortens or enlarges the time by order, Fed. R. App. P. 41(a). If a timely petition for rehearing is filed, the mandate issues 7 days after entry of the order denying the petition, unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order. Fed. R. App. P. 41(a). The period runs from the entry of judgment, and the date of entry is not stated in the materials before the court. The date of this opinion is not treated as the date of entry of judgment or as the mandate date. No petition for rehearing and no other filing is described in the materials before the court.
