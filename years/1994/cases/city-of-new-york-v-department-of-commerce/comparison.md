## Departure from history

The disposition and lineup match history (dismissal vacated and remanded, Judge Kearse writing, Judge Timbers dissenting), with Judge Stone taking Judge Leval's place as the second majority vote. The reasoning differs: the historical majority required the government to justify non-adjustment as essential to a legitimate governmental objective, but in the simulation that test is Judge Kearse's view alone, and the court's holding is only that the district court must state and apply a constitutional standard to the Secretary's actual reasons, with Judge Stone not joining Parts III.C and IV. The remand also differs in scope, because it leaves the measure of justification and the burden of persuasion open for the district court instead of directing the heightened framework.

## Historical comparison extract

On 1994-08-08 a Second Circuit panel of Judges Timbers, Kearse and Leval vacated the dismissal of the census challenge and remanded. Judge Kearse wrote the majority opinion, joined by Judge Leval; Judge Timbers dissented and would have affirmed. The historical majority required the government to justify its refusal to correct a known differential undercount as essential to a legitimate governmental objective, rather than relying on ordinary arbitrary-and-capricious review, and left the district court to apply that framework. The simulated judgment is the same: vacatur and remand, 2-1, Kearse writing, Timbers dissenting, with Judge Stone in Judge Leval's seat. The change is in the reasoning. In the simulation only Judges Kearse and Stone hold that arbitrary-and-capricious review does not end the constitutional inquiry and that the district court must apply a constitutional standard to the Secretary's actual reasons. The heightened "essential to a legitimate objective" test is Judge Kearse's alone, so the measure of justification and the burden stay open on remand. Source: City of New York v. U.S. Department of Commerce, 34 F.3d 1114, 1124-33 (2d Cir. 1994).
