## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Constitutional review of census nonadjustment

**Jurisdiction and reach:** The appeal challenges the Secretary’s census decision and the district court’s constitutional treatment after a bench trial. It does not establish racial purpose merely from unequal undercount. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Propose a limited vacatur and remand, without a predetermined adjustment or automatic heightened scrutiny.

**Governing law:** Independent review of the governing constitutional standard; historical fact findings for clear error; the level of deference to the Secretary’s good-faith census judgment must be identified rather than assumed. Constitutional representation requirements constrain the census, but mathematical perfection and identical accuracy in every locality are impossible. Determine whether the government’s actual reasons constitute a permissible good-faith judgment; do not import a redistricting burden shift without establishing that its rationale governs enumeration.

Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), and Karcher v. Daggett, 462 U.S. 725 (1983), concern representational equality in their settings. Department of Commerce v. Montana, 503 U.S. 442, 459-66 (1992), recognizes constitutional constraints and unavoidable apportionment choices. Franklin v. Massachusetts, 505 U.S. 788 (1992), addresses census-related review and federal remedial limits. Washington v. Davis, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), and Arlington Heights, 429 U.S. 252 (1977), require the appropriate purpose showing for a racial constitutional claim. U.S. Const. art. I, § 2 and amend. XIV, § 2; the 1989 stipulation and the Secretary’s 1991 decision; the district court’s findings at 822 F. Supp. 906 are record rulings, not circuit precedent.

**Legal relationship:** Resolving an open question; distinguishing apportionment and purpose precedents.

**Proposed holding:** The court must state and apply the constitutional standard to the government’s actual good-faith census choice; unequal undercount alone does not establish discriminatory purpose or automatically shift the burden.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose a limited vacatur and remand for a distinct constitutional determination, without adopting automatic heightened racial scrutiny or directing use of the PES. The proposed holding is that review of the census's constitutional function cannot end solely with the conclusion that the Secretary's decision survives ordinary administrative review. The court must determine whether the choice reflects a good-faith performance of the representative task, while respecting genuine technical uncertainty and the difference between numerical and distributive accuracy. This application to the present census-choice problem is a proposed circuit development, not a quotation of a Supreme Court census-adjustment rule.

The remand should identify the unresolved work precisely. The district court should assess whether the requirement of improved accuracy at every geographic level was an actual, technically supportable decision criterion; whether the competing loss-function analyses support the stated distributive rationale; and whether the treatment of future manipulation and cooperation concerns rationally served the present constitutional task rather than displacing it. Its existing findings remain findings. The court need not conduct a new census, adopt plaintiffs' statistics, count expert votes, or infer partisan intent from the mere fact that particular states would gain or lose seats.

The strongest objection is that this simply renames a demand for stricter administrative review. Stone should answer it by limiting the inquiry to the constitutional objective and declining to add a least-restrictive-means requirement, an across-the-board government burden of persuasion, or a mandatory methodological checklist. Plaintiffs retain the burden of establishing a constitutional defect. The Secretary may prevail despite a method that is less accurate for some purposes if the record supports a good-faith choice among genuinely competing constitutional and statistical considerations. A court's belief that adjustment would be wiser is insufficient.

At the same time, the finding that plaintiffs made a compelling attack cannot be treated as immaterial solely because the Secretary possesses discretion. The district court should explain whether its findings establish more than disagreement over wisdom under the independent constitutional standard. Because it framed the dispositive inquiry as whether the decision was sufficiently unreasonable to be arbitrary or capricious, a targeted remand is preferable to an appellate finding of bad faith or an appellate endorsement of every rationale.

**Independent or alternative considerations:** No additional rule is proposed beyond the interpretation and application stated here.

**Preferred scope:** The court must state and apply the constitutional standard to the government’s actual good-faith census choice; unequal undercount alone does not establish discriminatory purpose or automatically shift the burden. The lawful outer boundary is the issue and remedial limit stated here, not a broader rule about unpresented facts.

Case-level proposed scope: independent constitutional review of the government's good-faith census judgment, with a focused remand on the actual accuracy rationale and no predetermined adjustment. He may explain why an unsupported demand for superiority everywhere would not automatically answer demonstrated improvements affecting most people.

**Fallback position:** Proposed, not approved: remand requiring explicit findings and conclusions under *Montana* and *Franklin*, reserving a more comprehensive census standard and every disputed statistical determination. Affirmance would become acceptable if the necessary findings and correct constitutional analysis establish a lawful choice; it is not presently an approved fallback based merely on administrative deference.

Case-level nonjoin limits, also proposed: automatic strict scrutiny from minority undercount, conclusive constitutional preference for numerical over distributive accuracy, an assumption that the Secretary acted from racial or partisan hostility, disregard of § 195, or an immediate judicial order reallocating congressional seats without the necessary statutory and remedial analysis.

**Remedy and remand:** Require a focused constitutional determination addressing the actual accuracy rationale and the findings already made, preserving legitimate technical choices and plaintiffs’ applicable burden.

**Reserved questions:** No finding of partisan or racial hostility; no constitutional hierarchy making national numerical accuracy invariably superior to distributive accuracy.

**Separate writing:** Proposed only. A separate opinion would defend or delimit this proposition: The court must state and apply the constitutional standard to the government’s actual good-faith census choice; unequal undercount alone does not establish discriminatory purpose or automatically shift the burden. It would explain why the authorities and record support the disposition stated above and answer the contrary position addressed in the legal reasoning. ### Component 2

The packet does not establish that every plaintiff has standing for every form of relief. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Reserve an immediate adjustment order and require the statutory and remedial questions to be resolved before any particular correction is ordered.

**Governing law:** Independent statutory interpretation and jurisdictional review; equitable choices only within an established remedial power. Section 141(a) contemplates sampling and surveys, while § 195’s apportionment exception raises a separate limitation. Distinguish apportionment, other representative uses, and funding before prescribing relief; presidential review and redressability follow Franklin.

Franklin v. Massachusetts, 505 U.S. 788 (1992): the President is not an APA agency and relief involving presidential action requires separate analysis; Montana does not eliminate statutory limits. 13 U.S.C. §§ 141(a), 195; federal-question and equitable remedial sources as properly established; the 1989 stipulation cannot enlarge statutory or constitutional power.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** An identified defect in census reasoning does not itself authorize judicial reallocation of seats or disregard of § 195; each use and remedy requires its own legal basis.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should reserve the disputed statutory scope unless a correction is otherwise warranted. Before ordering any particular adjustment, the district court must address §§ 141 and 195 for each affected use and the remedial implications of *Franklin*. This preserves his commitments to equal political membership, genuine review, statutory limits, and no conversion of disparate impact into discriminatory purpose.

Accuracy is an important factual consideration but does not replace statutory authorization. Franklin also prevents treating the existence of an APA-style objection as sufficient authority to command the President.
