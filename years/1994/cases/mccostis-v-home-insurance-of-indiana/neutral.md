## Record and issues

**Material record:** McCostis was a law-firm partner and outside general counsel to Barr Laboratories, also serving as a director and secretary. After Barr became a client of Winston & Strawn, McCostis allegedly participated with Winston partner Lawrence Fox in a scheme that overbilled Barr by $756,251.75. Approximately $335,000 allegedly went to McCostis; more than $420,000 allegedly went to Fox and his designees. Winston reimbursed Barr and took an assignment of its claims.

Winston's twelve-count complaint against McCostis included federal racketeering, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, and negligent breach theories. It sought liability extending beyond the sum McCostis allegedly received. Home's professional-liability policy promised a defense against covered claims even when allegations were groundless, false, or fraudulent. Its definition of damages included monetary judgments and settlements but excluded fines, statutory penalties, sanctions, and the return or restitution of legal fees, costs, and expenses.

Home initially undertook the defense and then withdrew. McCostis sought declaratory relief. The district court granted Home summary judgment, reading the fee-return exclusion as encompassing the underlying action. The appellate dispute is whether the complaint necessarily seeks excluded restitution, or potentially seeks covered compensation, particularly for money allegedly received by someone else. *McCostis*, 31 F.3d at 111-12.

### Issue 1: Fee-return exclusion and potential duty to defend

**Preservation and reach:** The coverage dispute was decided on summary judgment. The pleaded overbilling is allegation, not an adjudicated admission by McCostis; indemnity and unbriefed estoppel theories are not automatically part of this appeal.

**Standard of review:** De novo review of summary judgment and policy ambiguity, with factual disputes and permissible inferences for the nonmovant.

**Governing test and burdens:** A defense is owed where the complaint potentially falls within coverage. Construe exclusions narrowly without rewriting clear text. If the fee-return language is ambiguous, examine relevant extrinsic evidence before applying construction against the insurer where uncertainty remains.

New York law gives the defense promise broad effect. *Colon v. Aetna Life & Casualty Insurance Co.*, 66 N.Y.2d 6, 8-9 (1985), and *Villa Charlotte Bronte, Inc. v. Commercial Union Insurance Co.*, 64 N.Y.2d 846, 848 (1985), require a defense where the complaint potentially brings the claim within coverage. Exclusions are narrowly construed. *Breed v. Insurance Co. of North America*, 46 N.Y.2d 351, 353 (1978). These rules do not create coverage contrary to unambiguous text, but they prevent an insurer from treating every contested allegation as an established excluded loss.

Home's best argument is economic substance. Winston reimbursed the client and sued on assigned rights for improperly collected professional charges. Recasting that recovery as RICO damages, negligence, or joint liability should not convert fee restitution into insured damages. The exclusion would have little practical work if an attorney could obtain coverage simply by attaching a tort label to a demand to refund the client's bill.

McCostis's strongest answer concerns the actual words and amounts. A return or restitution ordinarily suggests restoring what the defendant received or retained. Winston sought substantially more than the money allegedly paid to McCostis, including sums allegedly retained by Fox and others. Liability for another participant's receipt can plausibly be compensatory liability rather than the return of the insured's own fees. Home's interpretation may be sensible, but it is not the only sensible interpretation of the chosen language.

New York's sequence matters. Under *State v. Home Indemnity Co.*, 66 N.Y.2d 669, 671 (1985), an ambiguity is first examined through relevant extrinsic evidence. If that evidence fails to resolve the uncertainty, construction against the insurer becomes appropriate. *Matthews v. American Central Insurance Co.*, 154 N.Y. 449, 456-57 (1897), supplies the older contractual principle; *Ingersoll Milling Machine Co. v. M/V Bodena*, 829 F.2d 293, 306 (2d Cir. 1987), applies that sequence in federal court. Neither side is entitled to bypass a genuinely material intent inquiry merely because it favors an immediate legal conclusion.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** No directly controlling Supreme Court holding interprets this New York policy. Erie’s state-substantive-law obligation supplies the federal/state allocation, not a replacement coverage rule.

**Other governing authority:** Colon v. Aetna Life & Casualty Insurance Co., 66 N.Y.2d 6, 8-9 (1985); Villa Charlotte Bronte, Inc. v. Commercial Union Insurance Co., 64 N.Y.2d 846, 848 (1985); Breed v. Insurance Co. of North America, 46 N.Y.2d 351, 353 (1978); State v. Home Indemnity Co., 66 N.Y.2d 669, 671 (1985), binding New York authority. Matthews v. American Central Insurance Co., 154 N.Y. 449, 456-57 (1897); Ingersoll Milling Machine Co. v. M/V Bodena, 829 F.2d 293, 306 (2d Cir. 1987).

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** All demanded sums stem from improperly collected fees repaid by Winston, and relabeling restitution as RICO, fiduciary, or negligence liability should not defeat the exclusion.
