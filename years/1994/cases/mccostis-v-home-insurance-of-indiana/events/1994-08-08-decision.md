### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** McCostis appeals from a summary judgment declaring that Home owes him no duty to defend an action brought against him by Winston & Strawn. The district court entered that judgment for Home after denying McCostis's own motion for summary judgment and receiving further submissions, reading the policy's fee-return exclusion as encompassing the underlying action. The materials before us do not state the date on which judgment was entered. McCostis asks for a declaration that Home must defend or, alternatively, vacatur and further proceedings. Home asks for affirmance.

**Material record.** McCostis was a law-firm partner and outside general counsel to Barr Laboratories, and also served Barr as a director and secretary. After Barr became a client of Winston & Strawn, McCostis allegedly took part with Winston partner Lawrence Fox in a scheme that overbilled Barr by $756,251.75. Approximately $335,000 allegedly went to McCostis, and more than $420,000 allegedly went to Fox and his designees. Winston reimbursed Barr and took an assignment of Barr's claims. Winston's twelve-count complaint against McCostis pleads federal racketeering, fraud, breach of fiduciary duty and negligent breach theories and seeks liability extending beyond the sum McCostis allegedly received. Home's professional-liability policy promises a defense against covered claims even when the allegations are groundless, false or fraudulent. Its definition of damages includes monetary judgments and settlements and excludes fines, statutory penalties, sanctions, and the return or restitution of legal fees, costs and expenses (the fee-return exclusion). Home first undertook McCostis's defense and then withdrew. McCostis then sought declaratory relief. These facts are the allegations of the Winston complaint and the record of the coverage action. The overbilling is not an adjudicated fact.

**Jurisdiction.** The declaratory judgment is a final decision appealable under 28 U.S.C. 1291. The district court's own jurisdiction, which rests apparently on diversity of citizenship, is addressed in Part I of the opinion.

**Issues and standards.** (1) Whether federal subject-matter jurisdiction is established, a matter the court must examine on its own. (2) Whether the fee-return exclusion necessarily eliminates any potential duty to defend liabilities exceeding the fees McCostis allegedly received. Review of the summary judgment and of whether the policy is ambiguous is de novo, with the material factual inferences drawn for McCostis as the nonmovant. New York law governs the policy. Home bears the burden as the summary-judgment movant and, as the insurer invoking an exclusion, must show that the complaint falls solely and entirely within it and that it admits of no other reasonable reading.

**Preservation and reach.** Indemnity, waiver or estoppel from Home's initial defense and later withdrawal, and policy terms other than the fee-return exclusion are not part of this appeal.

**Dates.** Argued May 2, 1994; decided August 8, 1994; date of entry of the district court's judgment not stated in the materials before us.

### Judgment

The judgment of the district court is VACATED and the cause is REMANDED, by a vote of three to none. Judges Pratt, Walker and Stone-Khoury join every component. No judge dissents or concurs separately.

1. Summary judgment declaring that Home owes no duty to defend: vacated. For: Pratt, Walker, Stone-Khoury. Against: none.
2. Remand for an inquiry into admissible extrinsic evidence of the parties' intended meaning and for application of the New York sequence: for: Pratt, Walker, Stone-Khoury. Against: none.

4. No declaration for either party now, and no ruling on indemnity, allocation, the truth of the allegations, waiver or estoppel: for: Pratt, Walker, Stone-Khoury. Against: none.

#### Opinion of the Court

WALKER, Circuit Judge, for the court:

Richard M. McCostis, a lawyer, appeals from a summary judgment declaring that his professional-liability insurer, Home Insurance Company of Indiana, owes him no defense against a twelve-count complaint brought by Winston & Strawn. The district court read a policy exclusion for the return or restitution of legal fees, costs and expenses as reaching the whole of that action. We hold that, as applied to liability measured by sums that others allegedly received, the exclusion is reasonably open to more than one reading, that Home did not show its reading to be the only reasonable one, and that New York law calls for an inquiry into the parties' intended meaning before any construction against the insurer. We vacate and remand, and we direct the district court to resolve a threshold question of diversity jurisdiction.

**I. Jurisdiction**

We must satisfy ourselves of the jurisdiction of the district court as well as our own, whether or not the parties contest it. Bender v. Williamsport Area School District, 475 U.S. 534, 541 (1986). The Declaratory Judgment Act, 28 U.S.C. 2201(a), supplies a remedy and not a grant of jurisdiction. Skelly Oil Co. v. Phillips Petroleum Co., 339 U.S. 667, 671-72 (1950). The federal racketeering claims that Winston asserts against McCostis in a separate action do not make his suit against Home, which turns on the meaning of an insurance contract, one arising under federal law. The apparent basis of jurisdiction is diversity of citizenship, 28 U.S.C. 1332(a), which is assessed as of the time the action is filed. Freeport-McMoRan, Inc. v. K N Energy, Inc., 498 U.S. 426, 428 (1991) (per curiam). The materials before us do not set out the citizenship of the parties, and the name of the insurer does not establish it. See 28 U.S.C. 1332(c)(1) (citizenship of a corporation).

Section 1653 allows defective allegations of jurisdiction to be amended in the trial or appellate courts. It reaches, however, only incorrect statements about jurisdiction that actually exists, and not defects in the jurisdictional facts themselves. Newman-Green, Inc. v. Alfonzo-Larrain, 490 U.S. 826, 831 (1989). We will not resolve that distinction on this incomplete account. The judgment must be set aside in any event: on the grounds given in Part II if jurisdiction exists, and for want of jurisdiction if it does not. Before any further proceedings on the merits, the district court shall determine whether the parties were of diverse citizenship when the action was filed and shall permit any defective allegation to be amended under section 1653. If diversity existed, the proceedings go forward as set out below. If it did not, the action must be dismissed. Part II states the governing New York law on the footing that jurisdiction exists.

**II. The fee-return exclusion and the duty to defend**

*A. Governing principles.* New York law governs the policy. Erie R.R. Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64, 78 (1938). We review a summary judgment de novo. Summary judgment is proper only if no genuine issue of material fact remains and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(c), and the nonmovant's evidence is to be believed and all justifiable inferences drawn in his favor. Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc., 477 U.S. 242, 255 (1986). Whether policy language is ambiguous is a question of law that we also decide de novo.

An insurer's duty to defend is broader than its duty to indemnify. Seaboard Surety Co. v. Gillette Co., 64 N.Y.2d 304, 310 (1984). The insurer must defend when the allegations of the complaint, liberally read, fall within the risk insured, however groundless, false or baseless the suit may be. Colon v. Aetna Life & Casualty Insurance Co., 66 N.Y.2d 6, 8-9 (1985). It is relieved of that duty only if there is no possible factual or legal basis on which it might eventually be obligated to indemnify. Villa Charlotte Bronte, Inc. v. Commercial Union Insurance Co., 64 N.Y.2d 846, 848 (1985). An insurer that relies on an exclusion must show that the allegations of the complaint fall solely and entirely within it and that the exclusion is subject to no other reasonable interpretation. Seaboard, 64 N.Y.2d at 311-12. Exclusions are read narrowly, and an ambiguity in the policy is construed against the insurer that drafted it, particularly in an exclusionary clause. Breed v. Insurance Co. of North America, 46 N.Y.2d 351, 353 (1978); Matthews v. American Central Insurance Co., 154 N.Y. 449, 456-57 (1897). None of this permits a court to create coverage where the language is plain. Breed itself enforced the exclusion before it because its words were clear. We do not rewrite what is clear, and we do not treat every contested allegation as an established excluded loss.

*B. The text and the demand.* The exclusion removes from the policy's definition of damages the return or restitution of legal fees, costs and expenses. The question it presents can be put concretely. Does it exclude every compensatory judgment traceable to an improperly billed fee, or only the insured's restoration of the fees and expenses he received? Both answers can be explained from the language.

In ordinary usage, return and restitution describe the giving back of what the person ordered to give it back received or holds. On that reading the exclusion denies coverage when McCostis is made to restore the fees he himself is alleged to have taken, which on Winston's allegations came to about $335,000 of the $756,251.75 overbilled. Winston's demand is not so limited. It seeks liability beyond the sum McCostis allegedly received, including the more than $420,000 that Fox and his designees allegedly kept. A judgment making one participant answer for money another retained does not return anything the first participant holds. It can fairly be described as compensation for a loss, and the policy's definition of damages includes monetary judgments and settlements.

Home's reading is not unreasonable. Winston repaid Barr and sues on Barr's assigned claims to recover improperly collected charges, so every dollar of its demand traces to a fee. On Home's view the exclusion looks to the nature of the money and not to who now holds it, and it would do little work if a lawyer could obtain coverage for the refund of a client's bill by attaching a tort label to the demand. That is a sensible account of what an insurer might intend, but a sensible reading is not necessarily the only one. The exclusion speaks of return and restitution, words naturally read in terms of what the insured received, and it does not say in terms that it reaches every compensatory recovery traceable to a fee. As applied to a demand measured by sums that others allegedly received, it is reasonably susceptible of both readings. That is an ambiguity. It is not a license to insure dishonest profit. It concerns the scope of the insurer's promise to defend against a complaint alleging several kinds of liability, not whether a policyholder may keep, or be indemnified for, what he took.

*C. Home's principal argument.* Home answers that artful pleading cannot create coverage and that recasting a demand for refunded fees as racketeering, fiduciary or negligence liability should not convert fee restitution into insured damages. As a statement of New York law that is correct, because the duty to defend turns on the facts alleged and the words of the contract, and a label alters neither. But the point is not enough to sustain Home's judgment, because the potential for coverage here does not rest on labels. It rests on the language of the exclusion, on the alleged allocation of the money, and on the breadth of the damages demand.

We hold nothing broader. The presence of a fraud, racketeering, fiduciary or negligence count does not by itself create a potential for coverage, and a negligence count does not require a defense if every fact alleged falls wholly within an exclusion correctly construed. Equally, we do not hold that the exclusion can never reach fee restitution pleaded as a tort. The task is to compare the allegations with the contract, not to count causes of action. Nor does the policy's promise to defend allegations that are groundless, false or fraudulent change the analysis. It means that the defense obligation is measured by the allegations, before and apart from any finding of wrongdoing. It does not decide whether public policy or another policy term would bar payment of a particular adjudicated liability, and we do not decide that.

*D. The New York sequence and the summary judgment.* New York prescribes the order in which an ambiguity is addressed. When policy language is susceptible of two reasonable interpretations, the parties may submit extrinsic evidence of what it was meant to convey, and resolving the ambiguity is then for the trier of fact where that evidence presents a genuine dispute. If the extrinsic evidence is conclusory and fails to resolve the ambiguity, the question remains one of law, and the ambiguity is resolved against the insurer that drafted the policy. State v. Home Indemnity Co., 66 N.Y.2d 669, 671-72 (1985). Construction against the drafter is therefore the last step and not the first. Neither side may bypass a genuine inquiry into intended meaning because an immediate legal conclusion would suit it.

Measured against that sequence, the judgment for Home cannot stand. Home had to show that the allegations fall solely and entirely within the exclusion and that the exclusion is subject to no other reasonable interpretation. The district court read the exclusion as reaching the whole action as a matter of law. Because the exclusion is reasonably open to a second reading, that conclusion treated one reasonable reading as the only one. Nothing in the materials before us shows that the parties' intended meaning has been established by admissible evidence free of genuine dispute, and Home cannot fill that gap with its own preferred reading.

For the same reason, we do not direct judgment for McCostis or declare that Home must defend. To construe the exclusion against Home now would skip the step New York requires. We do not decide whether the record contains evidence of intended meaning or what such evidence shows. That is for the district court in the first instance.

**III. Remand and matters left open**

The judgment is vacated and the cause remanded. After resolving the jurisdictional question described in Part I, the district court shall afford the parties an opportunity to offer relevant extrinsic evidence of the intended meaning of the fee-return exclusion, if it is available and admissible. If that evidence establishes the meaning without genuine dispute, the court may decide the defense question accordingly. If it presents a genuine dispute of material fact, the dispute is not to be resolved against McCostis on summary judgment. If the evidence is conclusory or fails to resolve the ambiguity, the exclusion is construed against Home, and the court will then determine, applying the defense promise, whether the complaint potentially falls within coverage.

The remand does not call for findings about McCostis's actual participation in the alleged scheme or about who ultimately retained particular sums. Those matters belong to the underlying litigation unless a distinct coverage issue properly requires them. We leave open: whether the portion of the demand measured by the sums McCostis is alleged to have received is within the exclusion standing alone; indemnity of any adjudicated liability, including any bar arising from public policy; the truth of the allegations; waiver or estoppel arising from Home's initial undertaking of the defense and later withdrawal, which is not before us on this record; the allocation of any judgment or settlement; and the application of the policy's other terms, including its exclusion of fines, statutory penalties and sanctions and whether any component of recovery, such as multiplied damages, falls within it.

**Controlling propositions.**

1. An insurer may refuse to defend on the strength of an exclusion only if the complaint falls solely and entirely within it and the exclusion is subject to no other reasonable interpretation (Seaboard; Colon; Villa Charlotte Bronte). The test compares the allegations with the contract language, not the causes of action pleaded. The burden is Home's, as insurer and as summary-judgment movant, and review is de novo. Applied here, the exclusion is reasonably open to a reading limited to the insured's own restoration of fees, so Home did not carry its burden as to a demand measured by sums others received. The ambiguity rests on the text and on the sums allegedly received by others, not on tort labels, and the exclusion is not held categorically inapplicable to fee restitution pleaded as a tort.
2. The New York sequence is text, then extrinsic evidence of intended meaning, then construction against the insurer only if that evidence fails to resolve the ambiguity (State v. Home Indemnity; Matthews; Breed). The judgment for Home is therefore vacated, and none is directed for McCostis.

Both propositions are joined by Pratt, Walker and Stone-Khoury.

**Principal contrary argument.** Home contends that all demanded sums derive from improperly collected fees repaid by Winston, so relabeling restitution as racketeering, fiduciary or negligence liability cannot defeat the exclusion. The answer is in Part II.C: Home's reading is reasonable but not the only reasonable one, and the potential coverage rests on the text and the alleged allocation of the money, not on labels.

### Precedent and legal effect

This is a published opinion of a three-judge panel, joined by all three judges on each proposition above. It is precedential in this circuit on those propositions, limited to the described record: the exclusion's ambiguity as applied to alleged compensatory liability for amounts received by other participants, and the New York sequence governing the defense question. It follows Colon, Villa Charlotte Bronte, Seaboard, Breed, Matthews and State v. Home Indemnity as New York law and overrules or displaces no prior decision of this court. It is a federal court's application of New York law and binds no New York court. The portion of the demand measured by McCostis's own alleged receipts, indemnity, waiver and estoppel, and the other policy terms identified in Part III are not decided, and statements about them are not holdings. The jurisdictional direction in Part I applies settled law and is party-specific relief. The rule of Part II is a ruling for the circuit on the footing that diversity jurisdiction exists; if the district court finds it lacking, the judgment is vacated for want of jurisdiction and Part II supplies no rule. The vacatur and remand bind the parties, and the ambiguity ruling and the order of inquiry are law of the case on remand. There is no separate writing.

### Remedy and next stage

The judgment of the district court is vacated and the cause remanded for the proceedings described in Parts I and III. The scope of the remand is limited to the threshold diversity determination, the extrinsic-evidence inquiry and the application of the New York sequence to the defense question. The district court is not directed to make findings on McCostis's participation in the alleged scheme or on the retention of particular sums, and it is not directed to enter a declaration for either party.
