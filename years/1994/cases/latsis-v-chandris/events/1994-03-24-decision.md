- **Docket:** No. 93-7704.
- **Court:** United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit.

- **Decided:** March 24, 1994.
- **Panel:** James L. Oakes, Senior Circuit Judge; Amalya L. Kearse, Circuit Judge; Alex-Lamar Stone-Khoury, Circuit Judge.

### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** A judgment of the Southern District of New York entered in June 1993 for the defendants, after a jury found that Latsis was not a seaman within the Jones Act, 46 U.S.C. app. § 688. The judgment disposed of the action and is a final decision reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291.

**Material record.** Latsis was Chandris's fleet electronics superintendent, servicing a passenger fleet of about five or six vessels, and his work included both shoreside duties and voyages. The evidence on how much of his time he spent aboard ship conflicted sharply: Latsis put it at about seventy-two percent, and his supervisor at about ten percent. In May 1989, during a voyage of the S.S. Galileo from Baltimore to Bermuda, Latsis developed an eye problem. The ship's physician suspected a detached retina but did not arrange immediate specialist treatment, and Latsis alleges that the delay cost him most of the central vision in his right eye. Negligence and causation are contested and were never resolved. After returning to work, Latsis sailed with the Galileo to Germany and spent about six months supervising electronic work during a major conversion of the vessel, which involved substantial work on propulsion, machinery, and accommodation. The vessel returned to service as the Meridian. The parties agreed at trial that Latsis's work contributed to the function or mission of the fleet; they disputed whether his relationship to the vessels made him a seaman. The district court instructed the jury in terms of permanent assignment or substantial work aboard vessels, but told it to exclude the German conversion period because the Galileo was out of navigation. Latsis objected to that exclusion. The jury found that he was not a seaman and so did not reach malpractice, causation, or damages.

**Preservation.** The objection to the categorical exclusion of the conversion period was specific and is preserved. Latsis did not object in the same way to the rest of the seaman-status charge, which he now says framed the question in unduly temporal terms.

**Issue 1: the preserved exclusion.** Whether the instruction directing the jury to disregard the six-month conversion assignment misstated the law of seaman status and, if so, whether a new trial is required. The correctness of the legal standard in an instruction is reviewed independently; inferences from conflicting evidence belong to the jury; and prejudice is judged under Fed. R. Civ. P. 61 and 28 U.S.C. § 2111.

**Issue 2: the unpreserved objections.** Whether the broader connection formulation in the charge warrants relief although not objected to. The governing standard is Fed. R. Civ. P. 51 and the circuit's demanding plain-error review of unobjected-to civil instructions, which requires an exceptional showing and not merely a better alternative instruction.

### Judgment

**Component 1, the judgment and the preserved drydock exclusion:** The judgment is vacated and the case remanded for a new trial. For: Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury. Against: Judge Kearse, who would affirm.

**Component 2, the unpreserved objections to the broader charge:** The court grants no relief on the unobjected-to portions of the charge and does not decide whether they meet the plain-error standard. The vacatur rests on the preserved objection alone. Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury so hold. Judge Kearse would reach the unpreserved challenge and reject it as insufficient for relief under Rule 51.

**Component 3, remedy:** New trial. The jury reached only seaman status, so negligence, causation, and damages remain undecided and are open on retrial to the extent seaman status is established. The court makes no finding that Latsis is a seaman and none on the proportion of his time spent aboard ship. Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury; Judge Kearse dissenting.

### Opinions and holdings

**Writings and joins.** Opinion of the court by Judge Oakes, in which Judge Stone-Khoury joins in full. Dissent by Judge Kearse, joined by no one. There is no concurrence and no other separate writing.

**Opinion of the court.** OAKES, Senior Circuit Judge, in which STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judge, joins:

Antonios Latsis alleges that he lost most of the central vision in his right eye because the physician aboard the S.S. Galileo failed to arrange prompt specialist care for a suspected detached retina. His action never reached the merits. The jury was asked whether he was a seaman under the Jones Act, 46 U.S.C. app. § 688, and, having been told to disregard the six months he spent supervising the Galileo's conversion in a German yard, found that he was not. We hold that the instruction withdrawing that period from the jury's consideration was legal error, and that on this record the error cannot be treated as harmless. We vacate the judgment and remand for a new trial. Judge Stone-Khoury, who did not sit when the appeal was argued in December 1993, has participated in the decision on resubmission of the papers.

**I. The governing law.** The Jones Act gives a remedy to a "seaman" without defining the word. Two decisions of the Supreme Court in 1991 frame the inquiry. McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander, 498 U.S. 337, 355 (1991), held that a seaman need not aid in navigation or contribute to the transportation of the vessel, but must be doing the ship's work, and that "[t]he key to seaman status is employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation." Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni, 502 U.S. 81, 88-89 (1991), added that the inquiry is of necessity fact-specific, that an occupational label such as ship repairman does not settle it, and that not all ship repairmen lack the necessary connection as a matter of law.

Our own decisions require a sufficiently substantial, more or less permanent connection with a vessel or fleet. Harney v. William M. Moore Building Corp., 359 F.2d 649, 654 (2d Cir. 1966); Klarman v. Santini, 503 F.2d 29, 33 (2d Cir. 1974); Salgado v. M. J. Rudolph Corp., 514 F.2d 750, 755 (2d Cir. 1975). Salgado's description of the seaman as aboard primarily as an aid to navigation cannot survive Wilander, but the permanence requirement is a separate element that Wilander left standing. Weiss v. Central Railroad Co., 235 F.2d 309 (2d Cir. 1956), is a reminder that a mixture of shore and vessel duties does not by itself defeat status. Useful work is therefore necessary but not sufficient. The parties' agreement that Latsis's work contributed to the fleet's function establishes only that element. It does not answer whether his employment connection to the vessels was of the kind the Jones Act protects, and that is the question the jury was asked to decide.

We review independently whether an instruction states the governing standard correctly. Where the evidence supports competing inferences, the choice between them belongs to the jury. Even a preserved error does not require a new trial unless it affected a party's substantial rights. Fed. R. Civ. P. 61; 28 U.S.C. § 2111.

**II. The exclusion of the conversion assignment.** The district court was right that the question is the worker's employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation. Its instruction nonetheless treated two different questions as one. The first is whether a worker injured while a vessel is undergoing major reconstruction is a seaman for that injury; the answer there naturally turns on the vessel's condition at the time. The second is whether a worker's overall employment relationship with a vessel or fleet is the substantial and more or less permanent connection the cases require, where the injury occurred earlier, at sea, aboard a vessel that was indisputably in navigation. The vessel's status during a later refit is pertinent to the first question. It does not make the worker's continuing assignment irrelevant to the second.

Latsis was hurt on a voyage from Baltimore to Bermuda. His claim does not depend on having been injured during the German conversion. He offers the conversion assignment for what it shows about the relationship he already had: he sailed with the vessel to the yard, supervised work directed at her return to service, and remained a fleet employee afterward. An instruction that the jury must disregard those six months made one legal characterization, that the Galileo was out of navigation during the refit, conclusive on a question to which it is not conclusive. That was error.

Neither West v. United States, 361 U.S. 118, 122 (1959), nor McKinley v. All Alaskan Seafoods, Inc., 980 F.2d 567 (9th Cir. 1992), holds otherwise. West involved a vessel withdrawn from maritime service for a complete overhaul and a shore-based repair worker injured during the overhaul, and it concerned the vessel owner's duties to that worker. McKinley, which is persuasive only, concerned a worker killed in a fire aboard a hull under conversion, and it turned on whether that vessel was in navigation when the fire occurred. Each addresses an injury sustained while the vessel was being rebuilt. Neither addresses whether the continuing employment of a worker injured earlier, at sea, may be weighed in deciding whether his connection to the vessels was an enduring one.

Chandris's strongest argument is that a major conversion takes a vessel out of navigation and that land-based ship-repair work cannot manufacture a seaman's status. The argument is serious, and Chandris is entitled to make it to the jury. It may contend that the German assignment was qualitatively shoreside reconstruction work, that it says little about any sea-based connection, and that its scale makes it a poor guide to an enduring fleet relationship. All of that goes to weight. None of it justifies telling the jury, as a matter of law, to put six months of the assignment out of mind. Our holding is correspondingly narrow. We do not hold that conversion or repair time always counts, that all ship repair is qualifying service, that Latsis is a seaman, or that a fixed percentage of time aboard ship decides the matter. We hold only that a vessel's repair or conversion status does not categorically make a worker's continuing assignment irrelevant to seaman status for an earlier shipboard injury, and that the character and weight of that assignment are for the jury.

**III. Prejudice.** The error was not harmless. Status was the only question the jury decided. The evidence on time aboard ship conflicted sharply, seventy-two percent against ten percent, so the outcome did not follow from a single undisputed fact. The excluded period was six months of a continuing assignment, directly relevant to whether his relationship to the vessels was enduring, and the verdict was general, so it does not show what the jury would have concluded had it been permitted to consider that assignment. We cannot say that the instruction did not affect Latsis's substantial rights. We need not decide which party bears the burden on prejudice, because the conclusion is the same either way: the record shows the error was prejudicial and does not show it was harmless.

**IV. The unpreserved objections.** Latsis also attacks the broader connection formulation in the charge. He did not object to it as he objected to the exclusion. Under Rule 51 a party may not assign as error the giving of an instruction unless it objected before the jury retired, stating distinctly the matter and the grounds. Review of an unobjected-to civil instruction is for plain error. Schaafsma v. Morin Vermont Corp., 802 F.2d 629, 636 (2d Cir. 1986); Abou-Khadra v. Mahshie, 4 F.3d 1071, 1078 (2d Cir. 1993). We have described such an error as one that is plain and works a miscarriage of justice, or is an obvious instance of misapplied law. The standard is not met merely because a better instruction could have been given, and the importance of a developing area of maritime law does not substitute for preservation.

Because the preserved error independently requires a new trial, we have no occasion to decide whether any unobjected-to portion of the charge meets that standard, and we do not decide it. We rest the judgment on the preserved objection alone. We neither find the original phrasing plainly erroneous nor endorse it. On retrial, the court should charge under Wilander and the circuit connection cases in a way that distinguishes work that contributes to a vessel's mission from the employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation that status requires. The charge should permit the jury to consider evidence of the continuing fleet relationship, including the conversion assignment, with each side free to argue its character and weight. And it should leave genuine factual disputes, including the proportion of Latsis's time spent aboard ship, to the jury. This guidance is tied to the preserved error and is not a ruling on the unpreserved objections.

**V. Disposition.** The judgment of the district court is vacated and the case is remanded for a new trial on seaman status under a charge consistent with this opinion. Because the jury decided nothing else, the questions of negligence, causation, and damages remain open for any necessary further trial. We express no view on whether Latsis is a seaman or on how the conflict over the time he spent aboard should be resolved.

**Dissent (Judge Kearse).** Judge Kearse would affirm the judgment for the defendants and cannot join the vacatur, whether on the preserved exclusion or more broadly. Her dissent rests on two grounds. First, she would hold that a new trial is not supported under the preservation rules: no relief may rest on the unobjected-to portions of the charge, because Rule 51 and the circuit's plain-error cases (Schaafsma and Abou-Khadra) require an exceptional showing that is not made, and the preserved objection to the exclusion does not by itself carry a new trial. Second, she does not accept the majority's treatment of the conversion and navigation question. Giving weight to the Galileo's status as out of navigation during the conversion, and to the line of authority exemplified by West and McKinley, she would leave the judgment undisturbed. Her grounds do not depend on whether the exclusion was strictly correct or merely not a ground for reversal; either would support affirmance. She would not determine seaman status, negligence, causation, or damages. She also would not join a revival of the aid-to-navigation element, a fixed time-fraction test, a rule that conversion work is always probative, or any appellate determination of seaman status, none of which the majority adopts.

**Controlling propositions.**

1. *Rule.* A vessel's repair or conversion status does not categorically make a worker's continuing assignment irrelevant to seaman status where the injury occurred earlier, at sea, aboard the vessel while she was in navigation; the assignment is evidence on whether the worker's connection to the vessel or fleet is substantial and more or less permanent, and its character and weight are for the jury. *Authority:* 46 U.S.C. app. § 688; Wilander, 498 U.S. at 355; Gizoni, 502 U.S. at 88-89; Harney, Klarman, and Salgado (permanence requirement only); Weiss. *Test and burdens:* employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation, plus a more or less permanent connection to a vessel or fleet; no aid-to-navigation element; no numerical threshold; independent review of the instruction, with disputed inferences left to the jury. *Application:* the injury occurred at sea on a vessel in navigation, and the six-month assignment after it was relevant evidence of the continuing relationship. Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury.
2. *Prejudice.* An instruction that removes a substantial portion of a continuing assignment from a general-verdict seaman-status finding turning on conflicting evidence is not harmless under Rule 61 and 28 U.S.C. § 2111. This is an application to this record and not a general rule. Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury.
3. *Preservation.* The judgment rests on the preserved objection alone; the plain-error question on the unobjected-to charge is not decided. Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury.

**Matters left open.** Whether an injury sustained during a major conversion would itself support seaman status, and when a vessel under conversion is out of navigation; whether conversion or repair time ever counts without more, or counts in a given case; whether any unobjected-to portion of the charge meets the plain-error standard; which party bears the burden on prejudice; whether Latsis is a seaman, and how the conflict between seventy-two and ten percent is resolved; and negligence, causation, and damages.

### Precedent and legal effect

This is a published opinion. The holding in proposition 1 is circuit precedent on the votes of Judges Oakes and Stone-Khoury, two actual joins on a three-judge panel, and applies in the Second Circuit from March 24, 1994. The prejudice ruling decides only this record. The judgment vacating the defense judgment and ordering a new trial is party-specific relief. The retrial guidance on the charge is tied to the preserved error and is not a ruling on the unpreserved objections. The dissent has no precedential force.

The opinion follows Wilander and Gizoni and applies the circuit's permanence requirement from Harney, Klarman, and Salgado. It recognizes that Wilander displaced the aid-to-navigation element that Salgado's formulation contained, and it leaves the rest of those decisions in force. It treats West and McKinley as addressing injuries sustained during reconstruction and distinguishes them for the question decided here. It changes no rule of preservation. Rule 51 and the circuit's plain-error standard are applied as stated in Schaafsma and Abou-Khadra, and the opinion declines to extend or relax them. On remand, the district court is bound by this opinion as the law of the case on the matters decided.
