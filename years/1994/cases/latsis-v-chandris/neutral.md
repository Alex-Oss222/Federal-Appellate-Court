## Record and issues

**Material record:** Antonios Latsis worked as Chandris's fleet electronics superintendent, servicing a passenger fleet of approximately five or six vessels. His duties included shoreside work and voyages. The evidence concerning vessel time differed sharply: Latsis described spending roughly seventy-two percent of his time aboard ship, while his supervisor estimated approximately ten percent.

In May 1989 Latsis developed an eye problem aboard the S.S. Galileo during a voyage from Baltimore to Bermuda. The ship's physician suspected a detached retina but did not arrange immediate specialist treatment. Latsis alleged that the delay caused the substantial loss of central vision in his right eye. Negligence and causation remained contested and were not resolved by the jury.

After returning to work, Latsis sailed with Galileo to Germany and spent approximately six months supervising electronic work during a major conversion. The project included substantial work to propulsion, machinery, and accommodation. The vessel returned to service as Meridian. At trial the parties agreed that Latsis's work contributed to the fleet's function or mission; the dispute was whether his relationship to the vessels made him a Jones Act seaman.

The jury was instructed in terms of permanent assignment or substantial work aboard vessels, but was told to exclude the German drydock period because Galileo was out of navigation. Latsis objected to that exclusion. The jury found that he was not a seaman and therefore did not reach malpractice, causation, or damages. Judgment was entered in June 1993. *Latsis*, 20 F.3d at 47-50.

### Issue 1: Seaman-status instruction and preserved drydock exclusion

**Preservation and reach:** Latsis specifically objected to excluding the six-month drydock assignment. The stipulated contribution-to-function element does not resolve his employment connection.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the legal instruction; competing evidence and inferences belong to the jury. Civil prejudice is assessed under Rule 61.

**Governing test and burdens:** A seaman needs an employment-related connection to a vessel in navigation; useful work alone is insufficient. Whether later repair service bears on an enduring employment relationship differs from whether an injury during reconstruction itself would be covered.

*McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander*, 498 U.S. 337, 353-55 (1991), eliminates a requirement that a seaman aid in navigation. It does not make every employee who performs a useful task for a vessel a seaman. *Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni*, 502 U.S. 81 (1991), likewise prevents occupational labels from conclusively excluding otherwise qualifying maritime workers.

Circuit decisions including *Salgado v. M. J. Rudolph Corp.*, 514 F.2d 750, 755 (2d Cir. 1975), *Klarman v. Santini*, 503 F.2d 29, 33 (2d Cir. 1974), and *Harney v. William M. Moore Building Corp.*, 359 F.2d 649, 654 (2d Cir. 1966), require a sufficiently substantial, more or less permanent relationship to a vessel or fleet. *Weiss v. Central Railroad Co.*, 235 F.2d 309 (2d Cir. 1956), is an older reminder that mixed shore and vessel duties do not themselves defeat status. The Fifth Circuit's formulation in *Offshore Co. v. Robison*, 266 F.2d 769 (5th Cir. 1959), is persuasive, not binding.

Chandris's strongest argument is that a major reconstruction takes a vessel out of navigation and that land-based ship-repair work cannot manufacture seaman status. *West v. United States*, 361 U.S. 118, 121 (1959), and *McKinley v. All Alaskan Seafoods, Inc.*, 980 F.2d 567 (9th Cir. 1992), support careful attention to the character of the vessel and work during reconstruction.

Latsis's stronger distinction concerns the question for which the repair period is offered. His injury occurred while Galileo was operating at sea. The subsequent assignment bears on whether his employment relationship was an enduring fleet assignment rather than an occasional shoreside service call. Whether a vessel is in navigation for liability arising during a particular repair operation is not necessarily identical to whether every day of an employee's continuing assignment must be erased from the evidence of that relationship. The scale of the conversion remains relevant; categorical exclusion risks preventing the jury from considering the relationship as a whole.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** McDermott International, Inc. v. Wilander, 498 U.S. 337, 353-55 (1991), removes the aid-to-navigation requirement but preserves an employment connection; Southwest Marine, Inc. v. Gizoni, 502 U.S. 81 (1991), preserves fact-sensitive status analysis; West v. United States, 361 U.S. 118 (1959), addresses a vessel undergoing major repairs in its own setting.

**Other governing authority:** 46 U.S.C. app. § 688; Fed. R. Civ. P. 51, 61; Salgado v. M. J. Rudolph Corp., 514 F.2d 750, 755 (2d Cir. 1975), and related circuit connection precedents retain force except where Wilander displaced the navigation-duty element.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Major conversion removed Galileo from navigation, and land-based reconstruction service cannot by itself establish a sea-based employment connection.
