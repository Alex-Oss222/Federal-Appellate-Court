# Latsis v. Chandris, Inc.

**Status:** Version 1; proposed, not user-approved. **Approval reference:** None. The source brief states that every Stone position, alternative, and negotiating limit is a proposal pending approval.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose a new trial on the preserved drydock-instruction issue. The holding should protect the jury's assessment of an enduring vessel relationship without declaring Latsis a seaman as a matter of law or imposing a numerical threshold absent from governing law.

The Jones Act protects a maritime employment relationship, not merely a job title. Useful shipboard work is necessary but not sufficient. Conversely, a fleet employee does not necessarily become a stranger to the fleet each time a vessel undergoes a lengthy refit. The proper inquiry considers the character, continuity, and substantiality of the actual assignment. Here Latsis sailed with the vessel to the repair location, supervised work connected to its return to service, and continued his fleet employment afterward. Those facts could matter even if the vessel was temporarily out of navigation.

Chandris must remain free to argue that the six-month conversion was qualitatively land-based and that Latsis's ordinary work lacked the requisite vessel connection. The competing ten-percent and seventy-two-percent accounts should not be resolved on appeal. But an instruction excluding the entire conversion period makes one legal characterization conclusive before the jury evaluates what that assignment shows. On this record, the period was sufficiently substantial to make its exclusion prejudicial to the status determination.

Stone should resist treating every imperfection in the broader charge as plain error. *Wilander* does not itself answer every question about the necessary relationship, and the presence of an important doctrinal issue is not a substitute for preservation. The preserved error supplies a sufficient path to a new trial. The mandate should direct a charge that distinguishes contribution to a vessel's mission from the necessary employment connection, permits relevant evidence of the continuing fleet relationship, and leaves genuine factual disputes to the jury.

An older illustration, Judge Learned Hand's *The J. S. Warden*, 175 F. 314, 315 (S.D.N.Y. 1910), emphasizes function in the vessel's work rather than traditional nautical titles. That district-court reasoning is historically useful but does not displace circuit requirements concerning connection and navigation. Nor may Stone import the later Supreme Court disposition of this litigation or its numerical guidance into a March 1994 opinion. The proposed analysis follows the Master Jurisprudence's ordinary-law, preservation, and record rules rather than creating a special solicitude test.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** a categorical direction to disregard this continuing drydock assignment was erroneous and prejudicial; order a new trial on seaman status and, if established, the remaining claims.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** reserve the general status of major-conversion work and hold only that this employee's continuous fleet relationship could not be assessed by automatically excluding that evidence. The jury may still give the period limited weight.

**Nonjoin limit:** directing judgment that Latsis is a seaman, imposing a fixed percentage unsupported by then-governing law, treating all ship repair as qualifying service, or bypassing Rule 51 because a broader doctrinal restatement would be attractive.
