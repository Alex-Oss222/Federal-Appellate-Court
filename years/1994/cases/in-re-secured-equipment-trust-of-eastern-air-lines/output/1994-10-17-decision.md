## Decision summary

The question was whether the Secured Equipment Trust, created by Eastern Air Lines to raise $500 million through certificates secured by part of its aircraft fleet, is a "business trust." If it is, it counts as a "person" against whom an involuntary Chapter 11 case may be commenced under 11 U.S.C. § 303(a). The bankruptcy court dismissed the involuntary petition on the ground that the Trust was not a qualifying business trust, and the district court affirmed.

The court of appeals reversed. Judge Kearse, writing for the court and joined by Judge Stone, held that the Trust is a business trust. The Code does not define the term, and the court answered the question as one of federal law by examining the whole arrangement: the trust's formation, its purposes, its investment structure, the operations its instruments authorize, and the operations its trustee has in fact carried on. The court rested its holding on the certificate-financed capital-raising structure, the Trust's holding of the equipment, the commercial leasing and management powers the indenture conferred from the outset, and the large-scale exercise of those powers. It held that neither a fixed return to the holders nor secured-financing economics alone takes a trust out of the category, and that the absence of a residual profit interest in the holders does not defeat classification.

The court limited its holding. It did not hold that post-default management alone is dispositive, that eligibility follows from financial size, or that every collateral or indenture trustee is a business trust. It also stressed that eligibility decides very little: no order for relief is directed, no priority is altered, and the remaining requirements of section 303 and any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention go back to the bankruptcy court.

Judge Altimari dissented, joined by no other judge. He would have held that the Trust is not a business trust and would have affirmed the dismissal.

## The case and the result

**The dispute and the record.** Eastern Air Lines, Inc. created the Trust to raise $500 million through certificates secured by part of its aircraft fleet. In form the transaction was a sale of certificates, a purchase of aircraft with the proceeds, and a lease of the aircraft back to Eastern; the parties agree that its economic substance was a secured financing. The certificates were issued in three series with different priorities, interest rates, and maturities. First Fidelity Bank served as collateral trustee, and separate trustees represented the holders of the series. Eastern's rent was set to service the certificates' principal, premium, and interest; the indenture returned any excess to Eastern and provided that after full payment the equipment would be reconveyed to Eastern and the Trust dissolved. On default, the collateral trustee was authorized to possess, hold, lease, and sell the equipment.

Eastern entered Chapter 11 on March 9, 1989 and stopped making timely payments. A January 1991 stipulation returned aircraft and $230 million in equipment proceeds to the Trust, and the trustee thereafter maintained, marketed, leased, and sold aircraft with retained legal, accounting, and aviation help, taking possession of sixty-seven commercial jets and 165 engines, of which forty-seven jets remained under management two years later.

**The petition and the proceedings below.** On March 28, 1991, LNC Investments, Charter National Life Insurance Company, and Magten Asset Management Corporation, holders of $54.2 million in second- and third-priority certificates, filed the involuntary petition; the Resolution Trust Corporation later joined. The matter was transferred from New Jersey to the Southern District of New York. The collateral trustee and United Jersey Bank sought dismissal; the petitioning holders and Shawmut Bank Connecticut, National Association, the third-series trustee, opposed it. The bankruptcy court (Burton R. Lifland, Bankruptcy Judge) dismissed the petition on the ground that the Trust was not a qualifying business trust, in a decision reported at 153 B.R. 409 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. 1993). The district court (John E. Sprizzo, District Judge) affirmed. The petitioning holders seek reinstatement of the involuntary case; the Trust and the trustees who sought dismissal seek affirmance.

**Jurisdiction.** The district court's final disposition of the bankruptcy appeal is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 158(d). The materials before the court disclose no question about the timeliness of the appeals.

**Issues.** The first issue is eligibility: whether the Trust is a business trust, and so a "person" against whom an involuntary case may be commenced under 11 U.S.C. § 303(a). The standard of review is that the bankruptcy court's legal conclusions are reviewed independently and its findings of fact for clear error. In re Ionosphere Clubs, Inc., 922 F.2d 984, 988-89 (2d Cir. 1990). The petitioning parties must establish that the Trust is an eligible debtor. The second issue concerns reach and remedy. Eligibility is the only question that was decided below and the only one decided here. Other section 303 conditions, creditor qualification, grounds for an order for relief, priorities, and plan treatment were not resolved through the eligibility ruling.

**Judgment.**

1. Eligibility ruling (the Trust is not a business trust): reversed. Judges Kearse and Stone for reversal; Judge Altimari would affirm.
2. Dismissal of the involuntary petition: the district court's judgment affirming the dismissal is reversed, and the case is remanded for further proceedings on the remaining requirements of section 303 and any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention. Judges Kearse and Stone for; Judge Altimari against.
3. Relief and collateral questions: no order for relief is directed, and no priority is altered. The court decides nothing about creditor qualification, the grounds for an order for relief, priorities, or plan treatment. No judge on the panel would decide those matters.

Vote: two to one.

**Opinion and joins.** Opinion of the court by Judge Kearse, joined by Judge Stone in full. Dissenting opinion by Judge Altimari, joined by no other judge.

**Where the judges agree and differ.** All three judges apply the independent standard of review, place the burden on the petitioning parties, read the undefined term from the Code and the whole arrangement, and reject a categorical residual-profit requirement; all three would decide none of the questions reserved in Part V of the opinion of the court. Judges Kearse and Stone agree on the judgment, on the holding stated in Part III, on the limits stated in Part IV, and on the remedy. Judge Altimari disagrees on the judgment, on the weight of the post-default operations, and on the remedy. Each proposition of the opinion of the court has two joins.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

Before KEARSE, ALTIMARI, and STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judges.

KEARSE, Circuit Judge, joined by STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judge:

**I. The statutory question and the standard of review.**

The district court's final disposition of the bankruptcy appeal is reviewable here under 28 U.S.C. § 158(d), and the materials before us disclose no question about the timeliness of the appeals. We review the bankruptcy court's legal conclusions independently and its findings of fact for clear error. Ionosphere Clubs, 922 F.2d at 988-89. The meaning of the statutory term "business trust" and its application to the instruments that created this Trust are legal questions, and a legal definition does not become insulated from review merely because its application depends on a detailed financial arrangement. The facts that matter come from the indenture and from the bankruptcy court's own account of what the trustee did, and we disturb no finding of fact. The petitioning parties bear the burden of establishing that the Trust is an eligible debtor.

Section 303(a) permits an involuntary case to be commenced only under Chapter 7 or 11, and only against a person, except a farmer, a family farmer, or a corporation that is not a moneyed, business, or commercial corporation, that may be a debtor under the chapter invoked. Under 11 U.S.C. § 101(41) a person includes a corporation, and under § 101(9)(A)(v) a corporation includes a business trust. The Code does not define "business trust." No decision of the Supreme Court or of this court that controls the construction of the term has been identified, and decisions classifying entities under other statutes do not replace the Code's own text. The question is open in this circuit.

**II. The test.**

Three considerations guide the construction. First, Congress placed the business trust within the definition of corporation. That placement distinguishes an entity that conducts organized business through trustees from an ordinary donative or custodial trust whose incidental transactions merely preserve property. Second, the Bankruptcy Act's predecessor definition reached a business conducted through trustees where beneficial ownership or interest was represented by certificates or other written instruments, and the reports accompanying the 1978 Code describe its corporation definition as similar to the Act's. That continuity supports attention to collective investment, trustee management, and commercial operations. It does not mean that every instrument labeled a trust, or every holder of a security interest, is an eligible debtor. Third, lower-court decisions persuade but do not bind us. In re St. Augustine Trust, 109 B.R. 494, 496 (Bankr. M.D. Fla. 1990), correctly directs attention to the trust documents and the whole arrangement rather than to the bare fact that some business-like activity occurs. We agree with that much, and we adopt no enumerated list of characteristics as a checklist.

Whether a trust is a business trust under the Code is a question of federal law, answered by examining the whole arrangement: the trust's formation, its purposes, its investment structure, the operations its instruments authorize, and the operations its trustee has in fact carried on. The presence of some business-like conduct does not alone decide the question, and the absence of any single feature does not alone defeat classification.

**III. Application.**

On this record the Trust is a business trust.

The Trust was formed to raise capital from investors, and it did so on an institutional scale: $500 million through certificates in three series differing in priority, rate, and maturity, with a collateral trustee and separate series trustees. This was not a family settlement that happened to sell an asset. The investors' interests are represented by certificates, the form of beneficial interest that the predecessor definition singled out, and their capital was committed to an organized commercial transaction, the purchase of a fleet of aircraft and its lease to Eastern. The equipment was held in the Trust, to be reconveyed to Eastern after full payment. Leasing equipment for rent is itself a commercial activity, and the rent was the means by which the investors' return was produced.

The Trust's opponents emphasize that the holders' return was fixed and that any surplus reverted to Eastern. We accept those facts, and we accept the parties' agreement that the transaction was economically a secured financing. Neither fact removes the Trust from the statute. Many businesses earn specified rather than residual returns, and a contractually fixed return is no less an investment return because the investor lacks unlimited upside. Nothing in the Code's inclusion of business trusts makes participation in residual profit the boundary of the category, and the predecessor definition's focus on trustee-conducted business and certificated interests points the other way. The question is whether a financing enterprise conducted in this institutional form can be a business trust, not whether its participants must pretend that the underlying obligation was something other than what the parties agree it was. We hold that neither a fixed return nor secured-financing economics alone excludes a trust from the category, and that the absence of a residual profit interest in the holders does not defeat classification.

Nor was the trustee a mere custodian. The same indenture that fixed the holders' return authorized the collateral trustee, on default, to possess, hold, lease, and sell the equipment: in substance, to lease and dispose of an aircraft fleet on the certificateholders' behalf. Those powers were conferred when the Trust was formed, and they show what institution the parties created. We do not suggest that later distress allows a trustee to manufacture eligibility; a trust that was not a business trust at its formation does not become one because its trustee finds itself with assets to manage. Here, however, the original arrangement contained substantial continuing commercial leasing and management powers, and the trustee exercised them. After the January 1991 stipulation returned aircraft and $230 million in proceeds, the trustee maintained, marketed, leased, and sold aircraft with retained legal, accounting, and aviation professionals; it took possession of sixty-seven commercial jets and 165 engines and was still managing forty-seven jets two years later. We do not rest on the size of the collateral pool or on the number of professionals as independently making the Trust a business. Those facts matter because they make concrete the institutional function the instrument assigned from the start and show that the authorized powers were real and continuing, not remote contingencies.

We hold, accordingly, that this certificate-financed, trustee-operated equipment enterprise is a business trust within 11 U.S.C. § 101(9)(A)(v) where its original arrangement includes substantial continuing commercial leasing and management powers, as it does here, and where those powers have in fact been exercised, as they were here. The certificated capital-raising structure, the Trust's holding of the equipment, the original leasing and management powers, and the exercise of those powers together satisfy the statutory category.

**IV. The principal contrary argument.**

The strongest argument for affirmance is that the Trust does not conduct an enterprise for its own account. It holds collateral for a debt, returns any surplus to Eastern, and liquidates after default to enforce the security rather than to pursue a mission of its own. Professional management after default is common to secured financings, and a rule that treated it as sufficient would turn ordinary security trustees into involuntary debtors at the instance of junior holders.

The concern is real, and our holding is drawn to respect it. A trustee whose role is simply to hold a lien and distribute receipts does not become a business trust because it employs counsel or sells collateral after default. We do not hold that post-default management alone is dispositive, that eligibility follows from financial size or from the fact that a trustee performs some commercial transaction, or that every collateral or indenture trustee is a business trust. What distinguishes this Trust is the combination we have described: an organized certificate investment, an integrated equipment transaction, and substantial continuing commercial powers, authorized at the outset and exercised on a large scale. That description explains why the category is satisfied on this record. It is not a checklist in which every characteristic must appear in every future case. Nor were the Trust's leasing and sale powers less commercial because they were conferred to protect the holders' return.

The Trust's opponents also point out that the series trustees had already filed claims in Eastern's bankruptcy and that ordinary proceedings could address actionable trustee misconduct. The desirability of consolidating disputes, and the existence of another forum, do not rewrite the definition of the proposed debtor, and we place no weight on them in construing the statute. Eligibility, moreover, decides very little. It does not establish that an order for relief must issue, and it does not allow junior certificateholders to erase negotiated priorities or to obtain relief merely because the equipment lost value. The bankruptcy court must still decide whether the remaining requirements for an involuntary case are met, any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention, and the treatment of claims under their governing sources. The availability of those protections is not a substitute for reading the eligibility provision; it confirms that classifying the debtor need not predecide every later dispute.

**V. Matters not decided.**

We announce no universal profit-purpose prerequisite and no universal checklist for all trusts, and we do not decide whether trusts with no profit purpose fall outside the Code. We do not decide what weight, if any, decisions in other statutory settings that speak of participants sharing in an enterprise's gains carry in construing § 101(9)(A)(v); our holding rests on the Code, its predecessor, and this record. We express no view on creditor qualification, the grounds for an order for relief, priorities, or plan treatment, or on whether section 303(a)'s separate exception for a corporation that is not a moneyed, business, or commercial corporation applies to the Trust.

**VI. Disposition.**

The judgment of the district court is reversed, and the case is remanded to the district court for remand to the bankruptcy court to determine the remaining requirements of section 303 and any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention, consistent with this opinion. See 28 U.S.C. § 2106. We direct no order for relief and alter no priority.

## Separate opinions

**Dissenting opinion, Judge Altimari, joined by no other judge.**

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision. The finalized record states this dissent in summary form, and it is reported here as the record states it, in the third person.*

Judge Altimari would affirm the dismissal and does not join the holding, the application, or the disposition in the opinion of the court. He proceeds from the same standard of review, the same allocation of the burden, and the same inquiry into the whole arrangement. On that inquiry he would hold that the Trust is not a business trust. The Trust was formed to secure a debt; its holders receive a fixed contractual return; any surplus returns to Eastern; and on payment the equipment is reconveyed and the Trust dissolved. The possession, leasing, and sale that followed Eastern's default are incidental to protecting and recovering on the collateral, and the volume of that management does not convert a collateral-holding arrangement into a business trust. A contrary rule would expose ordinary collateral and indenture trustees to involuntary petitions by junior holders.

He would not make profit generation a universal prerequisite, would not fix the status of all liquidating trusts, would not treat the multi-factor tests applied by bankruptcy courts as binding, and would confine the holding to this indenture. On his view the dismissal stands and no remand is needed, and the remaining section 303 predicates, priorities, plan consequences, and the broader questions concerning nonprofit and liquidating trusts remain undecided.

## What the decision does

- **Controlling holding.** A certificate-financed, trustee-operated equipment enterprise is a business trust within 11 U.S.C. § 101(9)(A)(v) where its original arrangement includes substantial continuing commercial leasing and management powers and where those powers have in fact been exercised. The certificated capital-raising structure, the Trust's holding of the equipment, the original leasing and management powers, and the exercise of those powers together satisfy the statutory category. Neither a fixed return nor secured-financing economics alone excludes a trust from the category, and the absence of a residual profit interest in the holders does not defeat classification. The limits are stated in Parts IV and V of the opinion: post-default management alone is not dispositive; eligibility does not follow from financial size or from a trustee's performance of some commercial transaction; not every collateral or indenture trustee is a business trust; there is no universal profit-purpose prerequisite and no universal checklist; and the question whether trusts with no profit purpose fall outside the Code is not decided. The statement that a trustee who merely holds a lien and distributes receipts is not a business trust marks the limit of the holding and is not an independent rule for such trustees.
- **Legal effect and treatment of precedent.** The opinion is published and precedential. The holding, stated in Part III and limited by Parts IV and V, is the holding of the court on the votes of Judges Kearse and Stone, and it is binding circuit precedent on the classification of a certificate-financed, trustee-operated equipment enterprise whose original arrangement includes substantial continuing commercial leasing and management powers that have been exercised. The standard of review follows Ionosphere Clubs. The decision in St. Augustine Trust is treated as persuasive authority and followed only in its direction to examine the trust documents and the whole arrangement. No earlier decision of this court fixed a definition of business trust under the Code, and none is overruled or displaced. The reversal and remand are party-specific relief. The determination that the Trust is a business trust is the law of the case on remand as to eligibility. Nothing in the decision has preclusive effect on any other section 303 question, on priorities, or on the treatment of any claim. The dissent is not circuit law.
- **Effect on the parties.** The judgment of the district court affirming the dismissal of the involuntary petition is reversed. No order for relief is directed, and no priority is altered. Creditor qualification, the grounds for an order for relief, priorities, and plan treatment are not decided, and the Court expresses no view on whether section 303(a)'s separate exception for a corporation that is not a moneyed, business, or commercial corporation applies to the Trust.
- **Relief and next stage.** The case is remanded to the district court for remand to the bankruptcy court to determine the remaining requirements of section 303 and any properly presented grounds for dismissal or abstention, consistent with the opinion. See 28 U.S.C. § 2106.
- **Mandate and remand.** The mandate has not issued. Under Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 41(a) as in force on the date of this decision, the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order, and the timely filing of a petition for rehearing under Rule 40 stays it until the petition is decided. A petition for rehearing may be filed within the time Rule 40(a) fixes, which is generally 14 days after entry of judgment unless an order or local rule alters it. No petition and no other filing is recorded in this decision. Proceedings in the bankruptcy court on remand await issuance of the mandate and the district court's remand.
