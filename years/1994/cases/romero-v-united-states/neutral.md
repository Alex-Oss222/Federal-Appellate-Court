## Record and issues

Romero’s May 25, 1989 judgment included narcotics, firearms, assault, conspiracy-to-murder, and attempted-murder convictions involving a federal officer. The Second Circuit affirmed on February 20, 1990. On January 22, 1992, Romero sought a new trial based in part on a newspaper report of misconduct by DEA Group 33 agents who had arrested him and testified. He argued that the information would impeach those witnesses. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/28/267/581121/))

The district judge treated the application as a § 2255 motion, apparently measuring Rule 33’s two years from the district judgment. He found no evidence that the reported misconduct occurred in Romero’s case and concluded that the other proof supported the convictions even assuming thorough impeachment of the agents. Other claims were rejected as frivolous or previously resolved, but the retrieved opinion does not identify them sufficiently for an independent claim-by-claim reconstruction. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/28/267/581121/))

## Law entering the event

**Cutoff:** June 29, 1994. **Simulation holdings:** None supplied. The historical Rule 33 and pre-1996 § 2255 regime govern.

### Issue 1: Rule 33’s filing period

**Review and law:** Legal interpretation is de novo. The then-existing rule allowed newly discovered evidence motions within two years after final judgment. Where a conviction was appealed, the persuasive circuit consensus measured finality from issuance of the appellate mandate, not the district judgment. *United States v. Dayton*, 981 F.2d 1200 (11th Cir. 1993), collected that authority. No contrary circuit or directly controlling Supreme Court decision was identified in the record. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/28/267/581121/))

**Positions and paths:** Romero’s January 1992 motion was within two years even of the February 1990 affirmance, and necessarily within two years of its later mandate. The district-judgment calculation therefore cannot make the new-evidence claim untimely. Recognizing timeliness does not resolve its merits. Other grounds remained subject to the rule’s historical seven-day limit.

### Issue 2: New evidence and characterization

**Review and test:** Denial of a new trial is reviewed for abuse of discretion under the circuit’s demanding new-evidence standards. *United States v. DiPaolo*, 835 F.2d 46 (2d Cir. 1987), *Sanders v. Sullivan*, 863 F.2d 218 (2d Cir. 1988), and *United States v. Sposato*, 446 F.2d 779 (2d Cir. 1971), require material evidence with the requisite likely effect on a new trial; merely cumulative or impeaching information ordinarily does not suffice. ([Justia Law](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/28/267/581121/))

**Positions:** Romero argues that serious agent misconduct undermines central witnesses. The government emphasizes the lack of a link to misconduct in his prosecution and the remaining proof. A mere statement that some legally sufficient evidence remains is not, by itself, the complete new-trial inquiry. The question is whether the new material meets the governing probability and materiality requirements in the actual case.

**Paths and remedies:** Affirm on a properly supported failure of that showing; remand if the judge applied only a sufficiency test and the difference could matter. Clarify which claims were considered under Rule 33 and which, if any, under § 2255. Do not invent future successive-motion consequences or import later recharacterization rules.
