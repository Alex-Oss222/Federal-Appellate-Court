# Commitments: Gerda Dorothea DeWeerth v. Edith Marks Baldinger (Wildenstein & Co., Inc., third-party defendant-appellant), Nos. 93-7144, 93-7146

**Stage:** neutral modeling. **Legal cutoff:** 1994-10-27. **Inputs:** neutral.md, ENGINE.md, and the public sources cited below.

## Frank X. Altimari (Active Circuit Judge)

**Disposition:** By component. (1) Authority to entertain the motion after the mandate and the recall denial: yes; the district court could consider Guggenheim as a later event. (2) Rule 60(b)(6): reverse the reopening; a later state-court correction of a fully litigated federal prediction is not, without more, an extraordinary circumstance. (3) Rule 60(b)(5): not available. (4) Independent action: not the ground of the order under review and no pleadings were supplied, so he would not rest on it.

**Ground:** Standard Oil, 429 U.S. 17, holds that a mandate does not purport to deal with later events, so no appellate leave was needed; Eutectic and Fine bar only relitigating what the court of appeals actually decided. That settles power, not entitlement. Review of a Rule 60(b) ruling is for abuse of discretion (Browder, 434 U.S. at 263 n.7, as cited in his Kotlicky opinion), and a ruling built on a wrong legal premise is an abuse. On (b)(6) his own Bank of New York opinion requires extraordinary circumstances under Ackermann and Nemaizer, refused to vacate a consent decree after a later ruling undercut the related conviction, and invokes finality. His Maduakolam opinion confines (b)(6) to grounds not covered by other clauses. Kotlicky shows he reverses a denial where a default was involuntary and no merits ruling occurred; here the merits were fully litigated. Circuit law points the same way: Sarkisian says it is well settled that a change in decisional law is not grounds under (b)(6), and Matarese leaves a narrow "special circumstances" door for a retroactive change in law but found none where the original framework was unchanged. Moitie says a final judgment keeps its res judicata force though it rested on a legal principle later overruled. Erie and York do not themselves unwind final judgments. Pierce is a different case: same accident, parallel state judgment, removed plaintiffs; DeWeerth was the plaintiff, and nothing shows the Guggenheim parties are linked to this transaction. The Ackermann deliberate-choice rationale does not fit squarely, since DeWeerth appealed and sought certiorari and recall, so the ground is finality plus Sarkisian, not forfeiture. On (b)(5), the prior-judgment clause is not met (Guggenheim is not a judgment on which this one rests; Bank of New York read the clause the same way), and the prospective clause reaches executory or supervised decrees (Swift; Twelve John Does, persuasive; Sarkisian n.4), not a completed adjudication of a limitations bar with continuing consequences.

**Remedy:** Reverse the Rule 60 order and reinstate the 1987 judgment entered on this court's mandate; remand only for ministerial implementation, including the derivative third-party claim against Wildenstein (its posture is not in the packet). No remand to reweigh equities, because the defect is the legal premise.

**Join limits:** He could not join a rationale that the mandate or recall denial stripped the district court of power (Standard Oil). He could not join one that treats any state-court disapproval of a federal prediction as extraordinary per se, or that adopts Pierce as circuit law, since that cuts against Sarkisian and Bank of New York. He could not join a flat rule that a decisional change is never cognizable, because a panel cannot overrule Matarese; Matarese and Sarkisian must be reconciled. ## John M. Walker, Jr. (Active Circuit Judge)

**Disposition:** By component. (1) Authority to entertain the motion: yes, under Standard Oil, with no mandate bar to a later event. (2) Rule 60(b)(6): reverse the reopening. (3) Rule 60(b)(5): not available. (4) Independent action: not reached on this record.

**Ground:** In Axel Johnson he described (b)(6) as discretionary relief "appropriate to accomplish justice" (citing Matarese at 106) but required the movant to use the correct legal vehicle, examined whether the judgment was final at all (no Rule 58 judgment had been entered), and rested reinstatement on the mandatory statute, not on Rule 60. In FMC v. Boesky he treated an earlier appellate ruling in the same case as law of the case, reviewed legal questions de novo, and resolved the appeal on the dispositive ground. In Bausch & Lomb he applied New York law as New York decisions define it. Those writings support accepting Guggenheim as controlling New York law while asking separately whether Rule 60 supplies a vehicle. Without a statute, (b)(6) needs extraordinary circumstances (Ackermann; Matarese; Sarkisian; joined opinion Andrulonis). A mistaken but fully reviewed prediction, followed by denial of certiorari and no deliberate forfeiture, is a legal error corrected later, and Moitie, Sarkisian and Eutectic (Rule 60(b)(6) is no substitute for appeal) treat that as insufficient. Review: abuse of discretion, with the meaning of the rule, the mandate's scope and the state-law framework reviewed independently. On (b)(5) he would find no executory or supervised obligation (Swift; Twelve John Does; Sarkisian n.4; Rufo shows the flexible standard is for institutional decrees), and the reversed-prior-judgment clause does not fit.

**Remedy:** Reverse the order granting relief and reinstate the 1987 judgment on this court's mandate; remand for ministerial steps and the derivative Wildenstein claim. No remand on laches or diligence, which the 1987 panel did not reach and which are not needed.

**Sources:** His own, decided before the cutoff: Axel Johnson Inc. v. Arthur Andersen & Co., 6 F.3d 78 (2d Cir. 1993); FMC Corp. v. Boesky, 36 F.3d 255 (2d Cir. Sept. 23, 1994); Bausch & Lomb Inc. v. Bressler, 977 F.2d 720 (2d Cir. 1992). Joined, not authored, so given little weight on his individual view: Andrulonis v. United States, 26 F.3d 1224 (2d Cir. 1994) (McLaughlin, J.). He has no authored opinion on (b)(5) or on a post-judgment change in state decisional law. Authorities are those listed under Altimari, plus Rufo v. Inmates of Suffolk County Jail, 502 U.S. 367 (1992), and Liljeberg v. Health Services Acquisition Corp., 486 U.S. 847 (1988) (extraordinary circumstances weigh injustice to the parties and to other cases).

**Join limits:** He could not join a rationale that the district court lacked power after the mandate. He would resist a ground that decides the equities of laches, diligence or prejudice on this record, or the independent-action question, since the pleadings are absent. He could not join a per se rule in either direction: that Erie always compels reopening, or that Rule 60 is never available after a change in decisional law. Matarese and Sarkisian bind him as prior panel decisions and must be read together.
