## Decision summary

### The case and the result

**The dispute.** Gerda Dorothea DeWeerth inherited a Monet painting in 1922; her father had acquired it in 1908. The painting was moved for safekeeping during the war and disappeared in 1945 from a castle where American soldiers had been quartered. That chronology does not identify who took the painting or establish that a particular soldier did. Wildenstein & Co., Inc. handled the painting in the 1950s, and Edith Marks Baldinger bought it in 1957 in good faith. DeWeerth located the painting in 1982, demanded its return, and Baldinger refused.

**How the case reached this court.** In the ensuing diversity action the district court found for DeWeerth and rejected the limitations and laches defenses on the record before it. DeWeerth v. Baldinger, 658 F. Supp. 688 (S.D.N.Y. 1987). This court reversed, predicting that New York would require reasonable diligence in locating stolen property in addition to demand and refusal. DeWeerth v. Baldinger, 836 F.2d 103 (2d Cir. 1987). Supreme Court review was denied on June 13, 1988, and the judgment became final. In Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell, 77 N.Y.2d 311, 317-21 (1991), the New York Court of Appeals rejected a due-diligence addition to the limitations rule, while leaving diligence and prejudice relevant to laches. DeWeerth unsuccessfully asked this court to recall its mandate, then moved in the district court under Fed. R. Civ. P. 60(b). The district court reopened the judgment, and Baldinger and Wildenstein appealed. Wildenstein is a third-party defendant, and its position is distinct from the ownership dispute between DeWeerth and Baldinger.

**The decision under review.** Baldinger and Wildenstein appeal from the order of the district court granting relief under Rule 60(b) from a judgment that had become final, and from the resulting judgment. The order is reported at 804 F. Supp. 539 (S.D.N.Y. 1992); the papers before the court do not state its date of entry. The district court reasoned principally that the New York Court of Appeals' rejection of this court's predicted rule created extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6), and alternatively that Baldinger's continued possession of the painting implicated Rule 60(b)(5). Baldinger and Wildenstein seek reversal and preservation of the final judgment. DeWeerth seeks affirmance.

**Jurisdiction.** The order granting post-judgment relief and the resulting judgment support appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1291. The papers before the panel do not include the notices of appeal or the docket entries showing when they were filed. The panel proceeds on the assumption that the appeals were timely noticed, an assumption no paper before it contradicts, and its holding on jurisdiction is limited to that assumption.

**Issues and standards of review.**

1. Whether the district court had authority to entertain the Rule 60(b) motion after the mandate and the denial of recall. Baldinger challenges that authority. Review of the mandate's legal scope and of the district court's authority is independent.
2. Whether the later New York decision supplies extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6). The appeal challenges the reopening directly. The ultimate Rule 60 ruling is reviewed for abuse of discretion, which includes correction of an erroneous legal premise; the state-law and Rule 60 framework is reviewed independently. The movant bears the burden of establishing extraordinary circumstances.
3. Whether the judgment has prospective application within Rule 60(b)(5). The meaning of the clause is a legal question reviewed independently, and the ruling is reviewed for abuse of discretion within the correctly identified category. The movant must first identify a qualifying prospective operation.

The original title, limitations and laches merits are not before the court on direct review.

**What the court decided.** The court held that the district court had authority to entertain the motion, but that the later New York decision, standing alone on this record, supplies neither the extraordinary circumstances that Rule 60(b)(6) requires nor a prospective operation within Rule 60(b)(5). The order granting relief under Rule 60(b) and the resulting judgment are reversed, and the earlier final judgment is reinstated.

Judgment: The order granting relief under Rule 60(b) and the resulting judgment are reversed, and the earlier final judgment is reinstated. By component:

1. Authority to entertain the motion after the mandate and the denial of recall: the district court had authority. Altimari, Walker and Stone (3-0).
2. Rule 60(b)(6): the later New York decision, without an independently extraordinary circumstance on this record, does not justify reopening the final judgment; the relief is reversed. Altimari, Walker and Stone (3-0).
3. Rule 60(b)(5), prospective application: continued possession under this completed judgment is not prospective operation within the clause; the alternative ground is reversed. Altimari, Walker and Stone (3-0).
4. Remedy: the earlier final judgment is reinstated and the case is remanded only for ministerial steps to give it effect. Altimari, Walker and Stone (3-0).

Vote: 3-0 on each component. No judge dissents or concurs separately.  
Opinion and joins: Opinion of the court by Judge Walker, joined in full by Judge Altimari and Judge Stone-Khoury. There is no concurrence and no dissent.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

WALKER, Circuit Judge, joined by ALTIMARI and STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judges.

These appeals ask whether a federal judgment in a diversity action, made final by appellate review and the denial of Supreme Court review, may be reopened because the state's highest court later rejected the state-law rule that this court had predicted would decide the case. The district court reopened the judgment, reasoning principally that the New York Court of Appeals' correction of our prediction created extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6), and alternatively that Baldinger's continued possession of the painting implicated Rule 60(b)(5). We hold that the district court had authority to entertain the motion. We also hold that the later state decision, standing alone on this record, supplies neither the extraordinary circumstances that Rule 60(b)(6) requires nor the prospective operation to which Rule 60(b)(5) is addressed. We therefore reverse and reinstate the earlier final judgment. The record is summarized in the decision summary above.

**I. Authority to entertain the motion.** Baldinger first argues that the district court lacked authority to consider DeWeerth's motion because our mandate ended the litigation and because we declined to recall it. The argument fails. A mandate governs the record and the issues that were before the appellate court, and a district court does not disregard it by asking whether a later event warrants relief under Rule 60(b). The Supreme Court so held in Standard Oil Co. of California v. United States, 429 U.S. 17 (1976), where it ruled that a district court may entertain a Rule 60(b) motion without first obtaining leave of the appellate court. Our own decisions are consistent. In Eutectic Corp. v. Metco, Inc., 597 F.2d 32, 34 (2d Cir. 1979), and Fine v. Bellefonte Underwriters Insurance Co., 758 F.2d 50, 52-54 (2d Cir. 1985), we held that a district court may not review or alter what this court actually decided and that Rule 60(b) may not serve as a substitute for appeal. Those decisions protect matters the mandate disposed of. They do not make every later Rule 60 motion jurisdictionally impossible. DeWeerth's motion rests on an event that occurred after our judgment became final, a decision of the New York Court of Appeals, and the district court could consider it.

Nor does the denial of the motion to recall the mandate change the analysis. That denial was unexplained, and we draw no inference from it about the reasons for it. It did not decide that no ground later presented could satisfy Rule 60, and we need not declare whether or when repeat motions remain entertainable. The power to consider a motion, however, is not an entitlement to the relief it seeks. That power did not carry with it authority to vacate a final judgment unless the requirements of the Rule were met. We turn to them.

**II. Rule 60(b)(6).** Rule 60(b)(6) permits relief from a final judgment for "any other reason justifying relief from the operation of the judgment." We review the grant of such relief for abuse of discretion, but a district court necessarily abuses its discretion when it bases a ruling on an erroneous view of the law. Cooter & Gell v. Hartmarx Corp., 496 U.S. 384, 405 (1990). We consider independently what the Rule requires, the scope of our mandate, and the state-law framework. As the party seeking relief from a final judgment, DeWeerth bore the burden of establishing the circumstances the Rule demands.

Those circumstances must be extraordinary. The Supreme Court has explained that there must be an end to litigation someday, and it has described the case in which relief was warranted, Klapprott v. United States, 335 U.S. 601 (1949), as one of extraordinary circumstances, in which conditions beyond the movant's control had kept him from defending the action. Ackermann v. United States, 340 U.S. 193, 198-99, 202 (1950). This court has applied the same standard. We have said that relief under clause (6) is generally reserved for extraordinary circumstances or for a judgment that would work an extreme and undue hardship, that a post-judgment change in the law may in special circumstances be extraordinary, and that the motion may not be used as a substitute for appeal. Matarese v. LeFevre, 801 F.2d 98, 106-07 (2d Cir. 1986). We have also said that a change in decisional law is not, by itself, grounds for relief under clause (6). Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Sarkisian, 794 F.2d 754, 757 (2d Cir. 1986). Read together, those decisions leave a narrow door open and do not make later legal disagreement a routine means of reopening a final judgment. A panel of this court could not disturb either decision, and we do not try to.

We begin with what is not disputed. Under Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), and Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99, 109 (1945), a federal court exercising diversity jurisdiction applies the substantive law of the state, including its law of limitations, so that the outcome of the litigation is substantially the same as it would be in the state's courts. When the state's highest court has spoken, its construction governs the question in litigation still pending, and a federal court's confidence in an earlier prediction does not outrank it. Guggenheim is such a decision. It rejected the due-diligence requirement this court predicted in 1987, while preserving diligence and prejudice as considerations bearing on laches. 77 N.Y.2d at 317-21. We accept Guggenheim as the authoritative statement of New York law on this point, and we accept DeWeerth's contention that it rejected, rather than adjusted, the premise on which our judgment rested. Nothing in this opinion defends a federal court's power to make state policy or to hold a litigant to a rule the state does not have.

The question before us is a different one. It is not what New York law requires, which is now settled. It is whether an authoritative correction of the legal premise of a completed federal litigation, without more, permits reopening the final judgment under the federal rule that governs such relief. We conclude that it does not on this record.

DeWeerth litigated ownership and the timeliness of her claim. She obtained a favorable decision at trial, received appellate review of the legal question on which the case turned, and Supreme Court review was denied. The defect she identifies is not a denial of a hearing, a fraud in obtaining the judgment, or a continuing decree that operates unlawfully because circumstances have changed. It is an erroneous prediction of state law, later shown to be erroneous by the state's highest court. That is the ordinary form of legal error, and the prospect that a court's reading of the law will later be repudiated by another court is a feature of all litigation. A change in decisional law does not alone satisfy clause (6), Sarkisian, 794 F.2d at 757, and the Supreme Court has made the same point about the finality of judgments in the strongest terms. The consequences of a final judgment are not altered by the fact that the judgment may have been wrong or rested on a legal principle subsequently overruled in another case, and the doctrine of finality serves public interests beyond any individual judge's determination of the equities in a particular case. Federated Department Stores, Inc. v. Moitie, 452 U.S. 394, 398, 401 (1981). Moitie concerned an unappealed judgment; this judgment was reviewed on appeal, which makes the finality interest no weaker. The importance of this painting to DeWeerth and the clarity of the New York court's later statement are real, but they do not change the category of the claimed error. A later corrective precedent does not become an exceptional circumstance because its effect on a particular litigant is described as a severe legal error.

The interest in finality on which we rest is concrete, but it is not an interest in proof of reliance. It lies in the completed adjudication of competing claims to a chattel and in the legal settlement of entitlement to it. The papers before us describe no particular expenditure or transaction by Baldinger in reliance on the judgment, and we assume none. Nor does our conclusion rest on the idea that DeWeerth, by invoking a federal forum, consented to the application of an erroneous statement of state law. A diversity litigant is entitled to state substantive law and does not consent to judicial invention. Our holding rests instead on the federal rule that governs when a completed federal judgment may be reopened. In a case still open to decision, Erie requires the application of Guggenheim. In a case that has been finally decided, Rule 60 asks for more than a later decision showing that the prediction was wrong.

We give the district court's equitable judgment the respect it is due, and we recognize that Judge Broderick was closely familiar with the equities. But the discretion Rule 60(b)(6) confers is discretion to determine whether extraordinary circumstances exist. It is not discretion to treat the ordinary occurrence of a later corrective decision as extraordinary. The order rested principally on the authoritative correction, and no independently extraordinary circumstance appears in the record before us. Because the correction alone does not satisfy the Rule, the order rested on an erroneous view of what clause (6) requires.

DeWeerth offers two principal responses. She argues first that Guggenheim did not announce a new policy but explained the rule New York had always applied, as older demand-and-refusal decisions such as Gillet v. Roberts, 57 N.Y. 28 (1874), and Goodwin v. Wertheimer, 99 N.Y. 149 (1885), show, so that leaving our judgment in place treats her differently only because her suit was in federal court. We assume the premise but reject the conclusion. The difference she identifies arises because her case was decided on a prediction rather than by the state's highest court. Erie accepts that a federal court must sometimes predict state law, and every prediction carries the risk of later repudiation. If repudiation alone reopened judgments, the exception would consume the rule of finality throughout diversity jurisdiction, and the opportunity to correct an erroneous prediction on direct review is the system's answer to that risk, not a general exemption from finality once review is complete.

She relies second on Pierce v. Cook & Co., 518 F.2d 720, 722-23 (10th Cir. 1975) (en banc), which granted relief where federal and state judgments arising from the same accident diverged after the state's highest court changed the controlling rule. Pierce does not bind this court, and its circumstances differ. The divergent judgments there concerned persons injured in a single accident, and the court itself recognized that a change in law is ordinarily not extraordinary. Guggenheim arose from a separate dispute among different parties over a different work, and no judgment concerning this painting or this transaction in another forum conflicts with the judgment here. We do not adopt Pierce as the law of this circuit. We also do not decide whether such a relationship among judgments would warrant relief in a case that presented it.

We leave open what this holding does not reach. We do not hold that a later state decision can never contribute to extraordinary circumstances, and we do not announce a rule that Erie always compels reopening. Circumstances involving an independently extraordinary defect in the proceedings, a materially different relationship among judgments, or another recognized ground under Rule 60 are not before us and are left open. We hold only that, on this record, the later authoritative rejection of a state-law prediction does not justify reopening this fully litigated final judgment. Because the order cannot stand on the ground it rested upon, we do not reach whether the motion was made within a reasonable time.

**III. Rule 60(b)(5).** Rule 60(b)(5) provides relief where "it is no longer equitable that the judgment should have prospective application." The district court alternatively relied on that clause. We interpret the clause independently and review the ruling for abuse of discretion within the correct category. A movant must first identify a qualifying prospective operation before changed circumstances can justify relief.

The clause reaches decrees that are executory or that involve the supervision of changing conduct or conditions, which are provisional and remain open to adaptation as events change. It does not reach restraints that protect rights fully accrued. United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932). A judgment is not prospective within the clause merely because its consequences continue. Twelve John Does v. District of Columbia, 841 F.2d 1133, 1138-39 (D.C. Cir. 1988) (persuasive); see Sarkisian, 794 F.2d at 757 n.4 (doubting that the preclusive nature of a dismissal with prejudice is a prospective effect under the Rule).

The judgment here adjudicated competing claims to title and possession of a painting, and it was complete when entered. Baldinger's continued possession is a consequence of that adjudication, not a decree requiring the court to administer future conduct. The record establishes continued possession. It does not establish ongoing judicial supervision, an injunction, or any executory obligation. DeWeerth answers that the judgment continues to determine who may retain the painting despite what the state now says is the rule. The same is true of every judgment awarding property or money. Treating that consequence as prospective operation would subject final property and money judgments to continuing reconsideration whenever the law later changes, and it would let a movant avoid the extraordinary-circumstances requirement of clause (6) by invoking clause (5). We hold that continuing possession under this completed judgment is not prospective operation within the clause. We decide nothing about a genuinely continuing injunction or other executory decree, or about what showing would warrant relief from one. The order under review rested on the prospective-application clause, and we address no other clause of Rule 60(b)(5).

**IV. Limits of the decision and disposition.** Our decision is limited to the order under review. We do not decide the merits of the original limitations defense, laches, diligence, prejudice, the adequacy of the search for the painting, or who took it in 1945; the reinstated judgment is the product of the earlier proceedings, and this appeal is not a new direct appeal on the original property claim. The order under review is a ruling on a Rule 60(b) motion, and no question concerning an independent action to relieve a party from a judgment is presented by the papers before us; we express no view on one. Wildenstein's appeal challenges the same reopening and falls with it. We decide nothing about the merits of any claim between Baldinger and Wildenstein beyond the effect of reinstating the judgment.

The order granting relief under Rule 60(b) and the resulting judgment are reversed, and the earlier final judgment is reinstated. The case is remanded to the district court only for such ministerial steps as are needed to give effect to this disposition. The district court is not to reweigh the equities, to redecide the Rule 60 motion, or to decide laches or diligence on remand.

## What the decision does

- **Controlling holding:** This is a published, precedential opinion of the court, and each holding below was joined by all three judges.
  1. A district court may entertain a Rule 60(b) motion resting on a later event without leave of the court of appeals; it may not relitigate a matter the mandate actually disposed of, and an unexplained denial of recall does not settle every ground later presented. Authority to entertain the motion is distinct from entitlement to relief. Authorities: Standard Oil; Eutectic; Fine.
  2. Rule 60(b)(6) requires extraordinary circumstances, which the movant must establish. The later authoritative rejection of a state-law prediction, without an independently extraordinary circumstance on this record, does not justify reopening a fully litigated final diversity judgment. This is not a rule that a later state decision can never contribute to extraordinary circumstances. Authorities: Ackermann; Matarese; Sarkisian; Moitie; Erie and York distinguished as to pending cases.
  3. The prospective-application clause of Rule 60(b)(5) reaches executory or supervisory decrees, not a completed adjudication of entitlement whose consequences continue. Continuing possession under this completed judgment is not prospective operation. Authorities: Swift; Twelve John Does (persuasive).
- **Treatment of precedent:** The opinion follows Standard Oil, Eutectic, Fine, Ackermann, Matarese, Sarkisian and Swift as binding authority, applies Erie and York to distinguish pending from finally decided cases, treats Guggenheim as controlling New York substantive law, and treats Pierce and Twelve John Does as persuasive only. It does not adopt Pierce as circuit law and does not disturb Matarese. It changes no earlier rule of this circuit. It does not revisit the 1987 decision: in any case still open to decision, Guggenheim states the New York rule, and in this closed litigation the final judgment stands.
- **Limits:** The opinion is limited to a fully litigated and reviewed final diversity judgment, a motion resting on the later authoritative rejection of the state-law prediction on which it depended, and a completed adjudication of entitlement to property. The matters described as left open are not holdings. The reinstated judgment retains whatever preclusive and law-of-the-case effect it had before the Rule 60 order; this opinion decides nothing further about either.
- **Effect on the parties:** The order granting relief under Rule 60(b) and the resulting judgment are reversed. The earlier final judgment is reinstated. The relief is party-specific, and the reinstatement binds the parties to these appeals. Wildenstein's appeal falls with the reopening it challenged, and nothing is decided about any claim between Baldinger and Wildenstein beyond the effect of reinstating the judgment.
- **Mandate and remand:** The case is remanded only for ministerial steps to give effect to that disposition, and not for any reweighing of equities or decision of laches or diligence. This opinion does not enter a mandate, and its date is not a mandate date. The papers before the court supply no mandate date for any earlier version of these appeals, and none is assumed. As the rules stood on the date of decision, a petition for rehearing under Fed. R. App. P. 40(a) could be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment, unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order or local rule. Under Fed. R. App. P. 41(a), the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order; a timely petition for rehearing stays the mandate until its disposition unless the court orders otherwise, and if a petition is denied the mandate issues 7 days after entry of the order denying it unless that time is shortened or enlarged. The ministerial proceedings on remand follow the issuance of the mandate. No filing or later proceeding is assumed.
