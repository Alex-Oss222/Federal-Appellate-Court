# DeWeerth v. Baldinger

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 93-7144 and 93-7146. **Selected version:** October 27, 1994 amended opinion, 38 F.3d 1266. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Vincent L. Broderick. **Historical argument:** November 15, 1993. The initial decision in this appeal was May 16, 1994; October 27 is the amended disposition. The simulation concerns Rule 60 relief from an already final judgment, not an unconstrained first determination of ownership or New York limitations law. [Amended opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/38/1266/635762/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Gerda Dorothea DeWeerth inherited a Monet painting in 1922. Her father had acquired it in 1908. The painting was moved for safekeeping during the war and disappeared in 1945 from a castle where American soldiers had been quartered. That chronology does not itself identify a thief or establish that a particular soldier took it.

Wildenstein handled the painting in the 1950s, and Edith Marks Baldinger purchased it in 1957 in good faith. DeWeerth located it in 1982 and demanded its return. Baldinger refused. In the ensuing diversity action, the district court found for DeWeerth, rejecting the limitations and laches defenses on the record before it. See *DeWeerth v. Baldinger*, 658 F. Supp. 688 (S.D.N.Y. 1987). Wildenstein's third-party involvement must be kept distinct from the primary ownership dispute.

The Second Circuit reversed in *DeWeerth v. Baldinger*, 836 F.2d 103 (2d Cir. 1987), predicting that New York would require reasonable diligence in locating stolen property in addition to demand and refusal. Supreme Court review was denied June 13, 1988, and the judgment became final. This earlier circuit judgment and mandate are part of the historical procedural constraint, whatever Stone thinks of their prediction.

In *Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell*, 77 N.Y.2d 311, 317-21 (1991), the New York Court of Appeals rejected a due-diligence addition to the limitations rule, while preserving the relevance of diligence and prejudice to laches. DeWeerth unsuccessfully requested recall of the federal mandate. She then sought relief under Rule 60(b). Judge Broderick reopened the judgment, reasoning principally that the state's authoritative correction created extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6), and alternatively that continued possession implicated Rule 60(b)(5). See 804 F. Supp. 539 (S.D.N.Y. 1992). Baldinger and Wildenstein appealed. *DeWeerth*, 38 F.3d at 1268-71.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The order granting post-judgment relief and the resulting judgment support appellate jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. A Rule 60 decision is reviewed for abuse of discretion, including correction of erroneous legal premises. The meaning of the rule and the effect of the prior mandate are legal questions.

The initial threshold is the district court's authority to entertain the motion after an appellate mandate. *Standard Oil Co. of California v. United States*, 429 U.S. 17, 18-19 (1976), permits a district court to consider later events under Rule 60 without first obtaining appellate leave. *Eutectic Corp. v. Metco, Inc.*, 597 F.2d 32 (2d Cir. 1979), and *Fine v. Bellefonte Underwriters Insurance Co.*, 758 F.2d 50 (2d Cir. 1985), prevent relitigation of matters actually disposed of by the appellate court; they do not make all later Rule 60 motions jurisdictionally impossible.

The unexplained denial of recall of the mandate does not necessarily establish a merits rejection of every later ground for relief. See *Doe v. New York City Department of Social Services*, 709 F.2d 782, 788 (2d Cir. 1983). That threshold authority to consider a motion must not be confused with an entitlement to reopen the judgment.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), requires federal courts adjudicating state-law rights to apply the state's substantive law. *Guaranty Trust Co. v. York*, 326 U.S. 99, 109 (1945), makes state limitations law part of that obligation in diversity. Once the New York Court of Appeals speaks, its construction governs the unresolved state-law question in pending litigation. Federal appellate confidence in an earlier prediction does not outrank the state's highest court.

But Rule 60 presents a separate federal procedural question concerning a final judgment. *Ackermann v. United States*, 340 U.S. 193, 199 (1950), and *Klapprott v. United States*, 335 U.S. 601 (1949), distinguish exceptional circumstances from the ordinary consequences of completed litigation. The Second Circuit's *Matarese v. LeFevre*, 801 F.2d 98, 106 (2d Cir. 1986), and *Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Sarkisian*, 794 F.2d 754, 757 (2d Cir. 1986), preserve exceptional equitable authority without making later legal disagreement a routine reopening mechanism.

DeWeerth's strongest argument is that *Guggenheim* did more than announce a prospective policy change. It explained the state's existing rule and expressly rejected the federal prediction. Older demand-and-refusal decisions, including *Gillet v. Roberts*, 57 N.Y. 28 (1874), and *Goodwin v. Wertheimer*, 99 N.Y. 149 (1885), support the contention that the federal court had imposed a condition the state had never adopted. Leaving the contrary federal judgment intact imposes different substantive consequences because the action happened to be in federal court. The district judge also had first-hand familiarity with the equities.

Baldinger's strongest response is that a mistaken prediction remains a legal error in an adjudicated case, not an indefinitely provisional judgment. Later state decisions will often establish that a federal prediction was wrong. If that fact alone is extraordinary, the exception consumes finality throughout diversity jurisdiction. The possible availability of certification in the earlier appeal concerns how to avoid an erroneous prediction, not a general exemption from post-judgment rules after appellate review is complete.

*Pierce v. Cook & Co.*, 518 F.2d 720, 723 (10th Cir. 1975) (en banc), is persuasive authority for extraordinary relief where federal and state judgments arising from the same accident diverged after a change in controlling state law. It does not bind the Second Circuit, and the same-transaction circumstances distinguish it from the separate litigation in *Guggenheim*. That distinction is relevant because it concerns the exceptional relationship among judgments, not merely different party names.

Rule 60(b)(5)'s prospective-application clause does not encompass every judgment with continuing consequences. An ongoing injunction or supervised equitable decree differs from a completed adjudication fixing entitlement to property. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932), illustrates the former setting. The explanation in *Twelve John Does v. District of Columbia*, 841 F.2d 1133, 1139 (D.C. Cir. 1988), is persuasive: enduring effects alone are not prospective operation of the relevant kind. Here, continued possession following a final judgment does not make that judgment an executory decree requiring continuing judicial supervision.
