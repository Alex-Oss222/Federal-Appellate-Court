## Record and issues

**Material record:** Gerda Dorothea DeWeerth inherited a Monet painting in 1922. Her father had acquired it in 1908. ### Issue 1: Authority to entertain Rule 60 relief after an appellate mandate

**Preservation and reach:** Baldinger challenges the district court’s authority after the original appeal and the unexplained denial of recall. Authority to entertain the motion is distinct from the propriety of granting it.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the mandate’s legal scope and the district court’s authority; the ultimate Rule 60 decision remains discretionary within lawful bounds.

**Governing test and burdens:** A district court may consider a Rule 60 motion based on later circumstances without appellate leave. It may not relitigate a matter the mandate actually disposed of. An unexplained denial of recall does not necessarily settle every ground later presented.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Standard Oil Co. of California v. United States, 429 U.S. 17, 18-19 (1976), permits consideration of a later Rule 60 motion without prior appellate permission.

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 60; Eutectic Corp. v. Metco, Inc., 597 F.2d 32 (2d Cir. 1979); Fine v. Bellefonte Underwriters Insurance Co., 758 F.2d 50 (2d Cir. 1985); Doe v. New York City Department of Social Services, 709 F.2d 782, 788 (2d Cir. 1983).

### Issue 2: Extraordinary circumstances under Rule 60(b)(6) and authoritative state-law correction

**Preservation and reach:** The appeal directly challenges reopening based on Guggenheim’s rejection of the prior federal prediction. The original ownership and limitations judgment is not again on direct review.

**Standard of review:** Abuse of discretion, including correction of erroneous legal premises; independent review of the state-law and Rule 60 framework.

**Governing test and burdens:** Erie and York require state substantive law in pending diversity cases. Rule 60(b)(6) separately requires extraordinary circumstances for reopening a completed federal judgment. The moving party must establish those circumstances; legal error or a later corrective precedent does not automatically meet that burden.

*Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938), requires federal courts adjudicating state-law rights to apply the state's substantive law. *Guaranty Trust Co. v. York*, 326 U.S. 99, 109 (1945), makes state limitations law part of that obligation in diversity. Once the New York Court of Appeals speaks, its construction governs the unresolved state-law question in pending litigation. Federal appellate confidence in an earlier prediction does not outrank the state's highest court.

But Rule 60 presents a separate federal procedural question concerning a final judgment. *Ackermann v. United States*, 340 U.S. 193, 199 (1950), and *Klapprott v. United States*, 335 U.S. 601 (1949), distinguish exceptional circumstances from the ordinary consequences of completed litigation. The Second Circuit's *Matarese v. LeFevre*, 801 F.2d 98, 106 (2d Cir. 1986), and *Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Sarkisian*, 794 F.2d 754, 757 (2d Cir. 1986), preserve exceptional equitable authority without making later legal disagreement a routine reopening mechanism.

DeWeerth's strongest argument is that *Guggenheim* did more than announce a prospective policy change. It explained the state's existing rule and expressly rejected the federal prediction. Older demand-and-refusal decisions, including *Gillet v. Roberts*, 57 N.Y. 28 (1874), and *Goodwin v. Wertheimer*, 99 N.Y. 149 (1885), support the contention that the federal court had imposed a condition the state had never adopted. Leaving the contrary federal judgment intact imposes different substantive consequences because the action happened to be in federal court. The district judge also had first-hand familiarity with the equities.

Baldinger's strongest response is that a mistaken prediction remains a legal error in an adjudicated case, not an indefinitely provisional judgment. Later state decisions will often establish that a federal prediction was wrong. If that fact alone is extraordinary, the exception consumes finality throughout diversity jurisdiction. The possible availability of certification in the earlier appeal concerns how to avoid an erroneous prediction, not a general exemption from post-judgment rules after appellate review is complete.

*Pierce v. Cook & Co.*, 518 F.2d 720, 723 (10th Cir. 1975) (en banc), is persuasive authority for extraordinary relief where federal and state judgments arising from the same accident diverged after a change in controlling state law. It does not bind the Second Circuit, and the same-transaction circumstances distinguish it from the separate litigation in *Guggenheim*. That distinction is relevant because it concerns the exceptional relationship among judgments, not merely different party names.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (1938); Guaranty Trust Co. v. York, 326 U.S. 99, 109 (1945); Ackermann v. United States, 340 U.S. 193, 199 (1950); Klapprott v. United States, 335 U.S. 601 (1949).

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 60(b)(6); Matarese v. LeFevre, 801 F.2d 98, 106 (2d Cir. 1986); Travelers Indemnity Co. v. Sarkisian, 794 F.2d 754, 757 (2d Cir. 1986); Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation v. Lubell, 77 N.Y.2d 311, 317-21 (1991), controlling New York substantive law; Pierce v. Cook & Co., 518 F.2d 720, 723 (10th Cir. 1975) (en banc), persuasive and transaction-specific.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Guggenheim clarified existing New York law and expressly rejected the federal requirement. Enforcing a contrary judgment solely because the case was federal perpetuates the disparity Erie forbids, and the district judge reasonably weighed the unusual equities.

### Issue 3: Prospective operation under Rule 60(b)(5)

**Preservation and reach:** The alternative ground for reopening concerns the prospective-effect clause. Continued possession is established; ongoing judicial supervision is not.

**Standard of review:** Independent interpretation of prospective application; abuse of discretion within the correctly identified category.

**Governing test and burdens:** The prospective-application clause reaches an executory or ongoing equitable decree, not every completed judgment whose effects continue. The movant must identify qualifying prospective operation before changed circumstances justify relief.

Rule 60(b)(5)'s prospective-application clause does not encompass every judgment with continuing consequences. An ongoing injunction or supervised equitable decree differs from a completed adjudication fixing entitlement to property. *United States v. Swift & Co.*, 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932), illustrates the former setting. The explanation in *Twelve John Does v. District of Columbia*, 841 F.2d 1133, 1139 (D.C. Cir. 1988), is persuasive: enduring effects alone are not prospective operation of the relevant kind. Here, continued possession following a final judgment does not make that judgment an executory decree requiring continuing judicial supervision.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** United States v. Swift & Co., 286 U.S. 106, 114 (1932), illustrates modification of continuing injunctive obligations, not routine reconsideration of settled property entitlements.

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 60(b)(5); Twelve John Does v. District of Columbia, 841 F.2d 1133, 1139 (D.C. Cir. 1988), persuasive distinction between continued consequences and prospective application.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** Application of the established prospective/consequential distinction to this final property dispute.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The completed adjudication has continuing consequences but requires no executory administration; possession does not convert it into an injunction.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** The judgment continues to determine who may retain the painting despite the now-authoritative state rule, supporting equitable reconsideration of its continuing effect.

**Additional material position:** No independently verified additional amicus position is supplied. Counsel or amicus names alone are not evidence of an argument.

**Error and remedy constraints:** Authority to consider Rule 60, satisfaction of a stated ground, and discretion to grant relief are separate. A completed judgment may be reinstated without conducting a new direct appeal on the original property claim.

**Lawful paths:** These alternatives are unranked and imply no vote or order of decision.

Alternative: Consider changed circumstances under that clause only if a genuinely executory obligation is identified.

Alternative: Reject the prospective clause if the judgment merely settles entitlement with continuing consequences.
