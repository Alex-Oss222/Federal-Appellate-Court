### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** Johns appeals the final sentence imposed after his September 1992 plea of guilty to a cocaine conspiracy: fifty-one months' imprisonment, including a two-level increase for obstruction of justice under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 and the denial of a reduction for acceptance of responsibility under § 3E1.1. The record before us does not state the date on which the district court entered the sentence.

**Material record.** The prosecution identified three sales, and the sentencing inquiry also examined other narcotics dealings. During the presentence investigation Johns denied some additional transactions and purchases. The government had contrary statements and other evidence. The appeal does not depend on declaring the denials true or immaterial; their falsity and materiality are taken as established for the purposes of this appeal. The district court treated the denials as obstruction, denied the acceptance reduction, and imposed sentence. Appellate counsel first sought leave to withdraw under Anders v. California, 386 U.S. 738, 744 (1967). Replacement counsel then presented the guideline question decided here.

**Jurisdiction.** The final sentence is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and 18 U.S.C. § 3742(a)(1) and (2). The notice-of-appeal papers and docket receipts are not part of the record before us, and the decision does not rest on any contested fact about timeliness.

**Issue 1: the obstruction increase.** Whether a defendant's unsworn presentence denials of his own alleged narcotics transactions, assumed false and material, may support a § 3C1.1 increase notwithstanding the commentary's exclusion of a denial of guilt. Standard of review: de novo, because the question is the meaning of the guideline and its commentary. United States v. Perdomo, 927 F.2d 111, 118 (2d Cir. 1991).

**Issue 2: acceptance of responsibility.** Whether the denial of the § 3E1.1 reduction can stand once the obstruction ruling is set aside, and what the sentencing court must do on remand. Standard of review: the correct reading of the guideline is applied independently, with appropriate deference to supported findings. 18 U.S.C. § 3742(e).

**Dates.** Plea, September 1992. Argument, April 19, 1994. Decision, June 13, 1994.

### Judgment

The sentence is vacated and the case is remanded for resentencing, by component as follows.

- **Obstruction increase under § 3C1.1.** Vacated. The increase cannot rest on the presentence denials. Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury in the majority; Judge Altimari dissenting, who would sustain the increase.
- **Denial of the acceptance reduction under § 3E1.1.** Vacated to the extent it rested on the obstruction ruling, and remanded for an independent determination of acceptance under § 3E1.1. The court does not direct that the reduction be granted or denied. Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury in the majority; Judge Altimari dissenting.
- **Sentence of fifty-one months.** Vacated, with remand for resentencing consistent with the opinion of the court. Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury in the majority; Judge Altimari dissenting, who would affirm the sentence.

The vote on each component is two to one. The two judges in the majority agree in the judgment and in the reasoning stated in the opinion of the court. Judge Altimari disagrees with the judgment and with the court's reasoning on the first issue.

**Opinion of the court**

McLAUGHLIN, Circuit Judge, with whom STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judge, joins:

Lester Johns, Jr. pleaded guilty in the United States District Court for the District of Vermont (Franklin S. Billings, Jr., Judge) to a cocaine conspiracy and was sentenced to fifty-one months' imprisonment. The sentence includes a two-level increase for obstruction of justice under U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1, imposed because of what Johns told the probation officer during the presentence investigation, and the district court denied him a reduction for acceptance of responsibility under § 3E1.1. Johns appeals both rulings. We hold that the obstruction increase cannot rest on the unsworn denials at issue, because Application Note 1 to § 3C1.1 withholds the increase from a defendant's denial of guilt, and that protection limits the general example of false information to a probation officer in Application Note 3(h). We further hold that the denial of the acceptance reduction cannot stand to the extent that it rested on the obstruction ruling, but that acceptance is a separate question which removal of the increase does not answer in either direction. We vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing.

BACKGROUND

Johns pleaded guilty in September 1992. The prosecution identified three sales, and the sentencing inquiry also examined other narcotics dealings. In the presentence investigation Johns denied some additional transactions and purchases. The government had contrary statements and other evidence. Johns does not ask us to find his denials true or immaterial, and the government defends the sentence on the ground that they were materially false. We therefore take the denials to have been false and material, and we ask only whether the Guidelines permit them to be punished as obstruction.

The district court added two levels under § 3C1.1, declined any reduction under § 3E1.1, and imposed fifty-one months. Appellate counsel first sought to withdraw under Anders v. California, 386 U.S. 738, 744 (1967); replacement counsel then presented the guideline question now before us. We have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and 18 U.S.C. § 3742(a)(1) and (2).

Whether the facts described in a presentence report amount to obstruction of justice under § 3C1.1 is a matter of legal interpretation that we review de novo. United States v. Perdomo, 927 F.2d 111, 118 (2d Cir. 1991). We accept supported findings of fact and give due deference to the district court's application of the Guidelines to the facts, 18 U.S.C. § 3742(e), but a sentence imposed as a result of an incorrect application of the Guidelines must be remanded for further sentencing proceedings with such instructions as we consider appropriate. Id. § 3742(f)(1).

DISCUSSION

I. The obstruction increase

A. The guideline and its commentary

Section 3C1.1 calls for a two-level increase when the defendant willfully obstructs or impedes, or attempts to obstruct or impede, the administration of justice during the investigation, prosecution or sentencing of the offense of conviction. The word "willfully" matters. We have read the guideline to require that the defendant consciously act with the purpose of obstructing justice. United States v. Stroud, 893 F.2d 504, 507 (2d Cir. 1990).

Two notes in the commentary bear on this case. Application Note 3(h) lists, among examples of conduct to which the increase applies, providing materially false information to a probation officer in respect to a presentence or other investigation for the court. We applied the same example, under an earlier numbering, in United States v. Rodriguez, 943 F.2d 215, 217-18 (2d Cir. 1991) (citing Application Note 1(e)). Application Note 1 says something different. A defendant's denial of guilt, other than a denial under oath that constitutes perjury, is not a basis for the increase, and neither is a refusal to admit guilt or to provide information to a probation officer. The note also directs a favorable evaluation of the defendant's statements.

Commentary that interprets or explains a guideline is authoritative unless it violates the Constitution or a federal statute, or is inconsistent with, or a plainly erroneous reading of, that guideline. Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36, 38, 44-45 (1993). No one suggests that either note is inconsistent with § 3C1.1 or plainly erroneous. Both bind us, and the question is how they fit together. We refer to them as Application Notes 1 and 3(h); earlier editions of the Manual numbered the probation-officer example differently, as Rodriguez shows, and the edition governing Johns's sentence is a matter the district court should confirm on remand. We decide no question that would turn on a difference between editions.

B. The government's argument

The government's argument has real force. A defendant may remain silent, but he has no privilege to lie, and we know of no decision of the Supreme Court that supplies a constitutional right to do so. A probation officer depends on accurate information, and a false answer can mislead a presentence investigation and affect the sentence. In Rodriguez a defendant told the probation officer that he had no prior record when he had several prior convictions, and we sustained the increase. We rejected the argument that a lie is immaterial because the truth was likely to be discovered, holding that the definition of a material statement "embraces all false statements that would tend to affect a defendant's sentence." 943 F.2d at 218. On those premises, the government says, Johns's statements fall squarely within Note 3(h).

We accept the premises and reject the conclusion. Johns does not claim that the Constitution entitles him to deny his conduct. His claim is textual: that the Commission, in its commentary, withheld this additional punishment from a defined class of denials. The Commission is free to protect a defendant more broadly than the Constitution requires. The observation that the Constitution does not authorize lying therefore answers a different question from whether the Commission has instructed courts not to treat a particular kind of statement as obstruction. In 1994 the Guidelines bind the sentencing court, 18 U.S.C. § 3553(b), and authoritative commentary binds with the guideline it explains. The fidelity that requires a court to apply an increase when the Guidelines call for it requires the court to withhold it when they do not. Declining to apply Note 1 because its protection exceeds the constitutional minimum would substitute judicial policy for the governing sentencing source.

C. Reading the two notes together

The two notes must be read so that each has work to do. Suppose Note 3(h) controlled whenever a statement to the probation officer was false and material. A defendant interviewed for a presentence report is asked chiefly about his offense. Nearly every false denial of guilt made in that interview can be described as materially false information given to a probation officer, since under Rodriguez a statement is material if it would tend to affect the sentence, and a denial of the conduct being sentenced hardly fails that test. Read that way, Note 1 would protect a denial of guilt only when it was true or immaterial, and a true denial was never in danger of punishment as false information. The exception would disappear in the very setting where it is most likely to matter.

The structure of Note 1 confirms the point. The Commission excluded from the protection one class of false denials, those made under oath that constitute perjury. That qualification would be unnecessary if falsity alone removed the protection. We conclude that Note 3(h) states a general example and Note 1 states a specific qualification, and that where the two meet, the specific governs. An unsworn denial of guilt does not lose that character because it is false, because it is material, or because the defendant states it in a complete sentence rather than uttering the word "no."

This reading leaves Note 3(h) a wide field. A defendant who manufactures documents, falsely implicates an innocent third party, commits perjury, or conceals prior convictions engages in conduct whose obstructive character does not depend solely on a denial of guilt. Rodriguez is an example. The lie there concerned criminal history, a collateral fact bearing on the sentence, not a denial of the offense being sentenced, and nothing we say today disturbs that decision. Our insistence in Stroud that the guideline reach conscious obstruction, 893 F.2d at 507, points the same way, because the Commission has told us that a defendant who merely denies guilt is not, for that reason alone, obstructing justice.

D. Application

Johns's statements were unsworn. As the record describes them, they denied his own alleged narcotics transactions and purchases, the conduct into which the presentence investigation was inquiring. Nothing in the record before us shows that his statements went beyond denial: it describes denials of transactions and purchases, not fabricated documents, accusations against another person, sworn statements, or misstatements about a collateral matter such as criminal history. These were denials of guilt within Note 1. That they are assumed false and material does not take them out of the note. Note 3(h) therefore does not carry the increase, and an increase imposed on this basis rests on an incorrect application of § 3C1.1.

Our colleague would classify the same statements as punishable deception under Note 3(h) and sustain the increase. We agree with him on method: the commentary is authoritative, the notes must be read together, and the question is one of text. We part ways on classification. To treat the statements as deception because they were false and material is to treat the very features that define the false denials Note 1 addresses as the features that strip them of its protection. We do not think the Commission wrote Note 1 to say so little.

E. What we do not decide

The holding is limited to the case before us. We do not decide how § 3C1.1 applies to perjury, fabricated evidence, a false accusation against another person, or deception about criminal history, and we do not hold that any of them is protected as a denial of guilt. We do not hold that every false statement to a probation officer is obstruction, and we do not hold that none is. We do not decide whether the record would support an increase on some basis other than these denials, and nothing in this opinion directs or forbids such a finding; that is a matter for the district court on a record it makes.

II. Acceptance of responsibility

Johns contends that the denial of the § 3E1.1 reduction followed from the rejected obstruction rationale and from the district court's treatment of conduct outside the offense of conviction. The government answers that a guilty plea and the absence of an obstruction increase do not establish genuine acceptance, particularly if the defendant gives false accounts of pertinent conduct. Each position is partly right.

The two adjustments answer different questions. Section 3C1.1 asks whether the defendant willfully impeded the administration of justice. Section 3E1.1 asks whether the defendant has affirmatively accepted responsibility for his offense. A defendant may avoid the first and still fail the second. In United States v. Reyes, 9 F.3d 275 (2d Cir. 1993), the district court rejected an obstruction increase and nonetheless denied the reduction, and we upheld the denial. We explained that the defendant must truthfully admit the conduct comprising the offense of conviction, that the sentencing court may require "a candid and full unraveling" of that offense, and that a guilty plea does not by itself entitle a defendant to the reduction. Id. at 278-79. The absence of punishable obstruction is therefore not affirmative acceptance, and removing the obstruction increase does not compel the reduction.

The converse is equally true. The reduction question is a separate one, and a denial of the reduction cannot rest on an obstruction premise that the Guidelines do not permit. A sentencing court may not satisfy § 3E1.1 by repeating the obstruction analysis. If the district court withheld the reduction because Johns's presentence denials were materially false statements to the probation officer, it relied on the same premise we have just set aside. Whether those denials bear on acceptance in some other way is a question under § 3E1.1 itself, to be decided on its own terms. We cannot tell from the record whether the district court also rested its ruling on a ground independent of the obstruction rationale, and we will not supply one. See United States v. Austin, 17 F.3d 27, 30 (2d Cir. 1994) (vacating where the record furnished no foundation for the denial of the reduction).

The sentencing court must also keep the offense of conviction distinct from other conduct. As we read the commentary in Austin, a defendant is not required "to volunteer, or affirmatively admit, relevant conduct beyond the offense of conviction," and acceptance is measured by the offense of conviction. 17 F.3d at 30-31. Johns denied additional transactions and purchases beyond the three identified sales. The record does not tell us whether those transactions fall within the conspiracy to which he pleaded or only within relevant conduct, or what the edition of § 3E1.1 and its commentary governing his sentence provides about false denials of conduct of either kind. Those questions determine whether and how the denials bear on acceptance, and they depend on findings that we cannot make.

On remand the district court must therefore decide acceptance independently. It must identify what Johns falsely denied, state how that conduct relates to the offense of conviction under the edition of the Guidelines governing his sentence, and identify the § 3E1.1 criterion on which it relies. We do not direct that the reduction be granted, and we do not hold that it must again be denied. The remand should trade the invalid obstruction increase neither for an automatic credit nor for an automatic renewed refusal.

CONCLUSION

The sentence is vacated and the case is remanded for resentencing. On remand the district court shall calculate the sentence without an obstruction increase resting on Johns's presentence denials, leaving intact other sentencing findings that are supported by the record and do not depend on that increase, and shall determine acceptance of responsibility independently in the manner described in Part II. We make no findings of fact, and we express no view on the ultimate acceptance determination or the resulting sentence.

**Dissent (summary)**

Judge Altimari dissents and would affirm the sentence. His analysis is textual and rests on no constitutional ground. He agrees with the court that the commentary is authoritative, that the question is reviewed de novo, that Application Notes 1 and 3(h) must be read together, and that fabrication, false accusations, perjury and collateral lies lie outside the protection of Note 1. He parts from the court on classification. In his view Johns's presentence statements, being materially false information given to a probation officer, are punishable deception within Note 3(h), not protected denial of guilt, and the Note 1 exception does not displace the example for them. Because he would sustain the obstruction increase, he sees no basis to vacate the denial of the acceptance reduction on the ground that it depended on the obstruction rationale, and he would affirm the fifty-one month sentence in full.

**Controlling propositions**

*Proposition 1 (obstruction; Part I of the opinion of the court). Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury.* Rule: Application Note 1 to § 3C1.1 excludes a defendant's denial of guilt, other than a sworn denial amounting to perjury, from the basis for the increase, and that exclusion limits the example in Application Note 3(h) of materially false information to a probation officer. An unsworn denial of the defendant's own alleged criminal conduct does not lose that character because it is false, because it is material, or because it is stated in a complete sentence. Trigger: a § 3C1.1 increase resting on a defendant's presentence statements to the probation officer. Test: classify the statement as a denial of guilt, which Note 1 protects, or as conduct whose obstructive character does not depend solely on denial, such as fabricated evidence, a false accusation of another, perjury or concealment of criminal history, which Note 3(h) and the guideline itself may reach. The classification is a legal question reviewed de novo; the commentary is authoritative under Stinson; supported findings of fact are accepted and the application of the Guidelines to the facts receives due deference under 18 U.S.C. § 3742(e). The decision does not alter the allocation of burdens at sentencing. Authority: U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 and Application Notes 1 and 3(h); Stinson, 508 U.S. at 38, 44-45; Perdomo, 927 F.2d at 118; Rodriguez, 943 F.2d at 217-18 (distinguished, not disturbed); Stroud, 893 F.2d at 507; 18 U.S.C. § 3553(b). Application: Johns's unsworn denials of additional transactions and purchases were denials of guilt and cannot support the increase. Qualifications: no constitutional or right-to-lie rationale; no holding on perjury, fabricated evidence, false accusation or criminal-history deception; no holding that any or every false statement to a probation officer is or is not obstruction; no bar on a lawful obstruction finding on other conduct.

*Proposition 2 (acceptance of responsibility; Part II). Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury.* Rule: Removal of an obstruction increase neither compels nor forecloses the § 3E1.1 reduction. The absence of punishable obstruction is not affirmative acceptance. A denial of the reduction cannot rest on an obstruction rationale the Guidelines do not allow. The sentencing court must separately identify the conduct the defendant denied, state how it relates to the offense of conviction under the edition of the Guidelines governing the sentence, and identify the § 3E1.1 criterion applied. Standard: the correct reading of the guideline is applied independently, with appropriate deference to supported findings. Burdens: unchanged; the opinion decides no question about the quantum of proof. Authority: U.S.S.G. § 3E1.1 and its commentary; Reyes, 9 F.3d at 278-79; Austin, 17 F.3d at 30-31; 18 U.S.C. § 3742(e) and (f)(1). Application: the denial of the reduction cannot stand to the extent it rested on the obstruction ruling, and the record does not show an independent ground; remand for an independent determination. Qualifications: no direction to grant or deny the reduction; no appellate fact-finding; the ultimate acceptance determination and resulting sentence are left to the district court; Austin is applied for the offense-of-conviction distinction, and the question how the commentary treats the denied transactions is not decided.

**Principal contrary argument and answer.** The government's position and the dissent treat the denials as materially false information to a probation officer under Note 3(h), relying on the absence of any right to lie and on Rodriguez. The court answers that no constitutional right is asserted, that the Commission may protect denials beyond the constitutional minimum, that reading Note 3(h) to reach every false and material denial would leave Note 1 without work to do in a presentence interview, and that Rodriguez concerned deception about criminal history, not denial of the offense being sentenced.

**Matters left open.** The Guidelines edition governing the sentence; whether the denied transactions lie within the offense of conviction or only relevant conduct and what follows for acceptance; the district court's basis, if any, for denying the reduction apart from obstruction; whether any other conduct would support an obstruction increase; and the treatment under § 3C1.1 of perjury, fabricated evidence, false accusation of another and criminal-history deception.

### Precedent and legal effect

**Publication and weight.** Published. Propositions 1 and 2 are the holdings of the court on the votes of Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury and are circuit precedent within the stated limits. The dissent is not circuit law.

**Prior decisions.** Perdomo is followed on the standard of review. Stinson is applied to the authority of the commentary. Rodriguez is followed on materiality of false statements about criminal history and is distinguished, not overruled, as to denials of guilt. Stroud is applied on the requirement of willful obstructive purpose. Reyes is followed on the separateness of acceptance from obstruction. Austin is applied on the offense-of-conviction distinction for acceptance and is not extended. Anders is mentioned only for the procedural background.

**What changes.** In this circuit, an unsworn denial of a defendant's own alleged criminal conduct made in a presentence interview is a denial of guilt within Application Note 1 to § 3C1.1 and cannot by itself support an obstruction increase under Note 3(h), whether or not it is false and material. A court may not deny the § 3E1.1 reduction on the obstruction rationale so rejected, and the reduction does not follow automatically from removal of the increase.

**Distinguish.** Party-specific relief: vacatur and remand for resentencing in this case. Law of the case on remand: the presentence denials cannot support the obstruction increase, and acceptance is to be determined independently. Dicta: the statements that perjury, fabricated evidence, false accusations and criminal-history deception are not decided; the observation that the governing edition should be confirmed; the assumption that the denials were false and material, which is a premise and not a finding. Separate positions: the dissent only.
