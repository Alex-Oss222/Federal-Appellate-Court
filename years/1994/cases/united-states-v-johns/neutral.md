### Issue 1: Obstruction adjustment for presentence denial of alleged misconduct

**Preservation and reach:** Replacement counsel presented the guideline question after initial Anders proceedings. Falsity and materiality are accepted premises for this appellate issue, not findings newly made here.

**Standard of review:** De novo guideline interpretation; United States v. Perdomo, 927 F.2d 111, 118 (2d Cir. 1991).

**Governing test and burdens:** Reconcile the specific denial-of-guilt exception in § 3C1.1 Application Note 1 with the general example of material falsehoods to a probation officer. The Commission may provide protection beyond the minimum required by the Fifth Amendment.

Application Note 1 to § 3C1.1 excludes a defendant's denial of guilt, apart from perjury, and certain refusals to admit guilt or provide information from the obstruction enhancement. The commentary also directs favorable evaluation of the defendant's statements. Application Note 3(h), however, identifies materially false information to a probation officer as potential obstruction. The provisions must be read together, not by allowing the general example to eliminate the explicit qualification.

The government's strongest argument is straightforward: materially false answers mislead the sentencing investigation and are not compelled by the Fifth Amendment. An accused can remain silent but has no constitutional entitlement to lie. *United States v. Rodriguez*, 943 F.2d 215, 218 (2d Cir. 1991), upholds an enhancement for lies about prior convictions; likely discovery of the truth does not make deception immaterial.

Johns's response is textual rather than constitutional. The Commission chose to protect some denials beyond the minimum compelled by the Fifth Amendment. *United States v. Austin*, 17 F.3d 27, 31 (2d Cir. 1994), recognizes that difference. *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), requires faithful attention to authoritative commentary. A denial does not cease to be a denial because the defendant states it in a complete sentence rather than uttering the word “no.” At the same time, fabrication, false accusations against others, perjury, and lies about collateral sentencing facts need not receive the same treatment.

*United States v. Stroud*, 893 F.2d 504, 507 (2d Cir. 1990), also requires attention to the willful obstructive conduct the guideline addresses. *Rodriguez* remains distinguishable because criminal-history deception is not simply denial of the misconduct for which the defendant is being sentenced. Acceptance presents a separate inquiry under § 3E1.1 and *United States v. Reyes*, 9 F.3d 275, 279 (2d Cir. 1993): removal of an obstruction enhancement does not compel a reduction for acceptance.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36, 38, 44-45 (1993), makes guideline commentary authoritative unless inconsistent with governing law or the guideline. No Supreme Court holding supplies a constitutional right to lie.

**Other governing authority:** U.S.S.G. § 3C1.1 and then-applicable notes; United States v. Austin, 17 F.3d 27, 31 (2d Cir. 1994); United States v. Rodriguez, 943 F.2d 215, 218 (2d Cir. 1991); United States v. Stroud, 893 F.2d 504, 507 (2d Cir. 1990).

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** False answers impede presentence investigation; defendants may remain silent but have no privilege to mislead, and the guideline specifically covers false information to probation.
