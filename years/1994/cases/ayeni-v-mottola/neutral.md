## Record and issues

**Material allegations:** Agents entered the Ayeni apartment on March 5, 1992, before obtaining a warrant connected to an investigation of Tawa Ayeni’s husband. The complaint alleges intermittent searching and detention before the warrant issued. Mottola later arrived with a television crew preparing a “Street Stories” segment. The crew recorded the occupants, private papers, and the search despite protests. The alleged conduct included interference with attempts to cover faces and damage to furniture. A family photograph was seized. The television material was not broadcast. These allegations are accepted for the dismissal motion, not established facts. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Jurisdiction:** *Mitchell v. Forsyth*, 472 U.S. 511 (1985), permits collateral review of the legal denial of qualified immunity. The appeal does not authorize resolution of disputed historical facts. **Scope:** CBS had settled; the unidentified agents’ circumstances do not make them parties to Mottola’s appeal. Mottola’s responsibility for the earlier entry remains disputed. **Missing material:** Full complaint, affidavits, warrant application, communications records, and settlement terms. ## Law entering the event

**Legal cutoff:** September 12, 1994. **Notice cutoff for immunity:** March 5, 1992. **Simulation holdings:** None supplied.

### Issue 1: Nonassisting media entry and recording

**Review and test:** De novo review of legal sufficiency, accepting well-pleaded allegations. *Bivens*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), supplies the established federal-officer search claim. *Harlow* and *Anderson v. Creighton* govern immunity. The unlawfulness must have been apparent from existing law; a factually identical prior decision is not invariably required. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Governing sources:** *Payton v. New York*, 445 U.S. 573 (1980), protects the home; *Andresen v. Maryland*, 427 U.S. 463 (1976), and *United States v. Matias*, 836 F.2d 744 (2d Cir. 1988), require restraint in document searches. Section 3105’s limitation on private assistance informs the warrant-execution context, but is not an independently assumed damages cause of action. *Davis v. Scherer*, 468 U.S. 183 (1984), prevents substituting an unrelated statutory violation for a clearly established constitutional one. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Positions:** Plaintiffs emphasize that the crew served no warrant-execution purpose and recorded intimate material for public entertainment. Mottola emphasizes the lack of a prior case involving precisely this practice. The strongest constitutional question concerns unnecessary intrusion into a home, not a general prohibition on recording searches for legitimate evidentiary purposes.

**Paths:** Deny dismissal if existing home-search law made the alleged conduct plainly unauthorized. Grant immunity only if the historical law left its legality objectively uncertain at the required level of specificity. Preserve factual defenses and lawful law-enforcement recording.

### Issue 2: Force, property damage, and the photograph

**Law and review:** *Graham v. Connor*, 490 U.S. 386 (1989), and *Rivera v. United States*, 928 F.2d 592 (2d Cir. 1991), require objective reasonableness tied to the search’s purposes and actual circumstances. The plaintiff must establish each defendant’s actionable conduct and injury. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Positions and remedy limits:** Plaintiffs allege needless coercion and damage; Mottola may show necessary, reasonable measures. Those facts cannot be decided against the complaint at dismissal. The photograph is analytically distinct: if reasonably seized to identify the suspected perpetrator, it may fall within legitimate investigative purposes. The court should not declare every act alleged unlawful merely because the media entry was unlawful.

### Issue 3: Prewarrant entry, search, and detention

**Governing law:** *Segura v. United States*, 468 U.S. 796 (1984), distinguishes securing premises from conducting an unauthorized search. It did not validate the earlier unlawful search at issue there. Circuit law, including *United States v. MacDonald*, 916 F.2d 766 (2d Cir. 1990) (en banc), governs exigency. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Positions:** Mottola argues he was not present during initial entry. Plaintiffs point to communications and his possible coordination. Liability requires authorization, direction, or participation, not supervisory title alone. The extent of detention, whether a search began, and whether exigency existed require findings.

**Paths:** Permit fact development on personal participation and the character of the prewarrant activity. Do not turn *Segura* into categorical permission for warrantless home entry whenever an application is pending.

### Issue 4: Substantive due process

**Law and scope:** *Graham* requires use of the specific constitutional provision governing the intrusion. *Albright v. Oliver*, 510 U.S. 266 (1994), is available by the decision cutoff, but its separate rationales must not be represented as a single unqualified majority holding. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/035/35.F3d.680.94-6047.94-6041.1789.html))

**Positions and paths:** Plaintiffs characterize the conduct as conscience-shocking. Mottola argues that the Fourth Amendment supplies the relevant source and that the alleged conduct does not independently cross the due-process threshold. Reject a duplicative or unsupported substantive-due-process route without weakening a viable Fourth Amendment claim.
