Appeal from the final judgment of the United States District Court for the District of Connecticut (T. F. Gilroy Daly, District Judge), entered after a jury trial.

## Decision summary

Vinal S. Duncan was convicted by a jury on four counts: a conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. section 371 (Count One), bank fraud under 18 U.S.C. section 1344 (Count Two), conspiracy to make corrupt payments (Count Four), and corrupt payments under 18 U.S.C. section 666(a)(2) (Count Five). The district court imposed concurrent prison terms, the longest of sixty-five months, and, on the counts governed by the Sentencing Guidelines, applied a four-level increase for Duncan's role as an organizer or leader and a separate two-level increase for perjury.

The Court of Appeals affirmed in all respects. It held, on each of four questions, as follows.

1. Expert testimony. An Internal Revenue Service agent, Thomas Mulligan, testified about financial records, false returns, and the movement of funds. The court held that the challenged parts of his testimony stayed within factual explanation and that admitting them was not an abuse of discretion. In the alternative, the court held that any error was harmless.
2. Ex post facto challenge. Duncan argued that his bank-fraud conduct was completed in 1983, before section 1344 took effect on October 12, 1984. The court rejected the challenge because the April 1985 sale of the second parcel to the bank was an execution of the charged scheme after the statute took effect.
3. Count One. The count stands on the jury's express finding of a bank-fraud object and of an overt act after October 12, 1984. The court did not decide whether the IRS object of the conspiracy is time-barred.
4. Leadership adjustment. The four-level increase under section 3B1.1(a) of the Sentencing Guidelines was not clearly erroneous, and no resentencing is required.

Judges Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone were for affirmance on every component, and none was against. Judge Feinberg wrote the opinion of the court, joined in full by Judges Pierce and Stone. There is no concurrence or dissent. The mandate has not issued.

## The case and the result

**Posture and issues.** Duncan appeals from a judgment of conviction on Counts One, Two, Four, and Five. He seeks reversal or resentencing. The United States seeks affirmance.

**The bank transactions.** Duncan was a director of Security Savings & Loan Association and a member of its loan committee. In 1983 he and other committee members arranged to purchase two parcels on Meriden Road through a nominee, concealing their beneficial ownership from the remaining directors. They used a mortgage financed by the bank, leased property to the bank, and later approved sales of the parcels to the bank without disclosing their interests. The first parcel was sold for $200,000 in October 1984 and the second for $135,000 in April 1985. The transactions produced $135,000 above the initial purchase amount, and the beneficial owners received distributions by checks of less than $10,000.

**The Taft Group.** In 1986 Duncan, Richard Barbieri, and John Corpaci formed the Taft Group. It provided benefits to Waterbury Mayor Joseph Santopietro and his associates in return for favorable official treatment. Barbieri and Corpaci pleaded guilty, cooperated, and described Duncan's knowledge and participation. One transaction was a payment of $63,000 made through a lease of uninhabitable office space. At trial Duncan disputed guilty knowledge and intent. He did not deny that the transactions and corrupt payments occurred.

**The agent's testimony.** Thomas Mulligan, an agent of the Internal Revenue Service, testified about the financial records, the tax system, false returns, and the movement of funds. He had taken part in the investigation and had reviewed the records. Duncan objected that portions of the testimony supplied legal conclusions.

**The verdict.** The jury convicted on Counts One, Two, Four, and Five. Its verdict specifically identified a bank-fraud object of the Count One conspiracy and an overt act after October 12, 1984, the date on which section 1344 took effect.

**Jurisdiction.** The district court had jurisdiction under 18 U.S.C. section 3231. Appellate jurisdiction rests on 28 U.S.C. section 1291 and, for the sentencing challenge, 18 U.S.C. section 3742. The papers fixing the date of entry of judgment and the date of the notice of appeal are not among the materials before the court. Neither party presents a question about timeliness, and the court decides none.

**The issues and the standard of review for each.**

1. Whether portions of Mulligan's testimony crossed from expert explanation into legal instruction, and if so whether the error was harmless. Admission of expert testimony is reviewed for abuse of discretion, with independent review of the boundary between permissible assistance and an impermissible legal conclusion. Preserved nonconstitutional error in a criminal trial is reviewed under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a) and Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946).
2. Whether section 1344 was applied to an offense completed before the statute took effect. Ex post facto and statutory questions receive independent review, and the court takes the verdict and the evidence as they stand.
3. Whether the Count One conviction survives the argument that the IRS object of the conspiracy is untimely, given the verdict's express identification of a bank-fraud object. The legal effect of the alternative bases is reviewed independently.
4. Whether the four-level leadership adjustment is supported. The role finding is reviewed for clear error and the meaning of the guideline independently.

The relevant dates are the effective date of section 1344 (October 12, 1984), the two sales (October 1984 and April 1985), the formation of the Taft Group (1986), the argument (April 13, 1994), and this decision (December 2, 1994).

**Judgment.** The judgment of the district court is affirmed in all respects. By component:

1. Expert testimony. The admission of the challenged testimony was within the district court's discretion; alternatively, any error was harmless. Affirmed. For: Judges Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone. Against: none.
2. Ex post facto challenge to the bank-fraud convictions. The challenge is rejected. Affirmed. For: Judges Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone. Against: none.
3. Count One. The count is sustained on the jury's express bank-fraud finding, and the limitations argument directed at the IRS object is not decided. Affirmed. For: Judges Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone. Against: none.
4. Leadership adjustment. The four-level increase under section 3B1.1(a) is affirmed, without resentencing. For: Judges Feinberg, Pierce, and Stone. Against: none.

Duncan's issues are the four above; none is directed specifically at Counts Four and Five apart from the evidentiary objection in component 1. The convictions on Counts One, Two, Four, and Five and the sentence stand.

Opinion and joins: One opinion of the court, by Judge Feinberg, joined by Judge Pierce and Judge Stone as to every Part (I.A, I.B, I.C, II, III, and IV). No concurring or dissenting opinion is filed, and no judge qualifies a join.

### I. The testimony of Agent Mulligan

*A. The governing line.* Rule 702 of the Federal Rules of Evidence admits the opinion of a qualified witness when specialized knowledge will assist the jury, and Rule 704(a) provides that an opinion is not objectionable merely because it embraces an ultimate issue the jury must decide. Neither rule makes a witness a source of law for the jury. We have held that Rule 704 was not intended to allow experts to offer opinions embodying legal conclusions. United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135, 139-42 (2d Cir.), modified, 856 F.2d 5 (1988); see United States v. Bilzerian, 926 F.2d 1285, 1294 (2d Cir. 1991); Hygh v. Jacobs, 961 F.2d 359, 363-65 (2d Cir. 1992) (civil action; expert testimony that expresses a legal conclusion must be excluded). The line separates a factual conclusion that helps the jury understand the evidence, even on a matter the jury must decide, from an opinion that instructs the jury on the governing law or announces that the accused committed the charged offense. Scop also holds that an expert may not rest an opinion on his own assessment of other witnesses' credibility. Mulligan was not simply assessing the credibility of witnesses from their trial testimony, so that part of Scop is not at issue, and we do not extend it.

Three points about the line decide this appeal. First, it is functional. What matters is what the testimony conveys to the jury, not whether the witness says the word "guilty." A label that names an offense can carry an official legal judgment, and the fact that it names an uncharged offense, such as money laundering here, does not make it admissible. Second, the witness's status is not an exemption. That an agent took part in the investigation and reviewed the records bears on his foundation and on the context in which the jury hears him. See United States v. Schwartz, 924 F.2d 410, 425-26 (2d Cir. 1991) (agent testified both as an expert and as a witness to the investigation; his opinion explaining why the investigation continued did not offend Scop). It does not authorize him to apply the criminal law for the jury. Schwartz examined the testimony in context and holds nothing broader. Third, the rule does not bar what Rule 702 invites. An agent may organize a complicated financial record, identify discrepancies in filings, explain why a return or filing was false, trace the movement of funds, and describe how false filings and transactions bore on the administration of the tax system.

*B. Application.* Duncan's objection is aimed at references to money laundering and to filings that obstructed the IRS. He argues that they carried an official legal judgment even though Mulligan never said that Duncan was guilty. The government answers that Mulligan organized a complicated record and explained, from his investigation and his review of the documents, why particular filings were false, and that he did not tell the jury that Duncan was guilty of bank fraud or of either conspiracy.

Duncan is right on the principle. Omitting the word "guilty" would not save testimony that in substance told the jury that the transactions were legally money laundering or that the filings legally amounted to a charged offense. But considered in the context of the testimony of which they formed a part, the references he challenges did not do that. They explained what the documents showed, how the funds moved, and how false filings affected the working of the tax system. They did not instruct the jury on the elements of any count, and they did not announce that Duncan had the criminal intent the government had to prove or that he had committed any charged offense. An agent's statement that falsified filings interfered with the assessment and collection of tax describes an effect on tax administration that lies within the knowledge of an IRS agent. A statement that the accused thereby committed the charged conspiracy would cross the line. The challenged testimony stayed on the permitted side, and admitting it was not an abuse of discretion. We rest on what the testimony did in this trial. We do not hold that a government witness may use legal labels because he investigated the case, knows the participants, or names an uncharged offense, and testimony of this kind will not always survive scrutiny.

*C. Harmlessness, in the alternative.* Even if some phrase went beyond explanation, the judgment would stand. Duncan objected, so a nonconstitutional error is reviewed under Rule 52(a) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which directs that an error not affecting substantial rights be disregarded, and under Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750 (1946). The question under Kotteakos is whether we can say "with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." This court has put the same inquiry as whether it is highly probable that the error did not contribute to the verdict, United States v. Castro, 813 F.2d 571, 577 (2d Cir. 1987), and as whether the error had a substantial and injurious effect or influence in determining the jury's verdict, United States v. Teitler, 802 F.2d 606, 616 (2d Cir. 1986).

Three features of the inquiry matter here. The burden does not rest on Duncan. Because his objection was preserved, the government, as the beneficiary of the error, must persuade us that it was harmless, and it cannot do so by observing that he has not proved prejudice. Cf. United States v. Olano, 507 U.S. 725 (1993) (for forfeited error under Rule 52(b), the defendant bears the burden of persuasion on prejudice, a burden shifting that distinguishes Rule 52(b) from Rule 52(a)). Nor does the civil harmless-error rule, Fed. R. Civ. P. 61, or any civil authority applying it, supply the measure in a criminal trial. Second, the sufficiency of the remaining evidence is not the test. A verdict that enough evidence could support may still have been swayed by an error, and the question is the error's actual influence in this trial. Third, the court looks at the whole record, including how the challenged material figured in the case.

Measured that way, any error was harmless. The challenged references were a limited part of testimony that otherwise explained documents and transactions. Duncan did not deny at trial that the transactions and corrupt payments occurred; he disputed knowledge and intent. Whatever their precise legal flavor, the references characterized what the financial records showed. They did not supply proof of what Duncan knew or intended, and the testimony did not announce it. The proof on those contested questions came from Barbieri and Corpaci, participants who pleaded guilty, cooperated, and described Duncan's knowledge and participation, and from the documentary record of the transactions themselves. Compare United States v. Castillo, No. 92-1279 (2d Cir. Jan. 28, 1994) (Part II.B) (officers' repetition of a sole eyewitness's account of a firearm, used in summation to corroborate that contested fact, was not harmless under Kotteakos). The error in Castillo bore on a contested fact and was used to prove it; the references here did not bear on knowledge and intent. We are satisfied that the verdict was not substantially swayed by them. We reach that conclusion on the influence of the challenged material, not on the sufficiency of the other evidence and not on any failure by Duncan to show prejudice.

### II. Section 1344 and the Ex Post Facto Clause

Duncan argues that the conduct underlying his bank-fraud convictions was completed in 1983, before 18 U.S.C. section 1344 took effect on October 12, 1984, so that Count Two, and the bank-fraud object of Count One, punish a completed pre-enactment offense. We review the constitutional and statutory question independently. We do not substitute a different factual theory for the one the jury's verdict and the evidence support.

The rule. The Ex Post Facto Clause, U.S. Const. art. I, section 9, cl. 3, forbids applying section 1344 to an offense completed before the statute took effect, and a conviction may not rest on conduct that predates the effective date. United States v. Torres, 901 F.2d 205, 226 (2d Cir. 1990) (ex post facto error where the instruction and verdict form let the jury apply a statute on the basis of conduct before its effective date). The Clause does not bar application of a statute to criminal activity that continues after enactment. United States v. Ferrara, 458 F.2d 868, 874 (2d Cir. 1972) (conspiracy and overt acts continued long after the amendment). Section 1344 punishes one who knowingly executes, or attempts to execute, a scheme or artifice to defraud a financial institution. Two qualifications limit the reach of a continuing-scheme theory. Later acts of concealment after the central objectives of a crime or conspiracy have been attained do not extend it, United States v. Fletcher, 928 F.2d 495, 499 (2d Cir. 1991); United States v. Knuckles, 581 F.2d 305, 313 (2d Cir. 1978), and the lingering effects of a completed wrong do not either. An agreement whose object includes receipt of anticipated profit, by contrast, continues until the profit is realized. Fletcher, 928 F.2d at 500; Knuckles, 581 F.2d at 313.

The contentions. Duncan characterizes the wrong as the appropriation of a corporate opportunity in 1983, which later transactions and concealment could not turn into a new federal crime. The government's charged theory was broader in a way that matters legally: a purchase through a concealed nominee, followed by a profitable resale to the bank.

The evidence fits the government's theory. The parcels were bought through a nominee, with ownership hidden from the other directors and with financing from the bank, and the participants then approved the bank's purchases without disclosing their interests. The premium of $135,000 over the purchase amount was the product of those sales, and the beneficial owners received it. The resale to the bank was the means by which the scheme drew money from the bank. It was not a later effort to conceal a crime already complete, and it was not merely a lingering effect of the 1983 acquisition. The second parcel was sold to the bank in April 1985, months after section 1344 took effect, and that sale was an execution of the scheme within the statute. For Count One the jury also expressly found an overt act after October 12, 1984. We rest on the April 1985 sale and on that finding. We do not rely on the October 1984 sale, because the materials before us do not place it before or after October 12. We do not rely on any later agreement or effort to conceal the scheme, and we hold neither that concealment extends a fraud indefinitely nor that every later economic effect is an execution. Because the convictions do not depend solely on the 1983 acquisition and a post-enactment execution is shown, Duncan's temporal objection fails.

### III. Count One and the IRS object

Duncan argues that the IRS object of the Count One conspiracy is untimely and that this defect undermines the whole count. The jury's verdict, however, specifically identified the bank-fraud object and found an overt act after October 12, 1984. We hold that this express finding supplies a valid basis for Count One independent of the IRS object, and we therefore do not decide whether the IRS object is time-barred.

The rule. Griffin v. United States, 502 U.S. 46, 49-60 (1991), distinguishes a theory the evidence fails to support from a theory the law forbids. Jurors are equipped to decide whether the facts support a theory, so a general verdict on a multiple-object conspiracy stands if the evidence supports one object, even if it is inadequate as to another. When a possible ground is legally unavailable, the verdict may have to be set aside because one cannot tell whether the jury relied on it. Griffin is therefore not a rule that one valid object always cures another that is legally unavailable, and we do not read it that way. The question is whether a valid basis is shown by what this jury actually decided, without presuming how a silent verdict was reached.

Application. This is not a silent general verdict. The jury identified the bank-fraud object of the conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. section 371 and found an overt act after the statute's effective date. Part II holds that a bank-fraud object reaching the post-enactment sale to the bank is legally valid. A valid basis for Count One is therefore ascertainable from the verdict itself. Duncan answers that an untimely IRS object taints a general conspiracy conviction. That would matter if the verdict could have rested on the IRS object alone, or if the bank-fraud basis could not be told apart from it. Neither is so here. We leave undecided the limitations argument directed at the IRS object, and we leave undecided any broader proposition that Griffin cures every legally invalid alternative.

### IV. The leadership adjustment

Section 3B1.1(a) of the Sentencing Guidelines provides a four-level increase for a defendant who was an organizer or leader of criminal activity that involved five or more participants or was otherwise extensive. Duncan does not challenge the size or extensiveness predicate. He denies that he was a leader. The unchallenged perjury adjustment is not before us.

The standard. A district court's finding of a defendant's role under section 3B1.1 is a factual determination reviewed for clear error. United States v. Garcia, 936 F.2d 648, 656 (2d Cir. 1991); United States v. Beaulieau, 959 F.2d 375, 380 (2d Cir. 1992). We interpret the guideline independently. We follow United States v. Castillo, No. 92-1279 (2d Cir. Jan. 28, 1994) (Part VI), a published decision of this court, for the standard, the burden, and the inquiry: the government must establish the organizing or supervisory facts by a preponderance of the evidence; possession of proceeds is not by itself a basis for the adjustment; and the court looks to the discretion exercised, the nature and degree of the defendant's role in planning or organizing the offense, and the control and authority held over other participants. See also Beaulieau, 959 F.2d at 380 (control and authority over others, discretion, and role in planning). Castillo was a case under subsection (c), is confined to its record, and states no general rule about role enhancements, and we rely on it for no more. Under that inquiry a corporate title cannot substitute for actual authority, planning, or direction. Nor does the possibility that others also led weaken a finding about Duncan: there can be more than one leader or organizer of a criminal enterprise. Garcia, 936 F.2d at 656.

The contentions and application. Duncan argues that other participants controlled the deals and that his position does not establish criminal leadership. The government points to repeated planning, central involvement in the real estate ventures, the testimony of the participants who pleaded guilty, and the sham office lease as showing actual direction beyond a title. The question is whether the evidence permitted the sentencing court to find that Duncan organized or led, not whether we would rank the participants differently. It did. Duncan did not merely hold a position. In the bank transactions he and other committee members arranged the purchase of the parcels through a nominee, hid the beneficial ownership from the remaining directors, and later approved the sales to the bank without disclosing their interests. In the Taft Group he joined Barbieri and Corpaci in forming the enterprise in 1986, and Barbieri and Corpaci described his knowledge and participation in the arrangements by which benefits went to the Waterbury mayor and his associates, including the $63,000 payment made through the lease of uninhabitable office space. The sentencing court was entitled to credit that account over Duncan's denial that he led, and the fact that Barbieri, Corpaci, and others also exercised authority does not negate his. We see no indication that the finding rested only on a title or on Duncan's receipt of proceeds. The finding is not clearly erroneous, and the adjustment stands. No resentencing is required.

### Matters left open

The opinion does not decide: the timeliness of the appeal, whose filing dates are not before us; whether the IRS object of Count One is time-barred; whether Griffin cures every legally invalid alternative; whether any government witness may ever use a legal label, or whether Scop's credibility holding extends to an agent who also testifies to the investigation; whether concealment or later economic effects can ever extend a section 1344 offense; the date of the October 1984 sale relative to October 12, 1984; whether the size or extensiveness predicate of section 3B1.1(a) is met, which is unchallenged; the perjury adjustment; and any rule for role enhancements other than the standard, burden, and inquiry stated in Castillo, Part VI.

## What the decision does

**Controlling holdings, by Part and joins.**

- Part I.A and I.B (Feinberg, Pierce, Stone): Rules 702 and 704(a) admit an investigating agent's explanation of records, fund movements, false filings, and their effect on tax administration, but not instruction on the governing law or a pronouncement of guilt, and the agent's role is no exemption (Scop, Bilzerian, Hygh, Schwartz). The test is functional, applied to the testimony in context; review is for abuse of discretion with independent review of the boundary. Applied: the challenged references stayed within factual explanation.
- Part I.C (Feinberg, Pierce, Stone), alternative and independent ground: preserved nonconstitutional evidentiary error in a criminal trial is reviewed under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos; the government bears the burden; the question is the error's influence in this trial, not sufficiency and not the civil rule. Applied: any error was harmless.
- Part II (Feinberg, Pierce, Stone): the Ex Post Facto Clause bars applying section 1344 to an offense completed before October 12, 1984 but not to a scheme executed after; concealment and lingering effects do not alone extend a crime. Applied: the April 1985 sale to the bank executed the charged scheme.
- Part III (Feinberg, Pierce, Stone): a jury's express identification of a bank-fraud object and a post-enactment overt act supplies a valid basis for the conspiracy count independent of the challenged IRS object; Griffin is not a universal one-valid-object cure.
- Part IV (Feinberg, Pierce, Stone): role findings under section 3B1.1(a) are reviewed for clear error and the guideline independently; the government's burden is a preponderance; title or receipt of proceeds alone does not suffice; evidence of planning, concealment from the other directors, formation of the Taft Group, and the sham lease supported the finding.

**Precedent and legal effect.** This is a published opinion of the court, joined by all three judges, and it is precedent in this circuit to the extent of the holdings stated in Parts I through IV and summarized above. It follows Scop, Bilzerian, and Hygh, Kotteakos and Rule 52(a), Torres and Ferrara, Griffin, and Castillo, Part VI (for the standard, burden, and inquiry on role findings only). It cites Castillo, Part II.B, only by comparison. The harmlessness holding is an alternative ground, independent of the ruling that there was no error. No earlier decision of this court is overruled, displaced, or narrowed.

The statements that the October 1984 sale and later concealment are not relied on, and the matters left open above, mark the limits of the holdings and are not rulings. The affirmance of Duncan's convictions and sentence is party-specific relief. The characterization of the agent's testimony is a ruling on this trial record and is not a rule for other records.

**Effect on the parties.** The judgment of the district court is affirmed. There is no new trial, no resentencing, and no remand. Duncan's convictions on Counts One, Two, Four, and Five and his sentence stand.
