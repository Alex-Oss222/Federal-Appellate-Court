### Issue 1: Custody at commencement and legal expiration of supervised release

**Preservation and reach:** The district court dismissed on custody grounds. The record’s “probation” label and physical release date do not conclusively determine the legal term.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of custody jurisdiction and sentence construction; clear-error review of supported historical findings.

**Governing test and burdens:** Section 2255 requires custody under the challenged sentence when the proceeding begins. Supervised release can qualify; collateral consequences of a fully expired sentence cannot. A properly commenced case survives later release.

Section 2255 requires the movant to be in custody under the challenged federal sentence. *United States v. Brilliant*, 274 F.2d 618, 620 (2d Cir. 1960), treats that requirement as jurisdictional. *Carafas v. LaVallee*, 391 U.S. 234, 238 (1968), preserves a properly commenced case after release; it does not dispense with custody at commencement. *Maleng v. Cook*, 490 U.S. 488, 491-92 (1989), prevents collateral consequences of a fully expired sentence from substituting for the required restraint. Supervision can qualify as custody under the principle of *Jones v. Cunningham*, 371 U.S. 236 (1963), without physical imprisonment.

The record also must distinguish actual termination of imprisonment and commencement of supervised release from a temporary physical release that left an earlier custodial term legally running. *Stabile v. United States Bureau of Prisons*, 834 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1987), concerns the relationship between parole supervision and a sentence; it does not by itself resolve the supervised-release calculation. Neither a conclusory label in the district order nor an unexplained assumption that every early release shortens the total sentence should replace the applicable legal calculation.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Jones v. Cunningham, 371 U.S. 236 (1963), restraint outside prison can be custody; Carafas v. LaVallee, 391 U.S. 234, 238 (1968), preserves jurisdiction after release in a properly filed case; Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488, 491-92 (1989), excludes mere collateral consequences after full expiration.

**Other governing authority:** 28 U.S.C. § 2255 as then in force; United States v. Brilliant, 274 F.2d 618, 620 (2d Cir. 1960); Stabile v. United States Bureau of Prisons, 834 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1987), concerning parole in its own setting; 18 U.S.C. § 3624(e) requires attention to the legal commencement of supervised release.

### Issue 2: Holiday computation under Rule 6(a)

**Preservation and reach:** Scanio invoked the court’s closure and Rule 6(a); this is distinct from a newly formulated constructive-tender theory.

**Standard of review:** Independent interpretation of Rules 6(a), 82, and § 2255.

**Governing test and burdens:** Rule 6(a) computes periods; it does not automatically prolong a terminated custodial status. Rule 82 prevents using procedure to enlarge substantive jurisdiction. Authorities allowing computation of jurisdictional deadlines must be distinguished from continuing a legal status.

The government's strongest argument is that custody is a status, not an ordinary deadline. Rule 6(a) computes filing periods; Rule 82 forbids enlarging jurisdiction through the civil rules. If the first legally effective filing occurred November 29 and every restraint under the sentence had already ended, treating the motion as merely timely would not answer the absence of custody.

Scanio's strongest response is that the legal system itself made the designated filing office inaccessible while he still possessed the required status. *Union National Bank v. Lamb*, 337 U.S. 38, 41 (1949), and *Simon v. Commissioner*, 176 F.2d 230, 232 (2d Cir. 1949), show that the word “jurisdictional” does not invariably defeat ordinary computation rules. Those decisions concern time periods, however, and therefore do not themselves establish that a terminated custody relationship continues through a holiday.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Union National Bank v. Lamb, 337 U.S. 38, 41 (1949), concerns computation of a period, not automatic extension of custody.

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Civ. P. 6(a), 82; Simon v. Commissioner, 176 F.2d 230, 232 (2d Cir. 1949); § 2255 custody requirement.
