# Scanio v. United States

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 94-2002. **Decision date:** October 11, 1994. **Citation:** 37 F.3d 858. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Chief Judge Michael A. Telesca. **Historical argument:** May 26, 1994. The appeal concerns the custody requirement for a federal prisoner's § 2255 motion, not the later-enacted one-year collateral-review limitation. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/37/858/509353/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Scanio was convicted in 1988 of structuring a currency transaction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5324(3) and 5322(a), and received one year of imprisonment followed by two years of supervised release. His conviction was affirmed in *United States v. Scanio*, 900 F.2d 485 (2d Cir. 1990). He began imprisonment November 30, 1990, transferred to a Salvation Army halfway house on July 29, 1991, and was released from that facility on November 27, 1991, immediately before Thanksgiving.

The district court treated supervised release as beginning on November 27, 1991 and expiring at midnight on November 26, 1993. The later disagreement concerned both the adequacy of that determination and the significance of the court's closure. The difference between supervised release and old-law parole matters; one cannot automatically transfer a rule about the duration of a parole sentence to a separately imposed term of supervised release.

As described in the appellate record account, counsel went to the federal courthouse on Friday, November 26, 1993 to file Scanio's petition, but was informed that the clerk's office was closed by the chief judge's order for the extended Thanksgiving holiday. Counsel returned and filed on Monday, November 29, the first reopening day. The motion alleged ineffective assistance of trial counsel. The district court dismissed it in a one-page order because Scanio was no longer in custody when it was docketed. Its reference to “probation” did not accurately identify the supervised-release term actually imposed.

This was a challenge to the federal conviction. The published opinion's isolated reference to a “state court trial” should not be repeated as an additional, unverified state prosecution. No merits determination of the ineffective-assistance claim is before this panel. *Scanio*, 37 F.3d at 859-62.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The dismissal is a final collateral judgment appealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291 and the governing § 2255 appellate framework. Whether the district court had power to adjudicate the motion is a question of law; supported historical facts receive clear-error review. The court cannot assume § 2255 jurisdiction because the underlying claim is important.

The preserved dispute includes the expiration of custody and the effect of closure under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 6(a). A distinct constructive-filing theory would be a proposed refinement, not an argument that may silently be attributed to counsel. Before adopting it, the court must determine that it is properly reachable and afford both parties an opportunity to address its legal and factual predicates.

A dismissal for lack of custody does not answer whether another collateral vehicle is available. Any request for coram nobis would require the separate showing recognized in *United States v. Morgan*, 346 U.S. 502 (1954). It cannot be granted automatically by renaming an unsuccessful § 2255 motion.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

Section 2255 requires the movant to be in custody under the challenged federal sentence. *United States v. Brilliant*, 274 F.2d 618, 620 (2d Cir. 1960), treats that requirement as jurisdictional. *Carafas v. LaVallee*, 391 U.S. 234, 238 (1968), preserves a properly commenced case after release; it does not dispense with custody at commencement. *Maleng v. Cook*, 490 U.S. 488, 491-92 (1989), prevents collateral consequences of a fully expired sentence from substituting for the required restraint. Supervision can qualify as custody under the principle of *Jones v. Cunningham*, 371 U.S. 236 (1963), without physical imprisonment.

The government's strongest argument is that custody is a status, not an ordinary deadline. Rule 6(a) computes filing periods; Rule 82 forbids enlarging jurisdiction through the civil rules. If the first legally effective filing occurred November 29 and every restraint under the sentence had already ended, treating the motion as merely timely would not answer the absence of custody.

Scanio's strongest response is that the legal system itself made the designated filing office inaccessible while he still possessed the required status. *Union National Bank v. Lamb*, 337 U.S. 38, 41 (1949), and *Simon v. Commissioner*, 176 F.2d 230, 232 (2d Cir. 1949), show that the word “jurisdictional” does not invariably defeat ordinary computation rules. Those decisions concern time periods, however, and therefore do not themselves establish that a terminated custody relationship continues through a holiday.

A different question is when a completed application is treated as lodged. *Houston v. Lack*, 487 U.S. 266 (1988), recognizes filing upon delivery to prison authorities for forwarding in its particular setting. It does not control this represented applicant's circumstances: counsel was not a pro se prisoner dependent on the prison mail system. Nevertheless, it demonstrates that identifying the legally operative filing event is analytically distinct from enlarging a court's substantive jurisdiction. Any extension to an actual courthouse tender frustrated solely by official closure must be defended as a new rule on an open question, with administrable limits.

The record also must distinguish actual termination of imprisonment and commencement of supervised release from a temporary physical release that left an earlier custodial term legally running. *Stabile v. United States Bureau of Prisons*, 834 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1987), concerns the relationship between parole supervision and a sentence; it does not by itself resolve the supervised-release calculation. Neither a conclusory label in the district order nor an unexplained assumption that every early release shortens the total sentence should replace the applicable legal calculation.
