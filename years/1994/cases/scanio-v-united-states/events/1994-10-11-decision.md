### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** Scanio appeals from a one-page order of the district court dismissing his motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255, filed November 29, 1993, on the ground that he was no longer in custody when the motion was docketed. The record before the court does not state the date on which the order was entered. The motion challenges the federal conviction and alleges that Scanio's trial counsel rendered ineffective assistance. The merits of that claim are not before this court.

**Material record.** In 1988 Scanio was convicted of structuring a currency transaction under 31 U.S.C. §§ 5324(3) and 5322(a) and was sentenced to one year of imprisonment followed by two years of supervised release. The conviction was affirmed in United States v. Scanio, 900 F.2d 485 (2d Cir. 1990). Review standard: independent as to custody jurisdiction and construction, clear error as to supported findings. Application: the order rested on the label "probation" and on the November 27, 1991 release; neither establishes the legal term; the margin (three days on the district court's own calculation) makes the gap material; vacatur and remand for a supported determination follows. **Opinion of the court.**

STONE, Circuit Judge:

Charles D. Scanio appeals from an order of the United States District Court for the Western District of New York (Michael A. Telesca, Chief Judge) dismissing his motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255 on the ground that he was no longer in custody when the motion was docketed on Monday, November 29, 1993. The district court treated Scanio's two-year term of supervised release as having begun on November 27, 1991 and as having expired at midnight on November 26, 1993, the Friday after Thanksgiving, when the clerk's office was closed by order of the chief judge. We hold in Part II that the record does not establish when that term legally began and ended, so that the dismissal cannot stand as entered. We hold in Part III that Rule 6(a) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure does not, by its own force, continue a sentence that has fully expired until the next day on which the clerk's office is open. We decline in Part IV to adopt on this record a rule treating a motion as lodged on the day counsel unsuccessfully tried to file it, and direct instead that the question be taken up on notice to both parties, and on findings, if it is reached. The order of dismissal is vacated and the matter is remanded. We express no view on the merits of Scanio's claim of ineffective assistance of trial counsel.

#### I. Background, jurisdiction and review

In 1988 Scanio was convicted of structuring a currency transaction, in violation of 31 U.S.C. §§ 5324(3) and 5322(a). He was sentenced to one year of imprisonment, to be followed by two years of supervised release. This court affirmed the conviction. United States v. Scanio, 900 F.2d 485 (2d Cir. 1990). Scanio began serving his term of imprisonment on November 30, 1990. On July 29, 1991 he was transferred to a halfway house operated by the Salvation Army, and on November 27, 1991, immediately before Thanksgiving, he was released from that facility.

The district court treated the term of supervised release as running from November 27, 1991 to midnight on November 26, 1993. The account in the record of what followed is short. Counsel went to the federal courthouse on Friday, November 26, 1993 to file Scanio's motion and was told that the clerk's office was closed by order of the chief judge for the extended Thanksgiving holiday. Counsel returned and filed on Monday, November 29, the first day the office reopened. The motion, which attacks the federal conviction, alleges that trial counsel was ineffective. The district court dismissed it in a one-page order because Scanio was no longer in custody when it was docketed. The order refers to "probation." The sentence imposed was supervised release.

Several matters are not established. No finding states whether counsel had the completed motion with him on November 26, what he presented at the clerk's office, what the closure order provided, or what other means of filing, if any, were open that day. And the order identifies no sentencing or release document showing how the November 27 release relates to the legal expiration of the one-year term of imprisonment that began on November 30, 1990.

The order is a final disposition of a collateral motion and is appealable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The notice of appeal and docket receipts are not among the materials before us, and we have made no finding about the date of the notice. The appeal was argued by both sides on the custody question, nothing before us suggests that it is untimely, and we proceed on that footing. Whether the district court had power to hear the motion is a question of law, as is the construction of the sentence, and we review both independently. Findings of fact that the record supports are reviewed for clear error. The meaning of Rules 6(a) and 82 and of § 2255 is likewise reviewed independently. Whether and when a paper is treated as filed is a legal question, but the facts on which the answer might turn are for the district court; an appellate court does not find them or supply a finding that is missing.

#### II. Custody and the legal term of supervised release

Section 2255 is a remedy for a prisoner in custody under sentence of a federal court. We have treated that requirement as jurisdictional. United States v. Brilliant, 274 F.2d 618, 620 (2d Cir. 1960). Custody is measured when the motion is made. A person who has completely served his sentence when he moves is not in custody because of the lingering consequences of the conviction. Maleng v. Cook, 490 U.S. 488, 491-92 (1989). But a person who is in custody when he moves does not lose his case because he is released while it is pending. Carafas v. LaVallee, 391 U.S. 234, 238 (1968). Custody does not require confinement; restraint short of imprisonment may suffice. Jones v. Cunningham, 371 U.S. 236 (1963). A term of supervised release imposed as part of a sentence can therefore be custody under that sentence. Equity cannot supply the condition. The importance of a claim, and sympathy for the movant, do not enlarge the class of persons whom the statute reaches.

Scanio therefore had to be under the sentence on the day his motion was made. Whether he was depends on when his imprisonment legally ended, when supervised release legally began, and when the two-year term expired. Section 3624(e) of Title 18 makes the legal commencement of supervised release the operative fact. Those dates are fixed by the sentence and by the legal arrangement under which Scanio was released, not by the date on which he walked out of a particular building. Nor may a rule developed for another kind of sentence be carried over without examination. Stabile v. United States Bureau of Prisons, 834 F.2d 305 (2d Cir. 1987), concerned the relation between parole supervision and a sentence; it does not by itself resolve how a term of supervised release, which is imposed separately from the term of imprisonment, is to be calculated.

The district court's order does not meet that standard. It rests on a label and on a date. The label is inaccurate: "probation" is not the term imposed, and the differences among probation, parole and supervised release matter to how each is calculated. A misdescription of that kind would not by itself require vacatur if the calculation beneath it were sound and shown. But the order does not show it. Its other foundation, the physical release from the halfway house on November 27, 1991, is an event whose legal significance the order does not explain. It may mark the point at which imprisonment legally ended and supervision began. It may instead record an interim or early release that left the term of imprisonment legally running to a later date, in which case supervised release began later and expired later. The record does not show how the release date relates to the day on which the one-year term legally expired, taking account of any credit and the terms of the placement, or how the first and last days of the two-year term were counted. We cannot assume that every early physical release shortens the total sentence, and the district court could not assume it either.

The gap is material. On the district court's own calculation the term ended three days before the motion was docketed. A difference of a few days in the legal commencement of supervised release, or in the way the term is counted, could decide whether the motion was made in custody. The missing determination depends on documents and findings that an appellate court cannot create.

The strongest contrary position, which our dissenting colleague adopts, is that custody is tested when the motion is made, that supervised release begins when imprisonment ends, that time in a halfway house is part of the term of imprisonment so that the November 27 release started the two-year term, and that the reference to "probation" is therefore an immaterial slip. If the sentence and release documents show that imprisonment legally ended on November 27, 1991, that conclusion will follow, and the district court's calculation will stand. But it is a conclusion to be reached from the legal arrangement, and the order did not reach it that way. We do not hold that the halfway-house placement, or the release from it, has any particular legal significance. We hold that its significance must be determined and that a dismissal resting on an unexamined inference from the date of physical release cannot be sustained.

We therefore hold that a district court that dismisses a § 2255 motion for want of custody must ground its determination in the legal commencement and expiration of the term, established from the sentence and the legal release arrangement, and not in an inaccurate label or an unexamined inference from the date of physical release. Where the record does not establish those dates and the difference is material, vacatur and remand for a supported determination is the proper course. We do not decide when Scanio's term began or ended.

#### III. Rule 6(a)

Scanio argues that, because the clerk's office was closed on November 26 by order of the court's own chief judge, Rule 6(a) should carry his time to the following Monday, so that the motion docketed on November 29 was made while he was still in custody.

Rule 6(a) prescribes how to compute a period of time prescribed or allowed by the rules, by court order or by statute when its last day falls on a weekend or legal holiday and, since 1985, when the act to be done is the filing of a paper and the last day is one on which weather or other conditions have made the clerk's office inaccessible. The Federal Rules, however, do not extend or limit the jurisdiction of the United States district courts. Fed. R. Civ. P. 82.

Scanio is right that calling a requirement jurisdictional does not by itself place it beyond ordinary computation rules. In Union National Bank v. Lamb, 337 U.S. 38, 41 (1949), the Supreme Court applied the liberality of Rule 6(a) to the statutory time limit for taking an appeal to that Court, and in Simon v. Commissioner, 176 F.2d 230, 232 (2d Cir. 1949), this court applied the rule to the three-month statutory period for seeking review of a Tax Court decision. Both decisions concern periods within which an act must be done. They show that a deadline is not immune from Rule 6(a) because it is jurisdictional. They do not show that a status the statute requires continues because a clerk's office is closed.

Custody is a status. Section 2255, as it stood in 1993, did not fix a period within which a motion had to be made; the statute asked whether the movant was in custody under the sentence. There is no period for Rule 6(a) to compute. The question is not whether a deadline was met on the next business day but whether a sentence that had fully run was still running. A rule that kept a person in custody solely to preserve access to a forum would alter the statutory condition itself, not the manner of satisfying it, and would use a rule of procedure to enlarge the court's power, which Rule 82 forbids. Maleng and Carafas point the same way: the consequences of a completed sentence do not substitute for custody, and the principle that release after a proper commencement does not defeat the case presupposes custody when the case was commenced.

We hold that Rule 6(a), and the next-business-day principle it embodies, does not by its own force continue a fully expired sentence. We need not decide whether the closure on November 26 was a condition that made the clerk's office inaccessible within the meaning of the rule, because even if it was, the rule governs the computation of periods and not the duration of a sentence.

Scanio's response is that the court's own closure should not cost him a channel that was open to him while he held the required status. The equities of that argument are real. But equity cannot lengthen a sentence, and the closure did not extend Scanio's term. This Part decides only that Rule 6(a) cannot supply the custody the statute requires. It does not decide whether the motion was made while custody existed. That question is distinct, and Part IV addresses it.

#### IV. The closure, counsel's attempt to file, and further proceedings

Scanio's reliance on the closure and on Rule 6(a) is preserved. A different theory, that a completed motion actually presented at the clerk's office on November 26 should be treated as lodged on that day, is suggested by counsel's reported visit, but it is not shown to have been presented in those terms to the district court or developed in the submissions here. The theory would not extend custody. It would identify November 26, when on the district court's own calculation the term was still running, as the day on which the motion was made, and so would preserve the requirement that custody and filing coincide.

The law recognizes that the legally operative filing event need not always be the later docket entry. In Houston v. Lack, 487 U.S. 266 (1988), the Court held that a pro se prisoner's notice of appeal is filed when it is delivered to prison authorities for forwarding to the court. Houston rests on the prisoner's dependence on those authorities, and it does not control here. Scanio had counsel, who was not dependent on any prison mail system and who, on the account we have, went to the courthouse himself. Houston is nonetheless instructive, because it shows that deciding when a paper was filed is a different question from enlarging a court's power. Extending the principle to a represented movant whose completed motion was refused at the clerk's office solely because of an official closure would be a new rule. It would have to be justified openly and bounded by administrable limits, and it could not be adopted without a determination that the question is properly reached and without giving both parties a fair opportunity to address it.

The record does not permit that now. There is no finding that counsel had the completed motion on November 26, that he presented it at the place designated for filing, that the closure alone prevented its receipt, or that no other means of lodging it was available. An appellate court does not find those facts, and the government has not had an opportunity to meet the theory in the form in which it would have to be decided. We therefore do not adopt a constructive-lodging rule, and we do not extend Houston. We do not assume that a visit to the courthouse, an intention to file, or papers prepared elsewhere amounts to a filing. We hold only that the question, if it must be reached, should be taken up on notice to both parties and on findings if the facts are disputed.

The remand follows from the structure of the case. The district court must first determine, from the sentence and the legal release arrangement, when Scanio's term of supervised release legally began and expired. If the term was running when the motion was docketed on November 29, 1993, the motion was made in custody, and Scanio's later release does not defeat it. Carafas, 391 U.S. at 238. The district court may then address the motion's remaining requirements and its merits, on which we express no view. If the term had expired before the motion was docketed, the district court shall give both parties notice and an opportunity to be heard on whether the closure and counsel's attempt to file may bear on the date of filing and on whether that question is properly before it. If it reaches the question, it shall determine, with the burden of proof on the movant, whether counsel had and actually presented the completed motion at the clerk's office on November 26 while custody remained, whether the closure alone prevented its receipt, what other means of filing were available, and when filing first became possible. If the district court concludes that the theory is not properly before it, is unavailable under governing law, or is not supported by proof, and that the term had expired before the first effective filing, it shall dismiss for want of custody, because equity cannot replace the jurisdiction that custody supplies. Any ruling on the legal question is reviewable in the ordinary course, and we decide nothing about it today.

#### V. Matters not decided

We do not decide the merits of Scanio's claim of ineffective assistance of trial counsel, and nothing in this opinion promises collateral relief. We do not decide that Scanio was or was not in custody on November 29, 1993, or when his term of supervised release began or ended. We do not decide whether the November 26 closure fell within the inaccessibility provision of Rule 6(a), or whether a constructive-lodging rule is available for a represented movant. We do not convert the motion into an application for a writ of error coram nobis. That remedy requires its own showing under United States v. Morgan, 346 U.S. 502 (1954), and cannot be granted by relabeling an unsuccessful § 2255 motion.

The order dismissing the motion is vacated, and the matter is remanded to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.

**Dissenting opinion, Van Graafeiland, Senior Circuit Judge** (dissenting from the judgment and from Parts II and IV; joining Part III). Judge Van Graafeiland would affirm. In his view custody is tested when the motion is filed, and the controlling fact is the legal commencement of the supervised-release term. Under 18 U.S.C. § 3624(e) supervised release begins on release from imprisonment, and under 18 U.S.C. § 3624(c) time in a halfway house is part of the term of imprisonment, so the two-year term began with Scanio's release from the halfway house on November 27, 1991 and ended before the motion was docketed. The district court's reference to "probation" was a misdescription that does not matter to that conclusion. He would hold that Rule 6(a) computes periods for acts, that § 2255 as then in force prescribed no filing period to compute, that Rule 82 bars enlarging jurisdiction by rule, that Lamb and Simon concern time periods and not a custodial status, and that collateral consequences of an expired sentence do not satisfy the custody requirement, relying on Brilliant, Carafas, Maleng and, for non-physical restraint, Jones. He would not adopt a constructive-lodging rule on this record, would not extend Houston to a represented movant or rest on assumed facts about tender, and would order no hearing. He would not reach the ineffective-assistance merits or coram nobis; his affirmance would be without prejudice to any separate vehicle Scanio may be able to establish under Morgan, as to which he expresses no view.

**Position of Judge Pierce on Part III.** Judge Pierce does not accept the conclusion in Part III that the closure and Rule 6(a) cannot be given effect in computing whether the motion was filed while the term was running. He would give the closure effect in deciding that question. That disagreement does not affect Parts I, II, IV and V, which he joins, and he would not rest the judgment on any holding that Rule 6(a) cannot reach the closure.

### Precedent and legal effect

- **Publication.** The opinion is published. It is the opinion of the court in the Parts and on the joins stated above.
- **Part II (Stone and Pierce).** Precedential on two joins. A district court that dismisses a § 2255 motion for want of custody must ground the determination in the legal commencement and expiration of the term, established from the sentence and the legal release arrangement. Limited to a record in which the order rested on an inaccurate label and an unexamined inference from the date of physical release. It does not decide when Scanio's term began or ended, and it does not hold that a halfway-house placement or release has any particular legal significance. Brilliant, Carafas, Maleng and Jones are followed; Stabile is treated as a parole decision that does not by itself resolve a supervised-release calculation.
- **Part III (Stone and Van Graafeiland).** Precedential on two joins. Rule 6(a) and the next-business-day principle do not by their own force continue a fully expired sentence; Lamb and Simon are followed as decisions about periods for acts and distinguished as to a continuing status. The Part is joined by a judge who dissents from the judgment, and the vacatur rests on Part II and not on Part III; Part III states the rule that governs the remand. It does not decide whether the November 26 closure made the clerk's office inaccessible within Rule 6(a), and it does not decide whether the motion may be treated as made earlier by another route. Judge Pierce does not join it.
- **Part IV (Stone and Pierce).** Precedential on two joins only for the procedure directed on remand: notice and an opportunity to be heard for both parties, findings on the tender predicates with the burden on the movant, and dismissal if custody had expired and the theory is unavailable or unproved. No constructive-lodging rule is adopted, and Houston v. Lack is neither extended nor limited beyond its stated pro se prisoner setting.
- **Dicta and separate positions.** The statements in Part V of what is not decided are not holdings. The dissent and Judge Pierce's stated non-join are not circuit law.
- **Party-specific relief and law of the case.** The vacatur and remand bind the parties in this litigation. The directions in Parts II to IV govern the remand and are the law of this case on the issues they address.
- **Earlier law.** No earlier decision of this court is overruled or limited. The Supreme Court decisions cited are followed as binding authority according to their scope.

### Remedy and next stage

The order of the district court dismissing the § 2255 motion is vacated, and the matter is remanded to the United States District Court for the Western District of New York for further proceedings consistent with the opinion. The remand is limited. The district court is to determine the legal commencement and expiration of the term of supervised release from the sentence and the legal release arrangement. If the term was running when the motion was docketed on November 29, 1993, it may proceed to the motion's remaining requirements and merits. If the term had expired, it is to give both parties notice and an opportunity to be heard on the closure and counsel's attempt to file, make the findings described in Part IV if it reaches the question, and dismiss for want of custody if the theory is not properly before it, is unavailable, or is not proved. No view is expressed on the merits of the ineffective-assistance claim, and no coram nobis relief is granted or directed.

**Mandate.** No mandate has issued. Under Fed. R. App. P. 40(a) and 41(a) as in force on the date of decision, a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order or by local rule, and the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order. A timely petition for rehearing stays the mandate until the petition is disposed of, and if the petition is denied the mandate issues 7 days after the order denying it unless that time is shortened or enlarged. The remand takes effect on issuance of the mandate. No petition for rehearing, application to stay the mandate, or other filing is recorded as of the date of this decision.
