### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** Castillo appeals from a judgment entered May 19, 1992, after a retrial of one count of using or carrying a firearm in relation to a drug trafficking offense, 18 U.S.C. § 924(c). The judgment imposes an aggregate term of eighty-four months on four convictions, six years of supervised release, and a $200 special assessment. He also challenges the district court's ruling on his delayed motion for a new trial under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 33, which the district court treated as untimely. The government asks that the judgment be affirmed. Castillo asks for relief from the firearm conviction and the supervisory-role sentencing adjustment, and for a new trial on his trial-rights claims.

**Material record.** In July 1989 Officer Shawn Johnson, working undercover, bought cocaine from Castillo and Juan Fernandez. Johnson testified that Fernandez displayed a firearm at his waistband and partly drew it, while Castillo required Johnson to ingest cocaine. No gun was recovered. Johnson was the only eyewitness who supplied the government's account of a firearm, and his initial arrest report did not mention a gun. The defense attacked that omission and the difference between a gun at the waistband and an encounter described as occurring at gunpoint. An earlier appeal in this prosecution, *United States v. Castillo*, 924 F.2d 1227 (2d Cir. 1991), reversed the firearm conviction because of prejudicial expert testimony and required reconsideration of a sentencing enhancement on the remaining convictions. At the retrial of the firearm count the government called officers who repeated Johnson's earlier reports. The district court received those statements for rehabilitation, with instructions limiting their substantive use, and Castillo was again convicted under § 924(c). Castillo also asserts that his attorney refused to let him testify at that trial although he wished to, and that he believed counsel's decision was conclusive. His affidavit says so. He had been told at the first trial that the right to testify was his. A Rule 33 motion raising the point was made after the seven-day period. At sentencing the court applied a two-level adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c) for an organizing or supervisory role. No testimony from counsel and no post-trial evidentiary record on the disputed conversation is before the court.

**Jurisdiction.** The judgment is a final judgment of conviction and sentence, reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; the district court's jurisdiction rests on 18 U.S.C. § 3231. Nothing in the materials before the panel suggests that the notice of appeal was untimely.

**Issues and standards of review.**

1. Whether the officers' repetition of Johnson's reports was properly received for rehabilitation, whether the limiting instructions and the prosecution's use of the testimony cured any error, and whether any error was harmless. Admission and rehabilitation are reviewed for abuse of discretion, with independent review of the governing evidentiary rule. Castillo objected to the officers' repetition and challenged the limiting instructions and the summation use; Johnson's own initial account was received without objection. Preserved nonconstitutional error is tested under Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a) and *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946).
2. Whether the claim that counsel prevented Castillo from testifying can be decided on this appeal. The allocation of the right is a question of law; any ineffective-assistance claim requires the constitutional standard and an adequate factual record, and an appellate court may not find the disputed conversation.
3. Whether the Rule 33 motion was timely. Rule 33's time categories are construed independently; known conduct cannot be turned into newly discovered evidence by a finding.
4. Whether the reasonable-doubt charge shifted the burden of proof. The charge is read as a whole; a genuinely unconstitutional reasonable-doubt instruction is structural error, but disfavored wording alone does not establish one.
5. Whether the § 3B1.1(c) adjustment is sustained. The finding of role is reviewed for clear error and the meaning of the guideline independently.

**Relevant dates.** Offense and undercover purchase, July 1989. Earlier appeal, 1991. Judgment under review, May 19, 1992. Argument, September 15, 1993. Resubmission and decision, January 28, 1994.

### Judgment

The judgment of the district court is AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, and REMANDED, by component:

1. **Firearm conviction, 18 U.S.C. § 924(c):** vacated, and the count remanded for a new trial limited to that count. Judges Lumbard and Stone in the majority; Judge Oakes dissenting.
2. **Aggregate sentence (eighty-four months, six years of supervised release, $200 assessment):** vacated, and remanded for resentencing in light of the eventual disposition of the firearm count, because the aggregate term includes that count. Judges Lumbard and Stone in the majority; Judge Oakes dissenting.
3. **Convictions on the three other counts:** not disturbed; no judge votes to disturb them.
4. **Claim that counsel prevented Castillo from testifying:** no finding is made on the disputed conversation and no relief is granted on that ground on this appeal; any surviving claim is reserved for a proper channel and a developed record. Judges Lumbard, Oakes and Stone, unanimously.
5. **Rule 33 ruling:** affirmed on the ground of untimeliness alone. Judges Lumbard, Oakes and Stone, unanimously.
6. **Reasonable-doubt charge:** challenge rejected; no instructional ground for reversal. Judges Lumbard, Oakes and Stone, unanimously.
7. **Supervisory-role adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c):** sustained, and carried into the recalculation of the sentence. Judges Lumbard, Oakes and Stone, unanimously.

**Opinion of the court**

LUMBARD, Senior Circuit Judge.

**I. Background.** Manuel Castillo appeals from a judgment of the Southern District of New York entered after a second trial of the firearm count in his prosecution. The facts, which the earlier appeal in this prosecution also recounted in part, are stated above and need only be summarized. Johnson, an undercover officer, was the sole eyewitness to the weapon. No gun was recovered, his first arrest report said nothing of one, and the defense attacked both the silence and the contrast between a gun at the waistband and an encounter at gunpoint. At the retrial the government called officers who repeated Johnson's earlier reports, and the district court received them for rehabilitation under limiting instructions. We take the claims in the order in which they bear on the judgment.

**II. The officers' repetition of Johnson's reports.**

*A. Governing principles.* We review the receipt of evidence offered to rehabilitate a witness for abuse of discretion, but the discretion is exercised within the evidentiary rules, and we review their meaning independently. Because Castillo objected to the officers' testimony and challenged the instructions and the summation use, a nonconstitutional error requires reversal unless it was harmless under Rule 52(a) and *Kotteakos*. The government bears the burden of showing that a preserved error did not prejudice the defendant; that is the contrast the Supreme Court drew with the plain-error rule, under which the defendant bears it. See *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993).

Three propositions control. First, the officers' repetition could not come in as substantive evidence under Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B). That rule removes a prior consistent statement from the definition of hearsay only when it is offered to rebut a charge of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive and was made before the alleged motive arose. *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224, 233-34 (2d Cir. 1978); *United States v. Brennan*, 798 F.2d 581, 587 (2d Cir. 1986). The government concedes that the motive the defense asserts, which is to explain Johnson's ingestion of cocaine, existed when Johnson made his reports.

Second, a prior consistent statement that fails Rule 801(d)(1)(B) may still be received for the limited purpose of rehabilitating a witness, but only if it has probative force bearing on credibility beyond merely showing that the witness has repeated himself. *United States v. Pierre*, 781 F.2d 329, 331-33 (2d Cir. 1986). Such force exists when the statement casts doubt on whether an apparently inconsistent statement was made or is truly inconsistent, or when it clarifies or amplifies that statement. *Id.* at 333; *Brennan*, 798 F.2d at 588-89. A specific attack, such as one resting on an omission or a discrepancy between two descriptions, may be answered in that way; a generalized attack on credibility does not open the door. *Brennan*, 798 F.2d at 588-89. *Pierre* confined *Quinto*'s broader statement that every rehabilitative use must satisfy Rule 801(d)(1)(B), but it did not displace *Quinto*'s refusal to let a party bolster its witness merely by multiplying his accusation through additional witnesses.

Third, a limiting instruction under Fed. R. Evid. 105 is measured against the use actually made of the evidence at trial. And harmlessness under *Kotteakos* does not ask whether other evidence would support the verdict. "The inquiry cannot be merely whether there was enough to support the result, apart from the phase affected by the error." 328 U.S. at 764-65. It asks whether the error itself had substantial influence, so that one cannot say "with fair assurance, after pondering all that happened without stripping the erroneous action from the whole, that the judgment was not substantially swayed by the error." *Id.*

*B. Application.* The government's argument is serious, and we state it at its strongest. The defense did not rest on a general attack on Johnson's honesty. It pointed to a specific omission, a first report that did not mention a gun, and to a specific discrepancy between a gun at the waistband and an encounter at gunpoint. Under *Pierre* and *Brennan* a specific attack of that kind may be answered by a prior statement that has real answering force, and the government says the officers' testimony did that by showing that "gunpoint" described the same threatened display. It adds that the instructions confined the jury to credibility.

We accept the principle and not the application. Rehabilitative force is a function of what a particular statement does, not of the name the proponent gives it. A statement clarifies an inconsistency when its content shows how two accounts fit together, and it answers an omission when it supplies context that bears on why the omitted matter was left out. The officers supplied no account of a weapon of their own; Johnson was the only eyewitness who did. To the extent their testimony did no more than relate Johnson's earlier reports that a gun was displayed at the waistband, it said nothing about why the first report was silent and did nothing to square "waistband" with "gunpoint." It repeated the accusation. Repetition shows that Johnson told the same story more than once, which is precisely what *Pierre* holds is not enough, and a party may not convert it into support by presenting the same account through several witnesses. The repetition was not confined to proper rehabilitation, and receiving it as the district court did was error.

The error was not harmless. The proof of a firearm came from one witness, no weapon was found, and that witness's first report omitted it. On such a record the firearm element turns almost entirely on whether the jury believes Johnson. When officers, speaking with the authority of their office, repeat his accusation, they give the jury the appearance of further sources for it, and the prosecution used the repetition in that way, as support for the proposition that a firearm existed. The limiting instructions told the jury that it could consider the reports only on credibility. But the adequacy of an instruction is judged against the actual use of the evidence, and an instruction to treat repetition as bearing on credibility does little to neutralize a presentation that invites jurors to count several official witnesses in favor of one source on the single disputed element. The line between strengthening Johnson's credibility and corroborating that a gun was shown is not one that we can assume a jury would maintain.

The government's best reply is that the jury could have convicted without the officers' testimony. That is not the test. *Kotteakos* directs attention to the error's influence on the actual verdict, and where the disputed element is supplied principally by the witness being bolstered, we cannot say with fair assurance that the verdict was not substantially swayed. Our 1991 decision reversed this conviction because of prejudicial expert testimony; it does not decide whether a different error at the second trial was harmless, and we decide that question on the second trial's own record.

The firearm conviction therefore cannot stand. The remedy is a new trial and not an acquittal. Our ruling concerns the manner in which the government proved the firearm element. We do not hold that the evidence was legally insufficient, and we make no finding that Johnson testified falsely.

We state the limits of the holding. We do not hold that a prior consistent statement is inadmissible whenever it is offered after a motive to fabricate has arisen, and *Pierre* and *Brennan* remain the law of this circuit. A statement genuinely offered to clarify a specific inconsistency or omission, and shown to do so, may be received under an appropriate instruction. Nor do we decide which of the officers' statements, in what form, might be received at a retrial for such a purpose. On retrial the district court will rule on each offer against the function the statement serves, and the use made of it in argument must stay within that function. A statement is not corroboration merely because several officers heard it from the original witness, and repetition by officers who gave no account of a weapon of their own is not evidence that a weapon existed.

**III. The claim that counsel prevented Castillo from testifying.** Castillo says that he told his attorney he wished to testify, that counsel refused, and that he believed counsel's decision was conclusive. The choice whether to testify belongs to the accused personally. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44, 51-53 (1987). It is not counsel's to make. The question here is a different one: whether we can find, on this record, what was said between Castillo and his attorney before the retrial and what Castillo understood. We cannot. The affidavit is not a finding of the district court, and no testimony from counsel or other evidentiary record on the conversation is before us. The advice Castillo received at the first trial shows that he then knew the right was his, but that is evidence of knowledge and not a finding about a later conversation, and it cannot be turned into one. His affidavit, for its part, does not establish that counsel interfered or that any decision not to testify was unknowing. An appellate court does not find such facts, and we make no finding in either direction.

We also do not decide whether a claim of ineffective assistance lies. Such a claim requires both deficient performance and prejudice, neither of which may be assumed. *Strickland v. Washington*, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 694 (1984). A claim that depends on facts outside the trial record is ordinarily presented in a collateral proceeding on a developed record, such as a motion under 28 U.S.C. § 2255. See *United States v. Dukes*, 727 F.2d 34, 39 (2d Cir. 1984). We do not decide whether such a claim is available to Castillo, whether it would succeed, or what bearing this opinion has on it. We hold only that the disputed episode cannot be resolved on this appeal, and that nothing in our disposition gives counsel authority over a defendant's choice to testify or treats earlier advice as a finding about a later conversation. No stand-alone factfinding or collateral order is entered on the strength of the affidavit.

**IV. The Rule 33 motion.** Rule 33, as it then read, gave a defendant two periods. A motion for a new trial on the ground of newly discovered evidence could be made within two years after final judgment, and a motion on any other ground had to be made within seven days after the verdict or finding of guilty, or within such further time as the court fixed during that period. Castillo's motion was made after the seven days. He says his affidavit is newly discovered evidence, that new counsel first showed him the significance of the alleged refusal, and that his earlier lawyer's failure to move sooner should not defeat the motion.

*Dukes* answers the argument. Evidence is newly discovered only if it was found after trial and could not have been found sooner with due diligence, and *Dukes* declined to treat counsel's conduct, already known to the defendant, as newly discovered evidence merely because its significance was recognized later. It reasoned that the contrary rule would let such claims be brought up to two years after judgment, when the trial judge may recall little of counsel's performance, and that the practical difficulty defendants have in recognizing counsel's errors does not justify departing from the language of Rule 33. 727 F.2d at 38-39. Castillo's affidavit recounts an exchange to which he was a party and of which he knew when it occurred. It does not become newly discovered evidence because new counsel presented it. The motion rested on other grounds and was out of time.

This holding concerns timeliness alone. It is not an adjudication that counsel could prohibit a client from testifying, and, as Part III explains, it does not decide whether the alleged events could support relief in a separate constitutional vehicle.

**V. The reasonable-doubt charge.** Castillo contends, relying on *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 (1993), which was cited at argument and in a later letter, that the charge's "abiding conviction" formulation, in its negative phrasing, could suggest that jurors must establish uncertainty before acquitting and so shift the burden to the accused. We read the charge as a whole and ask whether it correctly conveyed the government's burden or required the accused to prove his innocence. *Sullivan* holds that a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction is structural error that no harmlessness analysis can save. It does not make a charge deficient. The question is whether this one was.

It was not. In *United States v. Torres*, 901 F.2d 205, 242-43 (2d Cir. 1990), reviewing for plain error, we rejected a challenge to materially similar language because, read where it appeared, it could not fairly be understood to shift the burden, and because the jury there was told repeatedly that the government bore the burden. The charge here is materially the formulation considered in *Torres*, and, read as a whole, it repeatedly places the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt on the prosecution and nowhere requires the accused to prove innocence. It does not resemble the "substantial doubt" language condemned in *Cage v. Louisiana*, 498 U.S. 39 (1990) (per curiam). Because the charge is not constitutionally deficient, *Sullivan*'s rule has nothing on which to operate. The challenge fails. We decide the charge actually given and express no view on formulations not used in it.

**VI. The role adjustment.** The district court added two levels under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c), which reaches a defendant who was an organizer, leader, manager, or supervisor in criminal activity. We review the finding of role for clear error and the meaning of the guideline independently. *United States v. Beaulieau*, 959 F.2d 375, 380 (2d Cir. 1992). The government must establish the organizing or supervisory facts by a preponderance of the evidence, and possession of drugs or proceeds is not by itself a basis for the adjustment. The court looks to the discretion the defendant exercised, the nature and degree of his role in planning or organizing the offense, and the control and authority he held over other participants. *Id.* at 379-80.

The government points to evidence that Castillo weighed and packaged the cocaine and held $80 of the $90 purchase money. Castillo answers that handling drugs and money shows custody and not control, and is as consistent with equal participation as with leadership. If the adjustment rested on possession or receipt of proceeds alone, the answer would be sound. It does not. The district court drew from Castillo's operational conduct, weighing and packaging the product and holding most of the purchase money, the inference that he organized the transaction and did not merely take part in it, and weighing and packaging were among the facts supporting the organizer finding in *Beaulieau*, 959 F.2d at 380. The question is not whether we would have drawn that inference in the first instance, but whether the district court's inference is permissible on this record, and it is. It is not clearly erroneous. Our holding is confined to the record described. It is not a rule that receipt of proceeds or possession of drugs supports an inference of supervision, and we announce no general rule about role enhancements. Because the aggregate sentence must be redetermined in light of Part II, the adjustment is carried into that recalculation.

**VII. Disposition.** The conviction under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c) is vacated and the count is remanded for a new trial. The aggregate sentence is vacated and the case is remanded for resentencing in light of the eventual disposition of that count, with the § 3B1.1(c) adjustment left in place. The convictions on the three other counts are not disturbed. The ruling that the Rule 33 motion was untimely is affirmed. No relief is granted on the testimony claim, which is left undecided on its merits for the reasons in Part III, and the challenge to the reasonable-doubt charge is rejected.

**Dissent in part (Judge Oakes, noted)**

Judge Oakes agrees with the governing principles stated in Part II.A and with Parts III through VI. He dissents from Part II.B and from the parts of Part VII that vacate the firearm conviction and remand for a new trial and resentencing. On his reading, the question under *Pierre* and *Brennan* is answered statement by statement. Reports that answered the omission of a gun from the first arrest report, or that addressed the difference between a gun at the waistband and an encounter described as at gunpoint, were admissible for credibility only, under an instruction confined to credibility, and none was received as substantive evidence under Rule 801(d)(1)(B). He accepts that bare repetition is not rehabilitation and that harmlessness is not measured by the strength of the other evidence; he differs from the majority on whether the officers' testimony as admitted was more than bare repetition and on prejudice. Examining the summation and instructions as actually given, he would find that the repetition did not substantially influence the verdict under *Kotteakos*, and he reaches that conclusion without relying on other evidence or equating sufficiency with harmlessness. He would affirm the judgment in its entirety, with no new trial and no remand for resentencing.

**Controlling propositions and their support**

1. *Governing principles on prior consistent statements* (Lumbard, Oakes, Stone). Rule: where the motive to fabricate preceded the statements, they are not substantive evidence under Rule 801(d)(1)(B); they may be received only for rehabilitation, and only if each has probative force on credibility beyond repetition, such as answering a specific omission or clarifying a specific inconsistency; bare repetition, including by additional witnesses, is not rehabilitation. Authority: Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B) and 105; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a); *Pierre*; *Brennan*; *Quinto*; *Kotteakos*. Test and burdens: abuse of discretion with independent review of the rule; the limiting instruction is measured against actual use; harmlessness asks about substantial influence on the verdict, not sufficiency, and the government bears the burden on preserved error. Application: Part II.A frames the inquiry.
2. *Application to this retrial* (Lumbard, Stone; Oakes dissenting). Rule: to the extent officers who gave no account of a weapon of their own merely repeated the sole eyewitness's account of a firearm, the repetition lacks rehabilitative force, and where the prosecution used it as corroboration of an unrecovered weapon on a single-witness element the error is not harmless; the remedy is a new trial, not acquittal. Limits: no categorical bar on genuinely clarifying statements; no finding of falsity; no insufficiency holding. Application: Part II.B.
3. *Right to testify* (Lumbard, Oakes, Stone). Rule: the choice to testify is the defendant's; an appellate court does not find a disputed attorney-client conversation, and earlier advice is not a finding about a later one; an ineffective-assistance claim requires *Strickland*'s showing on an adequate record. Authority: *Rock*; *Strickland*; *Dukes*; 28 U.S.C. § 2255 as a possible separate channel. Application: Part III.
4. *Rule 33 timeliness* (Lumbard, Oakes, Stone). Rule: an affidavit recounting the defendant's own previously known dealings with counsel is not newly discovered evidence merely because new counsel later presents it. Authority: Fed. R. Crim. P. 33 as then in force; *Dukes*, 727 F.2d at 38-39. Application: Part IV.
5. *Reasonable-doubt charge* (Lumbard, Oakes, Stone). Rule: the charge as a whole, including the "abiding conviction" formulation, does not shift the burden, and *Sullivan* does not invalidate that formulation by itself. Authority: *Sullivan*; *Cage*; *Torres*. Test: the charge is read as a whole. Application: Part V.
6. *Role adjustment* (Lumbard, Oakes, Stone). Rule: an organizing-role inference from weighing, packaging and control of most of the purchase money is permissible and not clearly erroneous; it is not a universal inference from receipt of proceeds. Authority: U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c); *Beaulieau*. Standard: clear error for the finding, independent review of the guideline; preponderance burden on the government. Application: Part VI.
