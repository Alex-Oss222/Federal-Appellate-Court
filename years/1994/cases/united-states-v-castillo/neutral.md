## Record and issues

**Material record:** During a July 1989 undercover cocaine purchase, Officer Shawn Johnson encountered Castillo and Fernandez. Johnson testified that Fernandez displayed a firearm at his waistband and partially drew it, while Castillo required Johnson to ingest cocaine. No gun was recovered. Johnson was the only eyewitness who supplied the government's firearm account, and the initial arrest report did not mention a gun. The defense challenged both that omission and differences between descriptions of a gun at the waistband and an encounter described as occurring at gunpoint.

An earlier appeal, *United States v. Castillo*, 924 F.2d 1227 (2d Cir. 1991), reversed the firearm conviction because of prejudicial expert testimony and required reconsideration of a sentencing enhancement on the remaining convictions. At the firearm retrial the government called officers who repeated Johnson's earlier reports. The district court admitted the statements for rehabilitation, with instructions limiting their substantive use. Castillo was again convicted under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c).

Castillo also asserted that his attorney refused to let him testify, although he wanted to do so. His affidavit claimed that he believed counsel's decision was conclusive. He had been told at the first trial that the right to testify was his. A delayed Rule 33 motion raised the attorney issue. The sentencing record also supported a disputed two-level supervisory adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c). These are distinct questions: timeliness of the motion, the constitutional allocation of the decision to testify, evidentiary error, jury instructions, and guideline application. *Castillo*, 14 F.3d at 803-08.

### Issue 1: Prior consistent statements, limiting instructions, and prosecutorial use

**Preservation and reach:** Castillo objected to the officers’ repetition of Johnson’s reports and challenged the limiting instructions and summation use. Johnson’s own initial account was admitted without objection; preservation must be assessed for the particular testimony and argument, not treated as uniform.

**Standard of review:** Abuse of discretion for admission and rehabilitation; independent review of the evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional error is assessed under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos.

**Governing test and burdens:** Under the then-current Rule 801(d)(1)(B), the government conceded that the motive to fabricate preceded the statements. Nonsubstantive rehabilitation therefore requires probative force beyond repetition, such as genuine clarification of an asserted inconsistency. A limiting instruction must be evaluated against the actual use at trial.

The government's strongest evidentiary argument is that the defense created an apparent inconsistency, not merely a general credibility attack. *United States v. Pierre*, 781 F.2d 329, 331-33 (2d Cir. 1986), permits a consistent statement with rehabilitative force beyond repetition to explain a claimed inconsistency. *United States v. Brennan*, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986), likewise recognizes that limited use. But *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), does not permit a party simply to multiply its witness's accusations through additional mouths.

The prior statements did not qualify for substantive admission under Rule 801(d)(1)(B): the asserted motive to explain Johnson's cocaine ingestion already existed when he made them. The difficult question is consequently whether repeating the waistband account actually clarifies the difference between that account and the gunpoint description. Repetition can show consistency, but that alone is not the independent rehabilitative function required by the precedents. Its practical effect also matters where the prosecutor relies on the repeated accusation as corroboration that a firearm existed.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946): a preserved evidentiary error requires assessment of its substantial influence on the actual verdict; binding. No directly controlling Supreme Court holding deciding this particular rehabilitation use has been identified.

**Other governing authority:** Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B), 105; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). United States v. Pierre, 781 F.2d 329, 331-34 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Brennan, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Quinto, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), binding circuit limits.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** Whether these statements genuinely clarify the particular inconsistency or merely repeat the accusation, and whether any misuse affected this verdict.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The additional officers had no independent firearm observation. Repetition of “waistband” did not reconcile it with “gunpoint,” and prosecutorial use invited substantive corroboration of the sole eyewitness on the disputed element.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** The defense manufactured an apparent contradiction. The statements explained that “gunpoint” described the same threatened display, and repeated instructions confined the jury to credibility rather than substantive use.

Relief must match the particular proven error; insufficiency is not presumed from trial error.

### Issue 2: Personal right to testify and alleged interference by counsel

**Preservation and reach:** Raised through Castillo’s post-trial affidavit and on appeal. The affidavit’s disputed account is not a trial-court finding; the separate Rule 33 timing limit does not eliminate the need to distinguish direct-appeal reach from collateral litigation.

**Standard of review:** The allocation of the right is a legal question. Any ineffective-assistance claim requires the applicable constitutional standard and an adequate factual record; the appellate court may not find the attorney-client conversation itself.

**Governing test and burdens:** The defendant holds the personal choice to testify. Prior advice can establish awareness of the right, but disputed interference by counsel and a knowing decision not to testify are different inquiries. A conventional ineffective-assistance claim requires deficient performance and prejudice, not assumption of either.

The right to testify is personal. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987), identifies its constitutional importance. Prior advice that the right exists is relevant, but is not logically identical to a finding that Castillo understood he could override counsel at the later trial. Conversely, his later affidavit does not prove unconstitutional interference without attention to the trial record and counsel's account. The court should not decide a contested attorney-client episode through appellate factfinding.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44, 51-53 (1987), recognizes the personal constitutional right to testify; Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 694 (1984), supplies deficient-performance and prejudice requirements. Both predate the event.

**Other governing authority:** Direct-appeal record limits; 28 U.S.C. § 2255 as a separate potential collateral channel. McMeans and Underwood, discussed in the reported record, are other-circuit persuasive authorities, not binding Second Circuit decisions.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** He was expressly advised of his right in open court at the first trial, and a later unsupported affidavit does not establish an unknowing waiver or constitutionally ineffective performance.

### Issue 4: Reasonable-doubt instruction

**Preservation and reach:** Raised on appeal; Sullivan was also cited at oral argument and in a post-argument letter. The supplied record does not independently reproduce every Rule 30 objection.

**Standard of review:** Read the charge as a whole to determine whether it correctly assigns proof beyond a reasonable doubt. A genuinely unconstitutional reasonable-doubt charge is structural; disfavored wording alone does not establish that error.

**Governing test and burdens:** The question is whether the “abiding conviction” language and the full charge lowered the government’s burden or required the accused to prove innocence.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 (1993), binding on a constitutionally defective reasonable-doubt instruction; Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), addresses materially different language.

**Other governing authority:** United States v. Torres, 901 F.2d 205, 242-43 (2d Cir. 1990), binding approval of the relevant formulation in its context.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** None if the instruction is materially the same as the one Torres approved and the full charge preserves the burden.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The negative phrasing could suggest that jurors must establish uncertainty before acquitting and impermissibly shift the burden.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** The full charge repeatedly placed the burden on the prosecution and used language already upheld in Torres; it did not use Cage’s substantial-doubt requirement.
