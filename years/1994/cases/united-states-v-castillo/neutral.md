# United States v. Castillo

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 92-1279. **Decision date:** January 28, 1994. **Citation:** 14 F.3d 802. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Judge Shirley Wohl Kram. **Historical argument:** September 15, 1993. The simulation concerns the retrial of the firearm count and the associated sentencing and post-trial rulings, not a fresh appeal from every component of the original prosecution. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/14/802/612862/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

During a July 1989 undercover cocaine purchase, Officer Shawn Johnson encountered Castillo and Fernandez. Johnson testified that Fernandez displayed a firearm at his waistband and partially drew it, while Castillo required Johnson to ingest cocaine. No gun was recovered. Johnson was the only eyewitness who supplied the government's firearm account, and the initial arrest report did not mention a gun. The defense challenged both that omission and differences between descriptions of a gun at the waistband and an encounter described as occurring at gunpoint.

An earlier appeal, *United States v. Castillo*, 924 F.2d 1227 (2d Cir. 1991), reversed the firearm conviction because of prejudicial expert testimony and required reconsideration of a sentencing enhancement on the remaining convictions. At the firearm retrial the government called officers who repeated Johnson's earlier reports. The district court admitted the statements for rehabilitation, with instructions limiting their substantive use. Castillo was again convicted under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c).

Castillo also asserted that his attorney refused to let him testify, although he wanted to do so. His affidavit claimed that he believed counsel's decision was conclusive. He had been told at the first trial that the right to testify was his. A delayed Rule 33 motion raised the attorney issue. The sentencing record also supported a disputed two-level supervisory adjustment under U.S.S.G. § 3B1.1(c). These are distinct questions: timeliness of the motion, the constitutional allocation of the decision to testify, evidentiary error, jury instructions, and guideline application. *Castillo*, 14 F.3d at 803-08.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final criminal judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291; the district court's prosecution jurisdiction comes from 18 U.S.C. § 3231. Evidentiary rulings receive abuse-of-discretion review, with independent review of the governing evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional trial error requires a prejudice inquiry under Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 52(a) and *Kotteakos v. United States*, 328 U.S. 750 (1946), not merely a finding that the remaining evidence could support conviction.

The then-existing Rule 33 allowed seven days for an ordinary new-trial motion and a longer period for newly discovered evidence. *United States v. Dukes*, 727 F.2d 34, 39 (2d Cir. 1984), prevents relabeling attorney conduct already known to the defendant as newly discovered evidence. That procedural bar does not itself adjudicate every possible collateral ineffective-assistance claim. The reasonable-doubt charge is assessed as a whole. The legal scope of § 3B1.1 is reviewed independently; its supported factual findings receive clear-error review.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

The government's strongest evidentiary argument is that the defense created an apparent inconsistency, not merely a general credibility attack. *United States v. Pierre*, 781 F.2d 329, 331-33 (2d Cir. 1986), permits a consistent statement with rehabilitative force beyond repetition to explain a claimed inconsistency. *United States v. Brennan*, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986), likewise recognizes that limited use. But *United States v. Quinto*, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), does not permit a party simply to multiply its witness's accusations through additional mouths.

The prior statements did not qualify for substantive admission under Rule 801(d)(1)(B): the asserted motive to explain Johnson's cocaine ingestion already existed when he made them. The difficult question is consequently whether repeating the waistband account actually clarifies the difference between that account and the gunpoint description. Repetition can show consistency, but that alone is not the independent rehabilitative function required by the precedents. Its practical effect also matters where the prosecutor relies on the repeated accusation as corroboration that a firearm existed.

The right to testify is personal. *Rock v. Arkansas*, 483 U.S. 44 (1987), identifies its constitutional importance. Prior advice that the right exists is relevant, but is not logically identical to a finding that Castillo understood he could override counsel at the later trial. Conversely, his later affidavit does not prove unconstitutional interference without attention to the trial record and counsel's account. The court should not decide a contested attorney-client episode through appellate factfinding.

The objection to an “abiding conviction” formulation is substantially answered by *United States v. Torres*, 901 F.2d 205, 242-43 (2d Cir. 1990). *Sullivan v. Louisiana*, 508 U.S. 275 (1993), does not make every challenged phrase a structural error; it addresses a constitutionally deficient reasonable-doubt instruction. The supervisory adjustment has a different foundation: evidence of Castillo's handling of cocaine and transaction proceeds permits an inference of an organizing role, subject to *United States v. Beaulieau*, 959 F.2d 375, 380 (2d Cir. 1992). Mere possession of money or drugs, without a role-related inference, would not automatically suffice.
