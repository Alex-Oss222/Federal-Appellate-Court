## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Prior consistent statements, limiting instructions, and prosecutorial use

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Castillo objected to the officers’ repetition of Johnson’s reports and challenged the limiting instructions and summation use. Johnson’s own initial account was admitted without objection; preservation must be assessed for the particular testimony and argument, not treated as uniform. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Reverse the firearm conviction and remand for a new trial, not acquittal.

**Governing law:** Abuse of discretion for admission and rehabilitation; independent review of the evidentiary rule. Preserved nonconstitutional error is assessed under Rule 52(a) and Kotteakos. Under the then-current Rule 801(d)(1)(B), the government conceded that the motive to fabricate preceded the statements. Nonsubstantive rehabilitation therefore requires probative force beyond repetition, such as genuine clarification of an asserted inconsistency. A limiting instruction must be evaluated against the actual use at trial.

Kotteakos v. United States, 328 U.S. 750, 764-65 (1946): a preserved evidentiary error requires assessment of its substantial influence on the actual verdict; binding. No directly controlling Supreme Court holding deciding this particular rehabilitation use has been identified. Fed. R. Evid. 801(d)(1)(B), 105; Fed. R. Crim. P. 52(a). United States v. Pierre, 781 F.2d 329, 331-34 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Brennan, 798 F.2d 581, 587-89 (2d Cir. 1986); United States v. Quinto, 582 F.2d 224 (2d Cir. 1978), binding circuit limits.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** A statement outside Rule 801(d)(1)(B) cannot be admitted as rehabilitation merely because it repeats the trial accusation; on this record the repetition and use had a prejudicial corroborative effect.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose reversal of the firearm conviction for a new trial because the repeated reports lacked a sufficiently distinct rehabilitative purpose and the error cannot confidently be treated as harmless. His reason is not distrust of police testimony as a category. It is the evidentiary structure of this prosecution: one eyewitness supplied the disputed firearm evidence, the weapon was not recovered, the initial report omitted it, and other officers' repetition risked supplying the corroboration the investigation itself lacked.

*Pierre* should be applied, not repudiated. A prior statement may resolve ambiguity when its context explains why two accounts are compatible. Here the central defect is that additional witnesses repeated the same substantive accusation. Calling that use rehabilitation does not explain how it resolves the asserted discrepancy. Nor does a limiting instruction necessarily neutralize a presentation that invites the jury to count multiple official witnesses as corroboration of a single source. The earlier *Castillo* decision makes particular care appropriate because it already identified the danger of bolstering the contested gun evidence; it does not conclusively decide harmlessness at a different trial.

Under *Kotteakos*, Stone should examine the actual prosecutorial use of the statements and their importance to this verdict. The government cannot prevail merely by identifying sufficient admissible evidence. Where the disputed element is supplied principally by the witness being bolstered, repetition by fellow officers can materially affect the jury's evaluation. A new trial, rather than acquittal, follows because the proposed error concerns the manner of proof, not a determination that no rational jury could find the firearm element.

**Independent or alternative considerations:** No additional rule is proposed beyond the interpretation and application stated here.

**Preferred scope:** A statement outside Rule 801(d)(1)(B) cannot be admitted as rehabilitation merely because it repeats the trial accusation; on this record the repetition and use had a prejudicial corroborative effect. The lawful outer boundary is the issue and remedial limit stated here, not a broader rule about unpresented facts.

Case-level proposed scope: inadmissible repetitive rehabilitation, prejudicial on this record, requiring a new firearm trial. The opinion may explain the limits of *Pierre* but should not prohibit all prior consistent statements offered for a genuinely clarifying purpose.

**Fallback position:** Proposed: rest reversal on the use and centrality of these particular statements without comprehensively redefining all permissible rehabilitation.

Case-level nonjoin limits, also proposed: treating repetition as corroboration solely because multiple officers heard the original witness, equating sufficiency with harmlessness, or announcing that counsel controls the defendant's personal choice to testify. Stone also should not join an acquittal mandate unsupported by an insufficiency holding.

**Remedy and remand:** Retry the firearm count with properly confined evidentiary rulings and adjust the sentence to the eventual lawful judgment.

**Reserved questions:** No categorical exclusion of genuinely clarifying consistent statements, no finding that Johnson testified falsely, and no insufficiency-based acquittal.

**Separate writing:** Proposed only. It would explain why the authorities and record support the disposition stated above and answer the contrary position addressed in the legal reasoning. ### Component 2

**Component:** Personal right to testify and alleged interference by counsel

**Jurisdiction and reach:** Raised through Castillo’s post-trial affidavit and on appeal. The affidavit’s disputed account is not a trial-court finding; the separate Rule 33 timing limit does not eliminate the need to distinguish direct-appeal reach from collateral litigation. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Do not decide the disputed attorney-client episode on this record; reserve any surviving claim for a lawful, factually adequate channel.

**Governing law:** The allocation of the right is a legal question. Any ineffective-assistance claim requires the applicable constitutional standard and an adequate factual record; the appellate court may not find the attorney-client conversation itself. The defendant holds the personal choice to testify. Prior advice can establish awareness of the right, but disputed interference by counsel and a knowing decision not to testify are different inquiries. A conventional ineffective-assistance claim requires deficient performance and prejudice, not assumption of either.

Rock v. Arkansas, 483 U.S. 44, 51-53 (1987), recognizes the personal constitutional right to testify; Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668, 687, 694 (1984), supplies deficient-performance and prejudice requirements. Both predate the event. Direct-appeal record limits; 28 U.S.C. § 2255 as a separate potential collateral channel. McMeans and Underwood, discussed in the reported record, are other-circuit persuasive authorities, not binding Second Circuit decisions.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** The procedural disposition cannot be read to give counsel ultimate authority over the defendant’s choice to testify or to transform prior advice into a finding about a disputed later conversation.

**Legal reasoning:** Rock allocates the choice to the accused. The record of earlier advice is evidence of knowledge, not evidence of every later interaction. The proposed new firearm trial supplies relief without resolving a disputed conversation, while any surviving ineffective-assistance claim must satisfy Strickland through a procedurally proper record. This preserves both the personal right and the limits of appellate factfinding.

### Component 3

**Component:** Timeliness of the Rule 33 motion

**Jurisdiction and reach:** The motion was made beyond the then-applicable seven-day period; Castillo claimed his affidavit was newly discovered evidence and blamed counsel for not filing earlier. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Uphold the Rule 33 timeliness ruling.

**Governing law:** Independent review of Rule 33’s legal time categories; no factual finding may convert already known conduct into newly discovered evidence. The version of Rule 33 then in force allowed seven days for ordinary grounds and two years after final judgment for newly discovered evidence. Dukes prevents relabeling known attorney conduct as new evidence.

No directly controlling Supreme Court holding on this particular Rule 33 classification identified. Strickland governs an independently viable assistance claim, not enlargement of Rule 33 by label. Fed. R. Crim. P. 33 as in force in 1994; United States v. Dukes, 727 F.2d 34, 39 (2d Cir. 1984), binding.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Proposed holding:** An affidavit recounting the defendant’s own previously known interaction with counsel does not become newly discovered evidence merely because new counsel later presents it.

**Legal reasoning:** Dukes is binding and addresses the attempted change in classification. Giving “newly discovered” a different meaning whenever a defendant later obtains advice would erase the ordinary period. That does not decide whether the known event supports relief under a separate constitutional vehicle.

**Proposed disposition:** Reject the reasonable-doubt challenge.

**Governing law:** Read the charge as a whole to determine whether it correctly assigns proof beyond a reasonable doubt. The question is whether the “abiding conviction” language and the full charge lowered the government’s burden or required the accused to prove innocence.

Sullivan v. Louisiana, 508 U.S. 275, 281-82 (1993), binding on a constitutionally defective reasonable-doubt instruction; Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39 (1990), addresses materially different language. United States v. Torres, 901 F.2d 205, 242-43 (2d Cir. 1990), binding approval of the relevant formulation in its context.

**Legal relationship:** Applying binding law.

**Legal reasoning:** Torres answers the particular language challenge. Sullivan addresses the legal consequence of a deficient charge, not a rule banning every contested phrase.
