## Record and issues

**Material record:** Hathaway had undergone hip-fusion surgery in 1973, with pins inserted into his hip. The fusion was unsuccessful, and he experienced arthritis and persistent pain. A specialist proposed additional surgery in 1978, which Hathaway then declined. Dr. Foote, a prison general practitioner, later provided measures including pain medication, orthopedic shoes, restrictions on lifting, and placement on a lower floor.

An x-ray in July 1980 revealed two broken pins. Hathaway was not promptly told of that finding. He repeatedly complained of pain over the following years, including numerous visits in 1981 and 1982. According to his trial testimony, after a nurse informed him of the broken pins in July 1981, he told Foote that he was willing to undergo surgery. Foote said a referral would be made. A renewed specialist referral did not occur until late 1982, after correspondence concerning the condition and the filing of this action.

The subsequent medical assessments were not uniform. One specialist considered pin removal not medically necessary but potentially helpful for pain; another recommended further surgery. An operation in October 1983 removed accessible portions of the pins and included further grafting. The later operation is relevant to the course of the condition, but its occurrence does not establish that every earlier conservative decision was constitutionally deficient.

An earlier appeal had reversed summary judgment because the medical-care claim presented factual issues. *Hathaway v. Coughlin*, 841 F.2d 48 (2d Cir. 1988). At the later trial, the other defendants obtained judgment for lack of personal involvement. Those dispositions are not the appeal now presented. The jury could not agree on Foote's liability, and the court declared a mistrial and denied his motions for judgment as a matter of law. *Hathaway*, 37 F.3d at 64-66.

### Issue 2: Deliberate indifference, expert proof, and historically established immunity law

**Preservation and reach:** Foote disputes both constitutional sufficiency and immunity. The standard available at decision must be distinguished from the law giving notice during the earlier treatment.

**Standard of review:** View evidence and reasonable inferences favorably to Hathaway under Hendricks; apply the legal culpability and immunity rules independently without deciding credibility.

**Governing test and burdens:** A serious medical need and actual awareness and disregard are required; negligence or disagreement over treatment does not suffice. Knowledge may be inferred from the actual medical sequence. Qualified immunity concerns clearly established law at the time of conduct, not notice created by this appeal.

*Estelle v. Gamble*, 429 U.S. 97, 104-06 (1976), distinguishes deliberate indifference to serious medical needs from negligence, malpractice, and disagreement over treatment. *Wilson v. Seiter*, 501 U.S. 294 (1991), requires attention to culpability as well as the seriousness of the condition. *Farmer v. Brennan*, 511 U.S. 825, 837, 842-45 (1994), explains that the official must know of and disregard the relevant substantial risk; actual knowledge may be inferred from evidence, but it is not replaced by a rule that the official merely should have known.

Hathaway's strongest case is not simply that a pin broke or that another physician would have chosen surgery earlier. It combines a serious and persistently painful condition, knowledge of broken hardware, failure to inform him, evidence that he reconsidered surgery once informed, and an extended interval without effective reassessment. A jury could find that a physician consciously persisted in an ineffective response rather than exercising an actual medical judgment about the changed problem.

Foote's strongest response is substantial. Hathaway had a complicated longstanding orthopedic condition, previously rejected surgery, received continuing treatment, and was later evaluated by specialists who did not regard pin removal as medically required. A physician does not become deliberately indifferent because conservative treatment fails to eliminate pain. A jury could find that Foote continued to exercise professional judgment, or that any delay amounted only to negligence.

The existence of those competing inferences is decisive at this stage. A court cannot select the professional-judgment inference simply because some care was provided. Equally, the jury must not be told that any inadequate treatment is deliberate indifference. The question remains actual awareness and disregard, applied to the condition, information, and available response.

Qualified immunity requires a historically grounded notice inquiry. *Estelle* predated the alleged conduct and made knowing disregard of serious medical needs actionable. *Farmer* supplies the governing articulation at decision, but cannot create notice retrospectively for a previously lawful act. The relevant proposition must therefore be defined at the appropriate level: not a right to a particular operation on a particular date, but a prohibition on consciously disregarding a known serious medical need through the course of conduct the plaintiff's evidence would establish.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 104-06 (1976); Wilson v. Seiter, 501 U.S. 294 (1991); Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825, 837, 842-45 (1994). Farmer articulates the current merits standard but does not retrospectively create notice.

**Other governing authority:** 42 U.S.C. § 1983; Hendricks v. Coughlin, 942 F.2d 109, 112 (2d Cir. 1991); the earlier Hathaway mandate at 841 F.2d 48 (1988).
