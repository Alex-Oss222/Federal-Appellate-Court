### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** After a trial in which the jury could not agree on Foote's liability, the district court declared a mistrial and denied Foote's motions for judgment as a matter of law under Fed. R. Civ. P. 50. Foote appeals. He seeks judgment ending the claim against him for insufficient constitutional proof and on the ground of qualified immunity. Hathaway seeks further trial proceedings. The materials describing the proceedings below do not state the date on which the order was entered or the date of the notice of appeal, and this decision resolves no question about the timeliness of the notice.

**Material record.** Hathaway underwent hip-fusion surgery in 1973, and pins were placed in his hip. The fusion failed, and he suffered arthritis and persistent pain. A specialist proposed further surgery in 1978, and Hathaway declined. Foote, a general practitioner at the prison, later provided pain medication, orthopedic shoes, restrictions on lifting, and placement on a lower floor. An x-ray in July 1980 showed two broken pins. Hathaway was not promptly told. He complained of pain repeatedly in the following years, with numerous visits in 1981 and 1982. He testified that a nurse told him of the broken pins in July 1981, that he then told Foote he was willing to undergo surgery, and that Foote said a referral would be made. A renewed referral to a specialist did not occur until late 1982, after correspondence about his condition and the filing of this action. The later medical assessments differed: one specialist regarded removal of the pins as not medically necessary but possibly helpful for pain, and another recommended further surgery. An operation in October 1983 removed the accessible portions of the pins and included further grafting. An earlier appeal in this case reversed a grant of summary judgment because the medical-care claim presented factual issues. Hathaway v. Coughlin, 841 F.2d 48 (2d Cir. 1988). At the trial that followed, judgment was entered for the other defendants for lack of personal involvement. Those judgments are not before the Court.

**Issue 1: Appellate reach after a mistrial.** Whether this Court may hear an appeal from the denial of Rule 50 relief after a mistrial, and, if so, how far. Standard: independent review of the Court's jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1291 and the collateral order doctrine; for a legally reachable immunity question, the evidence and reasonable inferences are taken in the light most favorable to Hathaway.

**Issue 2: Deliberate indifference, expert proof, and clearly established law.** Whether, on that view of the evidence, a constitutional violation can be found, including whether the inference of conscious disregard requires expert medical proof, and whether the law at the time of Foote's conduct (1980 through 1982) clearly established the relevant prohibition. Standard: de novo review of the legal questions; evidence and inferences viewed favorably to Hathaway under Hendricks v. Coughlin, 942 F.2d 109, 112 (2d Cir. 1991); no credibility determination.

**Dates that bear on the law.** The treatment at issue occurred from 1978 through 1983. Estelle v. Gamble was decided in 1976, Wilson v. Seiter in 1991, and Farmer v. Brennan on June 6, 1994, after the conduct and before this decision. The earlier appeal in this case was decided in 1988.

### Opinions and holdings

**Writings.** One opinion of the Court, written by Judge Altimari and joined in full by Judges McLaughlin and Stone-Khoury. There is no concurrence, dissent, or other separate writing.

**Opinion of the Court.**

Hathaway, an inmate, brought this action under 42 U.S.C. 1983 against several prison officials and Dr. Joseph Foote, a prison general practitioner, alleging deliberate indifference to his serious medical needs. The facts bearing on this appeal are set out above. The jury could not agree on Foote's liability, the district court declared a mistrial and denied Foote's motions for judgment as a matter of law, and Foote appeals. He contends that the evidence cannot establish a constitutional violation and that qualified immunity entitles him to judgment before a second trial. Hathaway's position is that a mistrial and the denial of Rule 50 relief do not authorize an appeal that would choose among competing medical inferences.

**Part I. The reach of this interlocutory appeal.**

We begin with our own power to hear the appeal. An order denying judgment as a matter of law after a jury has failed to agree does not end the litigation; it sends the case to a second trial. The existence of a Rule 50 order therefore does not itself create finality, and we rest our jurisdiction on no final judgment against Foote and on no certification. Whatever power we have comes from the collateral order doctrine as the Supreme Court applied it to qualified immunity in Mitchell v. Forsyth, 472 U.S. 511 (1985). Mitchell holds that "a district court's denial of a claim of qualified immunity, to the extent that it turns on an issue of law, is an appealable 'final decision' within the meaning of 28 U.S.C. 1291 notwithstanding the absence of a final judgment." Id. at 530. The reason is that the entitlement "is an immunity from suit rather than a mere defense to liability" and, "like an absolute immunity, it is effectively lost if a case is erroneously permitted to go to trial." Id. at 526.

The same holding limits what we may do. The appealable issue is "a purely legal one: whether the facts alleged ... support a claim of violation of clearly established law." Id. at 528 n.9. Where the resolution of immunity turns on disputed facts, no appeal lies. See Moffitt v. Town of Brookfield, 950 F.2d 880, 884 (2d Cir. 1991). The doctrine does not permit us to decide whether Foote or Hathaway should be believed, to choose among competing medical inferences, or to review the sufficiency of the trial evidence as such. Hathaway is right to insist on that boundary. Within it, the legal question is whether the evidence, taken as the plaintiff presents it, makes out a violation of clearly established law, so we view the evidence and the reasonable inferences from it in the light most favorable to Hathaway. See Hendricks, 942 F.2d at 112. We do so only to frame the legal question. We find no fact and decide no question of credibility.

Our decision in Whalen v. County of Fulton, No. 93-7457 (2d Cir. Mar. 29, 1994), Part II, applies the same approach to a different order. We held there that an order denying a qualified-immunity summary judgment motion without prejudice to renewal after discovery, and reserving the defense for decision on a developed record, is not a conclusive determination and is not an appealable collateral order. We also made clear that the label on an order does not control and that a functionally conclusive rejection of a purely legal immunity defense remains reviewable. Whalen turned on a reservation of the defense for a later record. The order before us is of a different kind. After a full trial, the district court denied Foote's motions and declared a mistrial. The record before us contains no indication that the court reserved the defense for later decision, denied relief without prejudice to renewal, or conditioned its ruling on further development. On that record, the denial operates as a conclusive rejection of the legal question whether Hathaway's evidence, taken as true, defeats immunity, and Foote faces a second trial. Had the court reserved the defense, Whalen would govern and the premise of our jurisdiction would be absent. We decide the appeal on the footing the record presents.

We therefore hear the appeal only insofar as it presents the legal question of qualified immunity on the evidence so viewed. To the extent Foote seeks judgment on the ground that the evidence is insufficient apart from immunity, or relies on his own account of the treatment or on conflicts in the medical evidence, those contentions are not properly before us on this interlocutory appeal, and we dismiss the appeal to that extent.

**Part II. Whether Hathaway's evidence permits a finding of a constitutional violation.**

Qualified immunity analysis requires a court to determine, as a necessary concomitant to deciding whether a right was clearly established, whether the plaintiff has asserted a violation of a constitutional right at all. Siegert v. Gilley, 500 U.S. 226, 232 (1991). That inquiry is part of the legal question Mitchell makes appealable, and we take it up as such, not as a free-standing review of the sufficiency of the evidence. We decide it de novo.

The Eighth Amendment, enforced here through 42 U.S.C. 1983, forbids deliberate indifference to the serious medical needs of prisoners. Estelle v. Gamble, 429 U.S. 97, 104-05 (1976). Estelle also marks what the Amendment does not reach. An inadvertent failure to provide adequate care, negligence in diagnosing or treating a condition, and medical malpractice do not become constitutional violations merely because the patient is a prisoner, and a difference of opinion over treatment does not state a claim. Id. at 105-06. A claim requires a serious medical need and a culpable state of mind. See Wilson v. Seiter, 501 U.S. 294 (1991) (applying the deliberate-indifference standard of Estelle to conditions of confinement, medical care included). Farmer v. Brennan, 511 U.S. 825 (1994), decided after the treatment at issue but before this decision, states the standard that governs the merits today. The official must both be aware of facts from which the inference could be drawn that a substantial risk of serious harm exists and draw the inference. Id. at 837. Actual knowledge may be shown by inference from the circumstances, and a factfinder may conclude that an official knew of a substantial risk from the very fact that the risk was obvious. Id. at 842. An official who actually knew of the risk may nonetheless escape liability if he responded reasonably. Id. at 844-45. The standard asks what the official actually knew and did. It is not met by showing that he should have known. See id. at 837, 842-45.

Viewed in the light most favorable to Hathaway, the record shows a serious and persistently painful condition, a failed hip fusion with arthritis. The condition changed in July 1980, when an x-ray showed two broken pins, and Hathaway was not promptly told. For two years he returned repeatedly, complaining of pain. In July 1981, after a nurse told him of the broken pins, he told Foote he was willing to undergo surgery, and Foote said a referral would be made. The renewed specialist referral did not come until late 1982, after correspondence about his condition and after he had sued. A jury crediting that testimony could find that Foote knew the hardware had broken, knew the pain continued, knew that Hathaway, who had declined surgery in 1978, now wished to proceed, and, having said a referral would be made, did not make it for more than a year. A jury could further find that this was not an exercise of medical judgment about a changed problem but a conscious decision to let an ineffective course continue. That is evidence from which actual awareness and disregard may be inferred.

Foote's response is substantial, and we do not discount it. Hathaway had a complicated, longstanding orthopedic condition. He had himself declined surgery in 1978. He received continuing treatment: medication, orthopedic shoes, restrictions on lifting, and a lower-floor placement. Specialists who later examined him did not agree, one regarding removal of the pins as not medically necessary though possibly helpful for pain and another recommending further surgery. A physician is not deliberately indifferent because conservative treatment fails to eliminate pain, and a delay that amounts to negligence is not a constitutional wrong. Estelle, 429 U.S. at 105-06. A jury could find on this evidence that Foote continued to exercise professional judgment, relied on specialist advice, understood Hathaway's complaints differently, or concluded that the risks and limited likely benefits of surgery favored continued conservative care. It remains open to Foote to make each of those showings at retrial.

But the existence of competing inferences defeats Foote's request for judgment; it does not support it. On the question we may decide, the evidence is taken in the light most favorable to Hathaway, and a court may not select the professional-judgment inference simply because some care was provided. Treatment of some kind does not conclusively defeat a finding of deliberate indifference, because care directed to the condition as it stood in 1978 does not establish that the changed problem disclosed in 1980 was met with judgment rather than disregard. Hathaway's 1978 refusal of surgery is relevant but not dispositive. The breaking of the pins, and his testimony that he then expressed willingness to proceed, bear directly on the premise on which reliance on that refusal rests. The 1983 operation neither proves nor disproves that Foote's earlier choices were constitutionally deficient, and we do not convert the later decision to operate into a retrospective constitutional prescription. Equally, nothing in this opinion permits a jury to find deliberate indifference merely because treatment was inadequate or unsuccessful. The question remains what Foote actually knew and whether he consciously disregarded it.

We therefore hold only that a reasonable jury, crediting Hathaway's testimony and the documented sequence, could find actual awareness and disregard. We do not hold that Hathaway has proved it. Foote's actual knowledge, his culpability, causation, and injury remain for decision on remand.

The appeal also presents whether this inference can rest on the evidence described without expert medical testimony. We decline to announce a categorical answer in either direction. Some questions in prison medical litigation cannot be answered by lay jurors, such as the evaluation of a treatment choice or the causal connection between a delay and an injury, and expert proof may be required for them. The inference at issue here is of a different kind. It rests on a sequence that lay jurors can follow without medical training: a known failure of surgical hardware, withheld information, persistent complaints of pain, a patient's changed position, and a promised referral not carried out for more than a year. We hold only that the evidence described can support the requisite inference without a universal expert-testimony prerequisite. We do not hold that expert evidence is unnecessary in prison medical cases, and we leave open whether it will be needed on causation or injury at retrial.

**Part III. Whether the law was clearly established when Foote acted.**

Foote argues that, whatever the evidence shows, the law did not clearly establish in 1980 through 1982 that his course of treatment was unlawful. Two features of the inquiry control. First, the relevant date is the date of the conduct, not the date of our decision, and notice created by a later decision cannot make earlier conduct unlawful. Our method asks whether the right was defined with reasonable specificity, whether Supreme Court or circuit decisional law then supported its existence, and whether, under preexisting law, a reasonable official would have understood that his acts were unlawful, all measured as of the time of the conduct. See Jermosen v. Smith, 945 F.2d 547 (2d Cir. 1991). Whether a right was clearly established at a particular time is a question of law, which we decide in light of all relevant precedents. Elder v. Holloway, 510 U.S. 510, 514-16 (1994). Second, the right must be defined with particularity, because the contours of the right must be sufficiently clear that a reasonable official would understand that what he is doing violates it. Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 639-40 (1987).

Defined at that level, the right at issue is not a right to a particular operation on a particular date, and it is not a general entitlement to adequate medical care. It is the prohibition on consciously disregarding a known serious medical need by persistent inaction, in the circumstances Hathaway's evidence would establish.

That prohibition was clearly established by 1980. Estelle, decided in 1976, held that deliberate indifference to the serious medical needs of prisoners violates the Eighth Amendment, and it distinguished that wrong from negligence, malpractice, and disagreement over treatment. 429 U.S. at 104-06. This circuit had applied the principle before the conduct at issue. See Williams v. Vincent, 508 F.2d 541 (2d Cir. 1974) (allegations supported an inference of deliberate indifference rather than an exercise of professional judgment where physicians may have chosen an easier and less efficacious treatment); Todaro v. Ward, 565 F.2d 48 (2d Cir. 1977) (applying Estelle; repeated instances of delayed or denied care may show deliberate indifference). A reasonable physician in 1980 through 1982 would have understood that the Eighth Amendment forbade knowingly leaving an inmate's serious and painful condition unaddressed after learning that it had changed, as distinct from treating it with ordinary professional judgment or with negligence. Our later decisions describe the right in the same terms. See Kaminsky v. Rosenblum, 929 F.2d 922 (2d Cir. 1991). Those decisions confirm what Estelle already provided and are not the source of notice.

Two sources are not. Farmer supplies the standard governing the merits at the time of decision, but it cannot create notice retrospectively for conduct that preceded it, and we do not use it for that purpose. Our 1988 decision in this case likewise postdates the conduct. It held only that the medical-care claim presented factual issues that precluded summary judgment, and it supplies no notice.

Foote's principal contrary argument is that the specific course he followed was not clearly unlawful. To the extent the argument is that the law gave no clear guidance on when a physician must refer a patient who earlier refused surgery for removal of broken pins, it defines the right at the level of the particular medical decision. The question is not whether the law required a given operation by a given date. It did not, and we recognize no such right. The question is whether the law forbade a physician from consciously disregarding a known serious medical need, and it did. Whether Foote's conduct fell within that prohibition, or instead reflected judgment or negligence, depends on facts the jury has not found. On the evidence viewed in the light most favorable to Hathaway, immunity does not entitle Foote to judgment before retrial.

**Part IV. Disposition.**

The appeal is dismissed to the extent it seeks review of the denial of Rule 50 relief apart from the legal question of qualified immunity or depends on crediting Foote's account. The order is affirmed insofar as it rejects Foote's claim of qualified immunity on the evidence viewed in the light most favorable to Hathaway. The case is remanded for retrial of the claim against Foote. We award no damages and make no ruling on damages; the first jury's inability to agree supplies no basis for one. The judgments for the other defendants are not before us and are undisturbed, and no claim against any other official is revived. Liability, if any, must rest on Foote's own conduct. On retrial the jury must be instructed that negligence and disagreement over treatment are insufficient, that inadequate treatment is not by itself deliberate indifference, and that the question is Foote's actual awareness and disregard.

**Controlling propositions, supporting judges, and application.**

1. *Reach after a mistrial (Part I).* Rule: the denial of Rule 50 relief after a mistrial is not itself final, and appellate power exists only to the extent the ruling conclusively rejects a purely legal qualified-immunity defense; review is confined to whether the evidence, viewed favorably to the plaintiff, shows a violation of clearly established law; the remainder is dismissed. Authority: 28 U.S.C. 1291; Mitchell, 472 U.S. at 526, 528 n.9, 530; Moffitt, 950 F.2d at 884; Whalen Part II; Fed. R. Civ. P. 50. Test: independent review of jurisdiction, with the plaintiff-favorable view of the evidence used only to frame the legal question. Burdens: the Court examines its own jurisdiction independently, and the holding allocates no burden of proof to either party. Qualification: an order that reserves the defense or denies relief without prejudice to renewal is governed by Whalen and would not support the appeal. Application: the order denied the motions after a full trial and mistrial, with no indication of reservation, so the legal immunity question is reviewable and the rest is dismissed. Supporting judges: Altimari, McLaughlin, Stone-Khoury.
2. *Constitutional violation on the plaintiff-favorable evidence (Part II).* Rule: a serious medical need together with actual awareness and disregard is required; negligence and disagreement over treatment are not enough; knowledge may be inferred from the circumstances; on the evidence described, the combination of knowledge of the broken pins, prolonged pain, withheld information, and failure to act on a promised referral permits a finding of deliberate indifference, and treatment of some kind does not conclusively defeat it. Authority: Estelle, 429 U.S. at 104-06; Wilson, 501 U.S. 294; Farmer, 511 U.S. at 837, 842-45; Siegert, 500 U.S. at 232; Hendricks, 942 F.2d at 112. Test and burdens: de novo review of the legal question, plaintiff-favorable inferences, no credibility determination; the plaintiff bears the burden of proving actual awareness and disregard at trial, and the holding decides only that a reasonable jury could so find. Qualification: no holding that Hathaway has proved the violation; actual knowledge, culpability, causation and injury remain for retrial. Application: the July 1980 x-ray, the nondisclosure, the repeated complaints, the July 1981 exchange with Foote, and the delayed referral permit the inference, while the continuing treatment and the specialists' differing views are competing inferences for the jury. Supporting judges: Altimari, McLaughlin, Stone-Khoury.
3. *Expert proof (Part II).* Rule: there is no categorical rule requiring or dispensing with expert testimony; the lay-comprehensible sequence described here can support the inference of conscious disregard without a universal expert-testimony prerequisite. Authority: Estelle; Farmer, 511 U.S. at 842. Qualification: expert proof may be required for questions a lay jury cannot evaluate, such as a treatment choice or causal connection, and the need for it on causation and injury at retrial is left open. Supporting judges: Altimari, McLaughlin, Stone-Khoury.
4. *Notice and qualified immunity (Part III).* Rule: for conduct in 1980 through 1982, Estelle, applied in this circuit before that conduct, clearly established the narrowly defined prohibition on consciously disregarding a known serious medical need by persistent inaction; Farmer and the 1988 decision in this case create no notice; the right is not a general medical-care duty and not a right to a particular operation on a particular date. Authority: Estelle, 429 U.S. at 104-06; Williams v. Vincent, 508 F.2d 541; Todaro v. Ward, 565 F.2d 48; Jermosen, 945 F.2d 547; Elder, 510 U.S. at 514-16; Anderson, 483 U.S. at 639-40. Test: right defined with reasonable specificity, supported by decisional law at the time of the conduct, and understood by a reasonable official as unlawful. Application: on Hathaway's evidence, a physician who knew of the broken pins and the changed circumstances and persistently failed to act would have been on notice. Supporting judges: Altimari, McLaughlin, Stone-Khoury.

**Principal contrary arguments and answers.** Hathaway contends that no appeal lies because the Court would be choosing among medical inferences. The Court agrees as to everything outside the legal immunity question and dismisses it, and it reaches only the legal question on the plaintiff-favorable view. Foote contends that continuing treatment, Hathaway's earlier refusal of surgery, and specialist advice show at most disagreement or negligence, and that the course was not clearly unlawful. The Court answers that these are competing inferences for the jury, that the changed circumstances after July 1980 alter the premise of the earlier refusal, and that the right is defined at the level of the prohibition on conscious disregard, not at the level of a particular operation.

**Matters left open.** Whether Foote in fact knew of the broken pins and consciously disregarded the need; causation and compensable injury; damages; whether expert proof will be required on causation or injury at retrial; whether any constitutional right to a particular operation on a fixed date exists (none is recognized here) or any general rule dispenses with medical experts; how the appeal would be resolved if the order had reserved the defense, a matter governed by Whalen; the timeliness of the notice of appeal; any review of the dismissed contentions on the merits; and the judgments for the other defendants. This opinion decides no question that depends on the facts a jury may find at retrial.

### Precedent and legal effect

This is a published opinion of the Court, precedential on the propositions stated in Parts I through III, each of which carries three joins. It follows Mitchell v. Forsyth and Moffitt v. Town of Brookfield on the reach of interlocutory review of qualified immunity. It applies Whalen v. County of Fulton, Part II, without narrowing or extending it, and distinguishes it on posture: Whalen concerned a without-prejudice denial of summary judgment that reserved the defense for a developed record, while this appeal concerns a denial of Rule 50 relief after a full trial and mistrial with no reservation shown. It applies Estelle v. Gamble, Wilson v. Seiter, Farmer v. Brennan, Siegert v. Gilley, and Elder v. Holloway as binding Supreme Court authority, and it applies the circuit's method for clearly established law in Jermosen v. Smith. It changes no earlier circuit law and overrules nothing. Farmer is treated as the present merits standard and not as a source of notice.

Circuit-wide precedent is limited to the propositions in Parts I through III: the reach of the appeal and dismissal of the remainder; the sufficiency of the described combination of evidence to permit a finding of deliberate indifference, with no categorical expert-proof rule; and the notice supplied by Estelle for the narrowly defined prohibition. Party-specific relief is the affirmance of the denial of immunity-based judgment on the evidence viewed favorably to Hathaway, the dismissal of the remainder, and the remand for retrial of the claim against Foote. That ruling rests on the evidence at the first trial viewed in Hathaway's favor; it does not bind a factfinder or determine what the evidence at retrial will show. The mandate of the 1988 decision in this case is neither extended nor reduced. There is no concurrence or dissent, and nothing in this opinion is dicta beyond the statements that the Court expressly reserves.

### Remedy and next stage

The appeal is dismissed in part, the order is affirmed in part, and the case is remanded to the district court for retrial of the claim against Foote on actual knowledge, culpability, causation, and injury, with the jury instructed as stated in Part IV. No damages are awarded and no ruling on damages is made. The judgments for the other defendants are not before the Court and are undisturbed, and no claim against any other official is revived. Costs are not addressed.

The mandate has not issued. Under Fed. R. App. P. 40 and 41 as in effect on the date of this decision, a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order, and the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order; a timely petition for rehearing stays the mandate until the petition is disposed of. The remand proceedings in the district court await the issuance of the mandate. No petition or other filing has been made or is assumed.
