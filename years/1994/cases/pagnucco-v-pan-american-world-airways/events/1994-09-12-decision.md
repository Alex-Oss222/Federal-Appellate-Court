### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** The final judgments of the Eastern District of New York entered after the consolidated willful-misconduct liability trial and the three separate damages trials. The entry dates of the individual judgments are not stated in the materials before the court.

**Procedural setting.** The court has before it a petition for rehearing submitted April 6, 1994. It acts on that submission by this amended disposition, which states the court's disposition of the appeals in full and takes the place of the preceding versions in this appeal.

**Jurisdiction.** The court has jurisdiction over the final judgments under 28 U.S.C. § 1291. The materials before the court do not include the notices of appeal or state their filing dates. No defect in timeliness appears on those materials, and the court decides no question of timeliness.

**Material record.** On December 21, 1988, a bomb destroyed Pan Am Flight 103 over Lockerbie, Scotland, killing its 243 passengers and sixteen crew members. These cases arise from the deaths of Robert I. Pagnucco, Walter L. Porter, and Harry M. Bainbridge. Pan Am and Alert Management Systems defended the consolidated liability proceedings, and the common liability determination also governed 207 other passenger cases. The three individual damages awards were separately tried.

The estates' theory was that an unaccompanied suitcase containing an explosive device in a radio-cassette player entered the interline baggage system at Frankfurt, traveled through Heathrow, and reached Flight 103. The route and mechanism were disputed and were supported substantially by circumstantial evidence. The applicable Air Carrier Standard Security Program required positive passenger-baggage matching at the pertinent high-security airports, or physical inspection or exclusion of unaccompanied baggage. Pan Am had stopped interline matching at Heathrow and Frankfurt and used x-ray screening instead. The estates presented security warnings, evidence concerning training and implementation, and evidence that management knew the limits of x-ray screening. Pan Am contested causation and the inference of willful misconduct, and sought to prove that its personnel understood the FAA to have cleared its procedures.

The trial judge restricted that evidence on the ground that an oral communication could not constitute the required regulatory waiver. The record distinguishes an uncertain proffer concerning security official Sonesen from a specific proffer concerning executive Cozzi, who would have described the task force's instruction to obtain FAA guidance and the reported clearance on which he acted. The trial judge also conditioned certain British regulatory evidence on Pan Am's acceptance of a violation of the American rule. After a thirteen-week liability trial the jury found willful misconduct causally connected to the deaths. The damages awards were $9.225 million for Pagnucco, $1.735 million for Porter, and $9 million for Bainbridge. The jury awarded no survival damages for conscious pain and suffering. The challenges to loss of society and parental care are to be distinguished from the unchallenged financial-contribution and service components.

**Relief sought.** The Pan Am defendants seek reversal, a new liability trial, and correction of the challenged damages. The estates seek preservation of the liability determination and the supported awards.

**Opinion of the Court.** STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judge, joined by VAN GRAAFEILAND, Senior Circuit Judge.

#### I. Background

This opinion states the court's disposition of these appeals on the petition for rehearing submitted April 6, 1994, and replaces the preceding versions. The record is summarized above, and we repeat here only what each issue requires. A jury, after a thirteen-week trial, found willful misconduct causally connected to the deaths of the three decedents, and separate trials fixed damages. The Pan Am defendants challenge the liability determination on evidentiary grounds and challenge the society and parental-care components of the awards. We take the issues in that order.

#### II. Regulatory communications, culpable understanding, and alternative defenses

**A. The Article 25 inquiry.** The Warsaw Convention, with the Montreal Agreement, makes a carrier liable for the death of a passenger caused by an accident within the Convention's terms, Art. 17, subject to a limit on liability. Article 25 withdraws that limit when the damage is caused by the carrier's willful misconduct. The estates therefore bore the burden of proving willful misconduct and its causal relation to the deaths. This court defines the term by the actor's state of mind: an act or omission done with knowledge that injury will probably result, or in a manner that implies reckless disregard of the probable consequences. Grey v. American Airlines, 227 F.2d 282 (2d Cir. 1955); Ospina v. Trans World Airlines, 975 F.2d 35 (2d Cir. 1992). A violation of safety rules, even an intentional one, is not by itself willful misconduct. Berner v. British Commonwealth Pacific Airlines, 346 F.2d 532 (2d Cir. 1965). The question is therefore not only what the security program required but what the persons directing Pan Am's operations knew and consciously disregarded. Pan Am does not challenge the basic willful-misconduct instruction, and we do not revisit it.

**B. Review.** Evidentiary rulings are ordinarily reviewed for abuse of discretion. That deference does not extend to a ruling whose premise is a mistake of law, and we review independently the theory of relevance on which this evidence was excluded. A mistaken ruling requires a new trial only if it affected the substantial rights of the party. Fed. R. Civ. P. 61; 28 U.S.C. § 2111. And the party claiming error from excluded evidence must have made its substance known to the court by an offer or in a context that made it apparent. Fed. R. Evid. 103(a)(2).

**C. Legal authorization and actual understanding.** Pan Am sought to show that its personnel understood the FAA to have cleared its substitution of x-ray screening for interline baggage matching. The district court restricted that evidence on the ground that an oral communication could not constitute the regulatory waiver the security program required.

If the question were whether Pan Am was legally authorized to depart from the written requirements, that ruling would be correct, and we say so at the outset. Pan Am remained subject to the written security requirements. An oral assurance, whatever its source, could not waive a binding regulatory procedure, and we hold nothing that would give it that effect. Our decisions on claimed official authorization are to the same effect. See United States v. Durrani, 835 F.2d 410 (2d Cir. 1987), discussing United States v. Duggan, 743 F.2d 59 (2d Cir. 1984). Those decisions address whether an official's assent excuses a violation of law. They do not decide what Article 25 asks.

Article 25 asks whether those directing Pan Am's operations acted with knowledge that their course would probably cause injury, or with reckless disregard of that probability. That is an inquiry into what those persons actually understood and why they acted as they did. That an oral waiver was legally ineffective tells us that the conduct was not excused. It does not tell us what the decisionmakers knew or believed when they made the choice. The district court's ruling treated the two as the same. It excluded evidence of the personnel's understanding because the communication could not operate as a waiver, and in doing so it denied the distinction between legal authorization and actual understanding. That premise was an error of law.

The distinction matters because a report of regulatory clearance is not necessarily offered for its truth. Offered to show its effect on the person who received it, it bears on what that person understood and why he proceeded. And where a party's state of mind is the central issue, this court has favored a liberal policy of admitting evidence directed to that state of mind, excluding it only where tangential and confusing matter clearly outweighs its relevance. Vinieris v. Byzantine Maritime Corp., 731 F.2d 1061 (2d Cir. 1984).

The Cozzi proffer illustrates the point. Cozzi, an executive, would have described the task force's instruction to obtain FAA guidance and the reported clearance on which he acted. Evidence that a responsible official was told to consult the regulator, received a report of clearance, and proceeded on that understanding has some tendency to distinguish a consciously reckless operational choice from a mistaken judgment. That tendency is what Article 25 makes consequential. The proffer was specific, it made the substance of the testimony known to the court, and the claim of error is preserved.

**D. The estates' response.** The estates have a strong answer, and we do not dismiss it. A belief that a regulator has tolerated a practice does not necessarily negate knowledge that passengers face a grave physical risk. One can knowingly endanger passengers while believing that a regulator has looked away. A jury could disbelieve Cozzi, conclude that the reliance was contrived, or find that even an honestly held understanding did not overcome knowledge of probable injury, and the weaknesses the estates identify in the offered accounts may substantially reduce their probative value. Those are reasons for the jury to test the evidence. They are not reasons to declare it legally irrelevant. Nor does Rule 403 rescue the ruling. A particularized Rule 403 objection remains available at the new trial, but it cannot sustain an exclusion whose stated foundation denied the distinction altogether. The estates are protected by Rule 105: on request the court may instruct the jury that no legally effective waiver occurred and that the evidence bears only on the carrier's state of mind.

We therefore hold that a regulatory communication's inability to create legal authorization does not make its effect on the decisionmaker's culpable understanding categorically irrelevant. The holding is narrow. We do not hold that any waiver existed, that Cozzi's reliance was genuine, or that a belief in the FAA's clearance is a defense under Article 25, and Pan Am is not entitled to an instruction that its conduct was excused. Nor do we hold that knowledge of illegality is an element of willful misconduct. Cheek v. United States, 498 U.S. 192 (1991), and Ratzlaf v. United States, 510 U.S. 135 (1994), construed particular criminal willfulness statutes. They do not establish such an element for Article 25, and we rest nothing on one.

**E. Alternative defenses.** The district court also conditioned the admission of certain British regulatory evidence on Pan Am's acceptance that it had violated the American requirement. Rule 8(e)(2) allows a party to state as many separate defenses as it has regardless of consistency. The Supreme Court has noted, in a criminal case, that the civil rules specifically authorize inconsistent pleading, and it held that a defendant who denies the offense may still be entitled to an instruction on entrapment. Mathews v. United States, 485 U.S. 58 (1988). That decision is persuasive here, not controlling. Its principle is that an asserted inconsistency between defenses is not a reason to make a party surrender one in order to present the other. Whether any particular item of British regulatory evidence is admissible depends on its own relevance, foundation, and Rule 403 balance. The condition the district court imposed was not a permissible basis for exclusion.

**F. Prejudice.** We do not minimize the estates' evidence of security warnings, prior bomb incidents, the shortcomings of x-ray detection, and the alleged failure to respond adequately to specific warnings. A jury could rely on that evidence to find willful misconduct, and nothing we say forecasts a different result. But the question is not whether another jury might find willful misconduct on this evidence. It is whether the exclusion of the defense's account of its own decisionmaking could materially have affected this jury's resolution of the central disputed element. That element was management's knowledge and disregard of probable injury. A trial built around that element is not fairly assessed by counting the remaining adverse incidents. The jury was asked to decide whether Pan Am's decisionmakers consciously disregarded a probable risk, and it did not hear, in the form properly offered, what those decisionmakers understood the regulator to have said and why they acted. The exclusion of the Cozzi account, reinforced by the improper condition on alternative regulatory defenses, was not harmless under Rule 61 and § 2111. A new trial of willful misconduct and its causal connection is required.

We do not rest that conclusion on the Sonesen proffer. The description of what Sonesen would have said was uncertain, and it cannot be treated as equally preserved or supported. Nor do we rest it on the rejection of a proposed curative instruction concerning an e-mail. That is a separate preservation question, and it does not by itself forfeit the preserved claim that Pan Am was barred from presenting other evidence of its actual understanding.

**G. The retrial.** At the new trial, evidence of the kind the Cozzi proffer described may be received on a proper foundation, with the limiting instruction described above on request, and Pan Am may present lawful alternative defenses. Each other proffer is to be evaluated separately. The district court will decide in the first instance whether the retrial can be confined to willful misconduct and its causal connection, and we leave that to it.

#### III. Causation and the other evidentiary rulings

Pan Am asks for judgment on causation. Sufficiency is assessed in the light most favorable to the verdict. The estates' theory that an unaccompanied suitcase containing an explosive device in a radio-cassette player entered the interline baggage system at Frankfurt, traveled through Heathrow, and reached Flight 103 rested substantially on circumstantial evidence, and the route and mechanism were disputed. The chain was sufficient to be evaluated by a jury. The new trial we order corrects the process by which the jury decided culpability. It does not select how the bomb traveled, and we adopt no account of its route.

The remaining evidentiary objections do not alter the result. Several objections to the estates' experts and to miscellaneous evidence of conduct were not preserved in the form now asserted. They cannot be pooled with the preserved objections as though all received ordinary review, and unpreserved civil objections receive only demanding review. Apart from the Cozzi and British-evidence rulings, no ruling is shown to require reversal on its own account, and a new trial ordered for one prejudicial exclusion does not validate unrelated proffers. Our mandate does not require the admission of every statement attributed to Sonesen, every proposed expert, or every alternative bombing scenario. Each retains its own requirements of preservation, foundation, hearsay, and cumulativeness.

For guidance at the new trial, and without deciding any particular offer, the settled rules are these. An investigative report may contain trustworthy evaluative findings under Rule 803(8)(C). Factually based conclusions are not excluded because they are opinions, and the court has both discretion and an obligation to exclude untrustworthy portions. Beech Aircraft Corp. v. Rainey, 488 U.S. 153 (1988); see Gentile v. County of Suffolk, 926 F.2d 142 (2d Cir. 1991). Admission of an evaluative finding does not carry with it every accusation embedded in the report. A security expert may explain practices and instruments but may not instruct the jury on the carrier's legal liability, because the court states the law and the jury applies it. Hygh v. Jacobs, 961 F.2d 359 (2d Cir. 1992); United States v. Scop, 846 F.2d 135 (2d Cir.), modified, 856 F.2d 5 (1988). An alternative bombing theory requires an adequate expert foundation under Rule 702. Pan Am need not establish an entire replacement account for the theory to be relevant, but unsupported possibilities do not become admissible merely because causation is disputed. An x-ray demonstration may be excluded as cumulative where the proposition it illustrates is stipulated.

#### IV. Damages

The damages questions will recur, and we decide them to guide further proceedings. Whether a category of damages is legally recoverable is a question we review independently. Whether a beneficiary was dependent, and the amount of any award, are for the factfinder.

**A. The framework.** In In re Air Disaster at Lockerbie, 928 F.2d 1267 (2d Cir. 1991), this court held that substantive federal common law governs the cause of action under the Convention and that recovery under Article 17 is for compensatory damages only. That decision binds us. The loss of the Convention's limit under Article 25 lifts the limit on compensatory recovery. It does not authorize punitive damages.

**B. Loss of society.** The decisions of the Supreme Court on loss of society in maritime settings support recognition of the category within the Lockerbie framework. Sea-Land Services, Inc. v. Gaudet, 414 U.S. 573 (1974), allowed a decedent's dependents to recover for loss of support, services, and society in a general maritime wrongful-death action, and American Export Lines, Inc. v. Alvez, 446 U.S. 274 (1980) (plurality opinion), carried the recovery for loss of society to a nonfatal injury. Mobil Oil Corp. v. Higginbotham, 436 U.S. 618 (1978), and Miles v. Apex Marine Corp., 498 U.S. 19 (1990), teach the limit on that reasoning: where Congress has prescribed the damages recoverable in a setting, courts do not supplement them. Higginbotham held that survivors could not recover loss of society under general maritime law for a death on the high seas, where the Death on the High Seas Act governs, and Miles applied the same principle of uniformity to seamen. Those limits are enforced in their own settings. They do not apply of their own force to this crash over land, and a party invoking an enacted limit must establish that it governs. To the extent Pan Am would import a high-seas pecuniary-loss limit mechanically, it has not shown a statutory basis for doing so.

We hold that, within the Lockerbie framework, a spouse and dependent children may recover compensatory damages for loss of society on proper proof. The recovery is not unrestricted. Gaudet speaks of dependents. Dependency is a factual predicate, not an incident of kinship, and it is not presumed for adult children. An appellate court cannot supply it.

The award to the Pagnucco estate includes society recovery for adult children. The record before us does not show a determination that they were dependent, and kinship does not establish it. We vacate the Pagnucco society award and remand for a determination of which adult beneficiaries, if any, were dependent and for redetermination of the award accordingly. As to the Porter and Bainbridge estates, Pan Am has not shown that the society components rest on a presumption of adult-child dependency or on any other category the law forbids, and we do not disturb them on that ground.

**C. Parental care.** Parental-care damages rest on a pecuniary foundation: the economic value of the training, nurture, guidance, and care the decedent would have provided. The ordinary expectation of such care is limited to minority. After majority, a child may recover only for a specifically proved pecuniary contribution, and this court has required children past majority to be very specific in showing that a parent's guidance had a pecuniary value. First National Bank in Greenwich v. National Airlines, Inc., 288 F.2d 621 (2d Cir. 1961). That case arose under the Death on the High Seas Act, and we follow it here for the pecuniary component of parental care under the federal common-law framework. Companionship may not be relabeled as guidance to duplicate society recovery, and the same loss may not be compensated twice.

The parental-care instructions did not separate ordinary care during minority, specifically proved pecuniary benefits after majority, and nonpecuniary companionship. The awards cannot stand on those instructions. We vacate the parental-care component of each of the three awards and remand for redetermination under corrected instructions requiring the minority limitation, a separate evidentiary basis for any claimed post-majority pecuniary contribution, and the avoidance of double counting.

**D. Scope of the damages remand.** The financial-contribution and service components were not challenged, and we do not reopen them. Because a new trial of willful misconduct is ordered, the district court should determine, in light of its result, which unchallenged and genuinely separable components may remain fixed. It should not reopen every dollar, and it should not treat existing awards as collectible on the strength of the vacated determination. We express no view on later changes in Warsaw damages law, on any unchallenged amount, or on the dependency of any adult beneficiary beyond what the record shows.

#### V. Disposition

The determination of willful misconduct and its causal connection to the deaths is vacated, and the cases are remanded for a new trial consistent with Part II. Pan Am's request for judgment on causation is denied, and no other evidentiary ruling is held to require reversal or a direction to admit evidence. The Pagnucco society award is vacated and remanded for a determination of the dependency of the adult children. The parental-care components of all three awards are vacated and remanded. The judgments are not otherwise disturbed, and the enforceability of the separable components will conform to the result of the new trial.

**Dissent in part (Judge Cardamone).** Judge Cardamone dissents from Part II and from the order of a new trial of willful misconduct. He would affirm the liability determination. In his view the preserved rulings on the Cozzi proffer and on the British regulatory evidence do not warrant a new trial of liability, and neither the Sonesen proffer nor the rejected curative instruction furnishes a ground for reversal. He applies the same governing framework as the court: review is deferential but does not extend to a legally mistaken premise; Article 25 asks about the carrier's knowledge or reckless disregard; knowledge of illegality is not an element; and a belief in the FAA's clearance is not a categorical defense. He agrees with the court's rejection of the challenge to causation, and he agrees with the court's rules and judgment on society and parental-care damages. Because he would not order a retrial, he would limit the remand to the Pagnucco society award and the parental-care components, would leave liability and the unchallenged components in place, and does not join the directions that conform the awards to the result of a new trial. Whether the dissent rests on the absence of error or on the absence of prejudice from any error is not stated.

**Matters left open.** The court does not decide whether Cozzi's account is credible, whether Pan Am's reliance was genuine or reasonable, or what weight the account deserves. It does not decide that knowledge of illegality is or is not an element of willful misconduct under Article 25. It does not adopt any account of the bomb's route or decide the truth of any alternative scenario. It does not decide the admissibility of any particular proffer at the new trial, including statements attributed to Sonesen and unpreserved expert objections. It does not decide whether the retrial can be confined to willful misconduct and its causal connection, or what effect the new trial has on the 207 other passenger cases governed by the common liability determination, which are not before the court. It does not decide the amount of any unchallenged component, any later change in Warsaw damages law, or the dependency of any adult beneficiary not shown by the record.

### Precedent and legal effect

- **Status.** Published opinion of the court. Parts II, III and IV carry two joins (Judges Stone-Khoury and Van Graafeiland) and are precedential in this circuit to the extent of their holdings. Judge Cardamone concurs in the result of Part III and in the judgment and stated rules of Part IV; his dissent from Part II and his grounds are not circuit law.
- **Prior decisions followed.** In re Air Disaster at Lockerbie, 928 F.2d 1267 (2d Cir. 1991) (federal common law governs the Convention cause of action; compensatory damages only); Grey, Berner and Ospina (the Article 25 standard); Vinieris (liberal admission of state-of-mind evidence where intent is central); First National Bank in Greenwich (specific proof of pecuniary value of parental guidance after majority); Beech Aircraft, Gentile, Hygh and Scop (evidentiary guidance, applied as settled law).
- **Prior decisions distinguished.** Durrani and Duggan address whether an official's assent excuses a violation of law, not the relevance of a reported clearance to culpable understanding. Cheek and Ratzlaf concern particular criminal willfulness statutes and do not supply an element for Article 25. Higginbotham and Miles enforce enacted damages limits in their own statutory settings and do not apply of their own force to this crash over land. Mathews is persuasive authority in its criminal setting.
- **What changes.** No prior decision is overruled. The court holds that a regulatory communication's inability to create legal authorization does not make its effect on culpable understanding categorically irrelevant under Article 25, and that a party may not be required to abandon one lawful defense to present another. It states that society damages for adult children require proof of dependency and that parental-care damages are limited to minority absent specific proof of pecuniary value after majority.
- **Party-specific relief and law of the case.** The new trial and the damages remand bind the parties and the three cases before the court, and the rulings in this opinion govern the proceedings on remand. The court makes no ruling in the 207 other passenger cases.
- **Dicta and separate positions.** The evidentiary guidance in Part III restates settled rules and decides no particular proffer. The observations in Part II about the estates' strongest response describe matters for the jury and are not holdings about the weight of any evidence. Judge Cardamone's dissent is a separate position and not law.

The determination of willful misconduct and its causal connection to the deaths is vacated and remanded to the Eastern District of New York for a new trial under Part II.
