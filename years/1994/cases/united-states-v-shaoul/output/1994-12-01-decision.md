# United States of America v. Jack Shaoul

Opinion by Circuit Judge Altimari, joined in full by Senior Circuit Judge Lumbard and Circuit Judge Stone-Khoury. There is no concurrence and no dissent.

## Decision summary

Jack Shaoul, an antiques dealer, was convicted by a jury of one count of conspiracy and ten counts of mail fraud arising from two insurance claims. He asked the court of appeals to overturn the conviction on two grounds. First, after the verdict the government learned that one juror was the uncle of the wife of an assistant United States attorney in the same district, a prosecutor who had no part in this case, and that the juror had not recalled the relationship when questioned before trial. Shaoul argued that this relationship alone required a new trial. Second, he argued that the jury was not properly instructed that it had to be unanimous about the facts supporting a conviction, and he raised two further objections to the charge.

The court rejected both grounds and affirmed in full, by a vote of 3 to 0. On the juror, Shaoul's lawyer had told the district court that the juror acted in good faith and then chose not to question him, so the claim that the juror answered dishonestly failed. The court also held that a juror's indirect family relationship to a prosecutor in the district who had nothing to do with the case does not, by itself, establish disqualifying bias, and that the record showed no actual bias and no reason to order a hearing. On the instructions, no objection had been made at trial, so the court reviewed only for plain error and found none. The court expressly left open several related questions, which are identified in the opinion and in the holdings below.

## The case and the result

### Posture

Jack Shaoul appeals from a final judgment of conviction after a jury trial on one count of conspiracy and ten counts of mail fraud, and from the denial of his motion for a new trial based on a juror's indirect relationship to an assistant United States attorney who had no part in the case. The district court also rejected his challenge to the jury instructions. It imposed concurrent forty-month prison terms, two years of supervised release, a $50,000 fine, the costs of imprisonment and supervision, and restitution of $13,356.90. The appeal raises no independent challenge to any component of the sentence.

### The material facts

Shaoul, an antiques dealer, submitted insurance claims arising from two alleged losses. The first was a reported theft of paintings and other items from his car in January 1991. His employee Edward Purcell testified that a painting attributed to Albert Pinkham Ryder remained in the shop and that Shaoul told him to conceal it, and that he photographed it beside a current newspaper after the reported theft. Louis Liskin described a backdated letter reciting a purchase interest, intended to support an inflated valuation, and testified that he understood the painting was not authentic. The second claim followed a genuine water leak at the shop in February 1991. Witnesses described moving previously broken glassware into the wet area and further damaging a purported Winslow Homer watercolor that had been identified as inauthentic. The valuation documents included an invoice for a sale that the named gallery owner testified had not occurred. Purcell and Liskin testified under nonprosecution agreements, which bore on their credibility but did not require the jury to disregard them, and other witnesses and documents corroborated them.

Before deliberations the court gave a general instruction on unanimity, explained that the government need not prove every alleged overt act, and twice directed unanimity on the material falsity of the relevant representations. When the jury asked whether all of the overt acts had to be proved, the court repeated that one was sufficient. Defense counsel did not object to the original charge or to that answer. After the verdict the government learned that a juror was the uncle of the wife of an assistant United States attorney. That assistant worked in the same district and had no involvement in this prosecution, and the juror had not recalled the relative's employment during voir dire. The government made the juror available at sentencing. Counsel expressly conceded the juror's good faith, declined to examine him, and argued that the relationship alone required a new trial.

### Jurisdiction

The court has jurisdiction over the final judgment under 28 U.S.C. section 1291; the district court's jurisdiction rested on 18 U.S.C. section 3231. The materials before the court do not show the date of entry of judgment or of the notice of appeal, and no question about the timeliness of the appeal is among those raised.

### Issues and standards of review

1. Whether the denial of a new trial under Fed. R. Crim. P. 33 on the ground of the juror's undisclosed relationship was an abuse of discretion. Review is for abuse of discretion, with independent review of the legal tests for nondisclosure and implied bias. The movant bears the burden of establishing the factual or categorical ground on which relief rests. Counsel's concession of good faith and decision not to examine the juror are preserved facts of record, and the legal consequence of the known relationship is open to decision.
2. Whether the unobjected-to unanimity instruction, the answer to the jury's question about overt acts, and the remaining charge objections show reversible error. Because no objection was made when the court could correct the charge or its answer, review is for plain error under Fed. R. Crim. P. 30 and 52(b) and *United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993): error, plainness, effect on substantial rights, and the discretionary inquiry into fairness, integrity and public reputation. The defendant bears the burden on prejudice.

### Judgment

The judgment of conviction and the order denying a new trial are affirmed.

- Component 1, juror relationship and denial of a new trial (Part II): Affirmed. Judges Lumbard, Altimari and Stone-Khoury concur. No judge dissents.
- Component 2, unanimity, the overt-act answer and the remaining charge objections (Part III): Affirmed. Judges Lumbard, Altimari and Stone-Khoury concur. No judge dissents.
- Remedy: No new trial, no remand, no juror hearing and no change to the sentence. The vote on the judgment as a whole is 3 to 0.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

ALTIMARI, Circuit Judge, joined by LUMBARD, Senior Circuit Judge, and STONE-KHOURY, Circuit Judge:

**I. Overview.** Shaoul asks for a new trial on two grounds. He says a juror's undisclosed family tie to the prosecuting office required one, and he says the jury was not properly instructed on unanimity and related matters. We reject both and affirm. The facts are set out above.

**II. The juror.**

*A. The record and how we review it.* Rule 33 permits a new trial when the interest of justice requires one. We review its denial for abuse of discretion, but we decide without deference the legal rules the district court used to judge a nondisclosure or a claim of implied bias.

Counsel's course at sentencing shapes what we can decide. The government produced the juror. Counsel did not merely fail to object; he expressly conceded good faith and gave up the chance to question the juror. The Supreme Court has distinguished forfeiture, the failure to assert a right in time, from waiver, the intentional relinquishment of a known right. *Olano*, 507 U.S. at 733. Counsel's election was of the second kind as to the factual inquiry. Shaoul's legal contention, that the known relationship by itself required a new trial, is a question of law that remained open whatever counsel chose to do about the facts, and we decide it on the merits. We do not decide whether counsel's election would also bar that contention, and we do not treat it as a bar. The election does determine the record. The record shows a particular relationship, an honest failure to recall it, and nothing about the juror's actual state of mind.

*B. Nondisclosure.* A party who seeks a new trial because a juror answered a voir dire question incorrectly "must first demonstrate that a juror failed to answer honestly a material question on voir dire, and then further show that a correct response would have provided a valid basis for a challenge for cause." *McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood*, 464 U.S. 548, 556 (1984). The burden is the movant's at each step. We apply that test to deliberate and inadvertent nondisclosures alike, and our decision in *United States v. Langford*, 990 F.2d 65, 68 (2d Cir. 1993), does not delete its first requirement: the juror there was treated as having answered dishonestly, and the court then found the second requirement unproved.

The first requirement is dispositive here. Counsel told the district court that the omission was made in good faith. A juror who honestly does not recall a relationship has not failed to answer honestly. Because the first requirement fails, we do not ask whether a truthful answer would have supported a challenge for cause.

The strongest form of Shaoul's argument is that an undisclosed tie to the prosecuting office calls for relief even when the omission was innocent. *United States v. Colombo*, 869 F.2d 149 (2d Cir. 1989), does not support that argument. The juror there was alleged to have deliberately concealed her relationship to a government attorney, and the court reasoned that her willingness to lie about it exhibited an interest strongly suggesting partiality, a reasoning that distinguished the cases in which nondisclosure was honest or not shown to be deliberate. *Id.* at 152. Nothing of that kind is present here. The nondisclosure route fails on the record counsel chose to make.

*C. Implied bias.* Shaoul's remaining argument does not depend on dishonesty. He contends that the relationship, once known, implies bias as a matter of law. An honest answer is not immunity from bias that is actually shown, or in exceptional circumstances inferred, and *McDonough* did not foreclose those avenues. *See* 464 U.S. at 556-57 (Blackmun, J., concurring). The question is whether this relationship, standing alone, establishes disqualifying bias. It does not.

The tie is attenuated twice over. It runs from the juror through his niece to her husband, not to the assistant himself, and the assistant, though employed by the office that prosecuted, had no role in the case. Employment by the sovereign that prosecutes does not by itself imply bias. In *Dennis v. United States*, 339 U.S. 162, 172 (1950), the Supreme Court held that federal employees were not challengeable for cause solely by reason of that employment, while stressing that the way remains open in every case to bring a contention of bias "from the realm of speculation to the realm of fact," that the trial court has a serious duty to determine whether actual bias exists, and that its rulings on such challenges receive broad discretion. *Id.* at 168. The juror here was not employed by the government at all. If a government employee is not presumed biased in a government prosecution, the uncle of the wife of an employee who had no part in the case is not presumed biased either.

*Colombo* is consistent with that conclusion. It said that relatives of attorneys actually involved in the investigation and prosecution should not sit as jurors. 869 F.2d at 152. The line it drew was between those who conduct the prosecution and others. That line follows the source of potential partiality, which is a connection to the people conducting the case, not an arbitrary degree of kinship or common employment in a large office. We read *Colombo* to require no more, and we decline to extend it to every relative of every lawyer in the prosecuting office. The Fourth Circuit's decision in *Garland v. United States*, 182 F.2d 801, 802 (4th Cir. 1950), which rejected a challenge based on a juror's sister's marriage to an uncle of the prosecuting assistant, is not binding on us. We take it for no more than persuasive support, and we do not read it or anything we say here to make family relationships categorically irrelevant.

We therefore hold only that a juror's indirect family relationship to an assistant United States attorney in the prosecuting district, who had no involvement in the prosecution, does not by itself establish disqualifying implied bias. A closer relationship, a tie to someone who took part in the case, exposure to information about the prosecution, or evidence of actual partiality may require a different inquiry and a different result, and we do not decide those questions. Nor does our conclusion rest on the strength of the evidence of guilt. That evidence would be no answer to a showing that the jury was partial. The claim fails because the dishonesty and the bias on which it depends have not been established.

*D. Actual bias and a hearing.* Actual bias is a question of fact that the party asserting it must establish, and the trial court is the primary judge of a juror's impartiality. *Dennis*, 339 U.S. at 168. The district court had no finding or testimony of partiality before it because counsel chose to ask no questions, and kinship alone cannot supply what the record lacks. The only evidence is the relationship and the juror's honest failure to recall it.

Nor is there a basis for a remand for a hearing. The government made the juror available at sentencing, and counsel, who had conceded good faith, declined to question him. Where counsel expressly declined to develop a matter and no one has alleged bad faith or any other factual basis for thinking the juror biased, we will not order the inquiry counsel passed up. The district court did not abuse its discretion in denying the motion.

**III. The instructions.**

*A. Review.* Shaoul did not object to the charge, or to the answer to the jury's question, when the court could have corrected either. Rule 30 requires an objection that states distinctly the matter and the grounds, and the court was likewise given no chance to correct its answer to the jury. His claims are therefore reviewed for plain error under Rule 52(b). *Olano* requires an error, meaning a deviation from a legal rule; that the error be plain, meaning clear or obvious; and that it affect substantial rights, which ordinarily means it prejudiced the outcome, with the burden on the defendant. 507 U.S. at 733-35. Only then may the court correct the error, and it should do so only if the error seriously affects the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings. *Id.* at 736. We read the charge as a whole and do not judge a sentence in isolation. *United States v. Locascio*, 6 F.3d 924, 942 (2d Cir. 1993).

*B. Unanimity and the overt-act answer.* Jurors must agree on the factual basis of a conviction at least where that basis divides into distinct conceptual groupings. *United States v. Schiff*, 801 F.2d 108, 114 (2d Cir. 1986). In this circuit a general instruction requiring unanimity ordinarily ensures that agreement, and a court need not give a more specific instruction unless the complexity of the evidence or other factors create a genuine danger of jury confusion. *Id.* at 114-15; *United States v. Natelli*, 527 F.2d 311, 325 (2d Cir. 1975); *United States v. Harris*, 8 F.3d 943, 945 (2d Cir. 1993); *United States v. Peterson*, 768 F.2d 64, 68 (2d Cir. 1985).

The charge here gave the general instruction and, on the mail-fraud counts, twice directed unanimity on the material falsity of the representations. Shaoul argues that because several overt acts and several alleged falsehoods were in play, different jurors could have settled on different predicates, and that the answer to the jury's question, which told the jurors that proof of one overt act sufficed, did not say that they must agree on which. That possibility arises whenever an indictment alleges more than one act or statement, and it is the reason for the general instruction rather than a reason to displace it. To displace the ordinary rule Shaoul had to show complexity or some other circumstance creating a genuine danger that jurors would convict on incompatible factual bases. The case involved two insurance claims by one defendant, tried together on a single conspiracy count and ten mail-fraud counts. Nothing in the record described to us shows that the jury could have reached its verdict by combining findings that were mutually exclusive, and the charge did not authorize it to do so.

The jury's question, as the record describes it, asked whether all of the overt acts had to be proved. That is a question about the government's burden and not a report of disagreement over which act occurred. *See United States v. DiLapi*, 651 F.2d 140, 145-46 (2d Cir. 1981) (notes asking whether every alleged act had to be proved did not show uncertainty about unanimity). The answer was accurate: one overt act was sufficient. It corrected a misunderstanding of the government's burden and did not tell the jury that it could convict without agreement. It was given against a unanimity direction that had been delivered and never withdrawn. That distinguishes a case in which jurors are told they need not agree on which of several specified acts was committed. *See DiLapi*, 651 F.2d at 146. Read together, the charge and the answer do not present an error that is plain.

We do not decide whether jurors must agree on a particular overt act in a conspiracy prosecution, or whether a materially different and more complex prosecution would require a particularized unanimity instruction. The charge as given presents no plain error on either view of those questions. We add a word about practice. Where the evidence permits jurors to select among distinct acts or statements, a specific direction can add clarity, and it may be sound practice to give one. *See Natelli*, 527 F.2d at 325. But a course that is advisable is not thereby required, and its omission without objection is not plain error. *Peterson*, 768 F.2d at 68; *cf. United States v. Remington*, 191 F.2d 246, 250 (2d Cir. 1951) (describing a requested instruction on agreement as to time and place as correct, but not treating its refusal as reversible where its substance was probably covered by the unanimity instructions). Because Shaoul has not shown an error that is plain, we do not reach the remaining requirements of *Olano*.

*C. The remaining objections.* Shaoul also objects that the court's more particular description of the alleged falsehoods broadened the indictment, and that a sentence reciting a stipulation that mailings occurred diluted the mailing element. A constructive broadening of an indictment differs from a clarification of the falsehoods it charges, and Shaoul's objections do not show that the jury was permitted to convict of an offense different from the one charged. A stipulation that mailings occurred establishes that fact but does not remove the separate requirement that each mailing be incident to an essential part of the scheme. *See Schmuck v. United States*, 489 U.S. 705, 710-11 (1989). The sentence reciting the stipulation is read within the charge as a whole, *Locascio*, 6 F.3d at 942, and so read it did not remove that requirement. Neither objection shows an error that is plain. As with the unanimity claim, our conclusion rests on the charge and the governing error standard, not on the strength of the government's proof.

**IV. Conclusion.** The conceded honest omission defeats the nondisclosure claim; the juror's indirect relationship to an uninvolved prosecutor does not by itself establish disqualifying bias; the record shows no actual bias and no ground for a hearing; and the charge presents no plain error. The judgment of conviction and the order denying a new trial are affirmed.

## What the decision does

**Controlling holdings.** The opinion is precedential on four propositions, each joined by all three judges.

1. Part II.B, joined by all three judges: a conceded honest voir dire omission fails the first requirement of *McDonough*, and *Langford* does not remove it.
2. Part II.C, joined by all three: a juror's indirect relationship to an assistant United States attorney in the prosecuting district, who had no involvement in the case, does not by itself establish disqualifying implied bias. The test and the burden are the movant's. Closer ties, participation, exposure to case information, and evidence of partiality are reserved.
3. Part II.D, joined by all three: no actual bias is shown on a record in which counsel conceded good faith and declined to examine the juror, and no hearing is warranted.
4. Parts III.B and III.C, joined by all three: the unobjected-to charge, read as a whole with the accurate answer to the jury's question, presents no plain error on unanimity, the overt-act answer, the description of the falsehoods or the mailing stipulation, and the court does not hold that a particularized unanimity or overt-act instruction is required.

**Treatment of precedent and limits.** The opinion follows and applies *McDonough*, *Langford*, *Colombo*, *Dennis*, *Olano*, *Schiff*, *Natelli*, *Harris* and *Peterson* and overrules none of them. It adopts no new rule beyond the four propositions above. It does not hold that the claim of juror bias is waived, does not hold that kinship to a prosecuting-office lawyer is irrelevant, does not decide whether jurors must agree on a particular overt act, and does not decide whether a more complex prosecution would require a particularized unanimity instruction. Its observations about closer relationships and about trial practice are not holdings. *Garland* is treated as persuasive only.

**Effect on the parties.** The affirmance is party-specific relief: it leaves Shaoul's conviction and sentence in place. It creates no law of the case beyond this appeal, and it is not a decision on any sentencing question, since none was raised. The judgment of conviction and the order denying a new trial are affirmed in full. The court orders no remand, no evidentiary hearing on the juror, no new trial and no sentencing relief.
