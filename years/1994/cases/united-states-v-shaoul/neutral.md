# United States v. Shaoul

## Case identification and simulation point

**Docket:** 94-1093. **Decision date:** December 1, 1994. **Citation:** 41 F.3d 811. **Court below:** Southern District of New York, Chief Judge Dominick L. DiCarlo of the Court of International Trade, sitting by designation. **Historical argument:** October 19, 1994. The appeal presents juror nondisclosure and jury-instruction issues following convictions for conspiracy and mail fraud. [Opinion and record account](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/41/811/564137/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

Jack Shaoul, an antiques dealer, submitted insurance claims arising from two alleged losses. In the first, he reported paintings and other items stolen from his car in January 1991. His employee Edward Purcell testified that a painting attributed to Albert Pinkham Ryder remained in the shop and that Shaoul directed him to conceal it. Purcell photographed it beside a current newspaper after the reported theft. Louis Liskin described a backdated purchase-interest letter intended to support an inflated valuation and testified that he had understood the painting was not authentic.

The second scheme followed a genuine water leak at the shop in February 1991. Witnesses described moving previously broken glassware into the wet area and further damaging a purported Winslow Homer watercolor that had been identified as inauthentic. The valuation documents included an invoice for a sale that the named gallery owner testified had not occurred. Purcell and Liskin testified under nonprosecution agreements, a fact relevant to credibility rather than a reason to disregard their evidence automatically. Other witnesses and documents provided corroboration.

The jury convicted Shaoul of one conspiracy count and ten substantive mail-fraud counts. Before deliberations the judge gave a general unanimity instruction, explained that the government need not prove every alleged overt act, and twice required unanimity concerning the material falsity of the relevant representations. When the jury asked whether all overt acts had to be proved, the court repeated that one was sufficient. Defense counsel did not object to the original instructions or that response.

After the verdict, the government learned that a juror was the uncle of an assistant United States attorney's wife. The prosecutor-relative worked in the same district but had no involvement in this prosecution. The juror had not recalled the relative's employment during voir dire. When the government made the juror available at sentencing, counsel expressly conceded good faith and waived examination, arguing that the relationship alone required a new trial.

The district court denied that motion and the instruction challenge. It imposed concurrent forty-month prison terms, two years of supervised release, a $50,000 fine, the costs of imprisonment and supervision, and $13,356.90 restitution. The appellate issues do not independently challenge each sentencing component. *Shaoul*, 41 F.3d at 812-15.

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The final criminal judgment is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, with trial jurisdiction supplied by 18 U.S.C. § 3231. The denial of a new-trial motion is reviewed for abuse of discretion, including independent examination of the legal rule used to assess nondisclosure and implied bias.

Preservation differs between the claims. Counsel deliberately relinquished the opportunity to question the juror and expressly declined to allege dishonesty. That is not merely a failure to object. The claimed legal consequence of the disclosed relationship remains available for review on the resulting record. By contrast, the instruction objections were not made when the court could correct the charge or its response and receive plain-error review under Rules 30 and 52(b).

*United States v. Olano*, 507 U.S. 725 (1993), requires an error that is plain and affects substantial rights, followed by the appropriate discretionary assessment of its effect on the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the proceedings. A preferred additional instruction is not necessarily a legally required instruction, and an unpreserved omission is not presumed prejudicial.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*McDonough Power Equipment, Inc. v. Greenwood*, 464 U.S. 548, 556 (1984), requires a materially dishonest voir dire answer and a showing that an accurate answer would have supported a challenge for cause. *United States v. Langford*, 990 F.2d 65, 68 (2d Cir. 1993), does not delete the first requirement. Its examination of the second followed a finding that dishonesty existed. Counsel's express concession of good faith therefore defeats this particular nondisclosure route on the record he elected to submit.

That does not establish that a genuinely biased juror may serve simply because an answer was honest. An independent bias claim must be distinguished from failure to satisfy *McDonough*. Shaoul's difficulty is evidentiary and legal: the record establishes a particular indirect relationship to a prosecutor uninvolved in his case, not actual bias or a close connection to the prosecuting team.

*United States v. Colombo*, 869 F.2d 149, 152 (2d Cir. 1989), rejected an excessively broad suggestion that prosecutorial family relationships could never justify cause challenges. Its discussion of relatives of lawyers actually involved in the investigation or prosecution does not require exclusion of every relative of every lawyer employed by the office. The distinction concerns a source of potential partiality, not an arbitrary preference for one degree of kinship. Conversely, *Garland v. United States*, 182 F.2d 801, 802 (4th Cir. 1950), is not binding and should not be read to make family ties categorically irrelevant.

The instruction issue is governed by *United States v. Schiff*, 801 F.2d 108, 114-15 (2d Cir. 1986), *United States v. Harris*, 8 F.3d 943, 945 (2d Cir. 1993), and the earlier line including *United States v. Natelli*, 527 F.2d 311, 325 (2d Cir. 1975), and *United States v. Remington*, 191 F.2d 246, 250 (2d Cir. 1951). A general unanimity charge ordinarily suffices; unusual complexity or a genuine risk that jurors will convict on incompatible factual bases can require greater specificity. *United States v. Peterson*, 768 F.2d 64, 68 (2d Cir. 1985), also constrains an attempt to turn an advisable additional charge into plain error.

Shaoul's strongest point is that several acts and alleged falsehoods could permit different jurors to select different predicates. But the actual mail-fraud charge expressly required unanimity concerning material falsity. The overt-act question asked whether every act had to be established; it did not necessarily reveal disagreement over which act occurred. The judge's accurate answer did not suspend the general unanimity command. The appeal need not decide every abstract question about whether jurors must agree on a specific overt act if the charge is adequate even assuming the proposed agreement requirement.

The remaining instruction objections do not establish a different offense from the one charged. Describing the alleged falsehoods more particularly is not necessarily a constructive broadening of the indictment. Likewise, a stipulation that mailings occurred does not remove the separate requirement that they have the required relationship to the scheme. The entire charge, rather than the stipulation sentence in isolation, supplies the legal direction.
