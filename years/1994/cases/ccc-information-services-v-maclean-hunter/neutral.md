## Record and issues

The Red Book published periodically updated, region-specific forecasts of used-vehicle values. The reported evidence distinguishes the numbers from historical sale quotations or mechanically calculated averages: editors exercised judgment about expected values, regional groupings, options, and mileage adjustments. CCC systematically copied substantial portions into its commercial databases, offering individual figures and averages with another guide. The lower record included canceled Red Book subscriptions in favor of CCC’s service. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/044/44.F3d.61.93-7687.1312.html))

The district court found insufficient originality, treated the values as facts or merged ideas, and concluded that state regulatory references had placed the material in the public domain. CCC also pleaded intended use, fair use, waiver, estoppel, consent, and untimeliness. The retrieved account does not independently establish the procedural disposition of every one of those defenses. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/044/44.F3d.61.93-7687.1312.html))

**Jurisdiction:** Final judgment in a Copyright Act controversy, with original jurisdiction under § 1338 and appellate review under § 1291. **Preservation:** Originality, merger, and public-domain arguments were squarely decided. The status of other pleaded defenses requires confirmation before an unconditional liability directive. **Additional material:** Amici appeared, but the complete briefs and their specific positions were not retrieved. **Missing material:** Complete summary-judgment submissions, Red Book samples, remaining-defense rulings, judgment date, and notice history.

### Issue 1: Originality and protected contribution

**Review and law:** Summary judgment and the legal protection question are reviewed de novo. Sections 101, 102, and 103 protect sufficiently original expression and compilation choices, not facts as such. *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone Service*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), requires independent creation and minimal creativity, not novelty or mere labor. *Kregos v. Associated Press*, 937 F.2d 700 (2d Cir. 1991), *Key Publications v. Chinatown Today*, 945 F.2d 509 (2d Cir. 1991), and *Eckes v. Card Prices Update*, 736 F.2d 859 (2d Cir. 1984), address original selection and evaluative compilations. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/044/44.F3d.61.93-7687.1312.html))

**Positions:** Maclean points to predictive judgment and discretionary selection. CCC argues that vehicle values and their efficient presentation are functional information. The correct inquiry separates public facts and underlying valuation methods from original contributions actually copied.

**Paths:** Reject protection for mechanical facts or unoriginal arrangement, but do not deny all protection simply because forecasts are numerical. Any infringement judgment must identify the protected material, not reward effort alone.

### Issue 2: Merger

**Governing law:** Section 102(b), *Baker v. Selden*, 101 U.S. 99, and circuit merger doctrine preserve ideas, systems, and methods. *Kregos* cautions against defining a compilation’s idea so specifically that all original selection necessarily disappears into it. The circuit assesses merger in the context of the alleged copying. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/044/44.F3d.61.93-7687.1312.html))

**Positions:** CCC argues that the idea “this vehicle is worth this amount” can be expressed only by the figure. Maclean argues that CCC copied virtually the entire evaluative product, not a necessary fragment to discuss an idea or independently value a vehicle.

**Open question and paths:** Determine the proper level of abstraction and the actual scope of copying. Wholesale appropriation matters, but cannot by itself transform an unprotectable idea into expression. A narrow decision protecting original forecasts and compilation choices need not monopolize valuation methods or independently reached estimates.

### Issue 3: State regulatory reference and public access

**Law:** The state provisions identified the Red Book or an average involving it as an alternative valuation standard, with other approved methods available. *Building Officials & Code Administrators v. Code Technology*, 628 F.2d 730 (1st Cir. 1980), expressed concerns about privately authored material adopted into law, but its preliminary-injunction disposition was not a final holding that every referenced work loses copyright. ([Law Resource](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/F3/044/44.F3d.61.93-7687.1312.html))

**Positions:** CCC emphasizes access to the standards governing insurance payments. Maclean distinguishes regulatory reference to a privately authored guide from governmental authorship or wholesale incorporation of a mandatory code.

**Paths and limits:** Reject automatic forfeiture from these references if unsupported by law, while reserving materially different cases of mandatory incorporation and necessary public access. A possible constitutional concern does not itself decide an unpleaded takings claim.

### Issue 4: Fair use, other defenses, and the judgment directive

**Governing authority:** Section 107 requires consideration of purpose, nature, amount, and market effect together. *Campbell v. Acuff-Rose*, 510 U.S. 569 (1994), rejects treating commercial character as automatically dispositive. It also cautions against treating an unsuccessful licensing request as inherently defeating fair use. ([Legal Information Institute](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/510/569))

**Positions:** Maclean relies on extensive commercial substitution and market displacement. CCC can distinguish transformative or otherwise justified uses from simple resale and invoke its properly preserved defenses. The reported copying is strongly unfavorable to a defense of mere competitive republication, but that assessment does not establish that every pleaded defense was adjudicated.

**Remedy constraints:** Reverse erroneous summary judgment. Direct liability judgment only if the record conclusively establishes infringement and eliminates all material preserved defenses. Otherwise remand those defenses and the remedy rather than infer their abandonment.
