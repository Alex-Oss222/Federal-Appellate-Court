## Record and issues

**Material record:** The Pro-Choice Network, clinics, and physicians sued individuals and organizations associated with anti-abortion demonstrations in western New York. The complaint filed September 24, 1990 asserted a conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) and state-law claims, including trespass and New York civil-rights claims. The litigation concerned obstruction and harassment affecting access to medical facilities as well as protected advocacy on public sidewalks.

An initial restraining order prohibited obstruction of clinic access. A planned demonstration then proceeded without the anticipated blockade, and the order continued by agreement. The later record nevertheless included crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment, and contempt proceedings against several defendants. The significance of that history was disputed: plaintiffs treated it as proof that ordinary prohibitions had failed; defendants emphasized occasions on which the restraining order protected access and argued that the record did not justify excluding peaceful speakers.

The preliminary injunction prohibited obstruction and trespass, regulated health-endangering noise, and established fifteen-foot zones around entrances and driveways and around people and vehicles seeking access. It permitted two sidewalk counselors within specified areas but required them to cease counseling when an approached person declined it. Thus the decree combined fixed geographic restrictions, moving restrictions, and a listener-triggered command. Those provisions require separate analysis rather than one conclusion about whether anti-abortion protest is permissible.

After *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263 (1993), the district court dismissed the federal claim with leave to amend but retained the pendent state claims and declined to dissolve the injunction. The appeals from the original injunction and refusal to vacate were considered together. The principal district opinions are 799 F. Supp. 1417 (1992) and 828 F. Supp. 1018 (1993).

### Issue 2: Fixed and moving fifteen-foot restrictions

**Preservation and reach:** The challenged zones form part of the injunction under review; Madsen was decided during the appeal and addressed in supplemental submissions.

**Standard of review:** Independent constitutional review of the legal burden; clear error for historical findings; abuse of discretion for equitable choices within the correct speech standard.

**Governing test and burdens:** A content-neutral injunction may burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest. Fixed access corridors and zones moving around people or vehicles impose different burdens and require separate necessity analysis.

*Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 764-66 (1994), requires a content-neutral injunction affecting speech to burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest. This is more demanding than ordinary time, place, and manner review, but it is not strict scrutiny simply because an injunction binds identified anti-abortion defendants. Selection based on their conduct is different from suppression based on their viewpoint.

The strongest defense of the zones is operational: a prohibition on actual obstruction may be difficult to enforce once a crowd surrounds a patient or blocks a doorway, and the prior violations can justify preventive space. *Madsen* itself sustained an entrance-area restriction on its record. A court need not await a completed medical injury before protecting access.

The strongest challenge concerns fit. A moving fifteen-foot exclusion can prevent peaceful communication even when a person is walking freely and no doorway is obstructed. A fixed zone may have a stronger access justification, but its location and extent must be connected to the actual obstruction problem. The existence of a numerical limit does not establish tailoring. The cease-and-desist provision goes further still if it prohibits otherwise lawful speech solely because a listener has rejected the message, rather than prohibiting following, intimidation, or interference with departure.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Madsen v. Women’s Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753, 764-66 (1994), supplies the controlling injunction standard and recognizes an entrance-access restriction on its own record.

**Other governing authority:** First Amendment; Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(d); the supported state-law prohibitions on obstruction and trespass. A prior violation may support necessity but is not a categorical prerequisite to every preventive restriction.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Repeated crowding and interference make prevention necessary before a patient is surrounded or a doorway blocked; ordinary obstruction language has not reliably secured access.

### Issue 3: Listener-triggered cessation, noise, and physical-access commands

**Preservation and reach:** The cease-and-desist requirement and noise/access terms are distinct provisions under review; their validity cannot be decided merely from approval or rejection of the zones.

**Standard of review:** Independent First Amendment and Rule 65(d) analysis, with proper review of underlying findings and remedial discretion.

**Governing test and burdens:** A public listener may decline communication and proceed freely, but objection to the message alone is not a censor’s authority. Objective prohibitions on physical interference, coercive following, and medically dangerous noise serve different interests from protection against offense.

The interests in unobstructed clinic access, public safety, and safe medical treatment are substantial. The First Amendment does not authorize trespass, physical obstruction, assault, or threats. Conversely, a listener's objection to a message does not itself authorize the government to suppress public-sidewalk advocacy. *Boos v. Barry*, 485 U.S. 312 (1988), forecloses treating emotional offense to political expression as a general justification for suppressing it. Restrictions tied to medically consequential noise must be distinguished from restrictions justified merely by distress at the message.

The noise provision is not necessarily invalid for using health and safety language. Read against the record and the medical setting, an objective prohibition on excessively loud sound that endangers patients can be enforceable without requiring a protester to know each patient's private condition. It would be different if enforcement turned on whether clinic occupants disliked the speaker's viewpoint. Rule 65(d)'s specificity requirement and the First Amendment both constrain that distinction.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Boos v. Barry, 485 U.S. 312 (1988), rejects protection from political offense as a general suppression interest. Madsen supplies the burden/necessity rule for this injunction.

**Other governing authority:** First Amendment; Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(d); state-law access and trespass protections.

**Applicable simulation holding:** None supplied or verified. No proposed position in these briefs is an adopted holding.

**Open question or conflict:** Whether the terms regulate objectively harmful conduct with adequate specificity or reach speech because of the unwilling listener’s reaction.

**Appellant's strongest supported position:** The cessation provision suppresses otherwise peaceful sidewalk communication solely on the listener’s instruction, and unclear health language may invite viewpoint-based enforcement.

**Appellee's strongest supported position:** Patients must be able to refuse contact and obtain treatment without harassment; the medical setting makes objectively harmful noise and interference particularly serious.

**Additional material position:** No independently verified additional amicus position is supplied. Counsel or amicus names alone are not evidence of an argument.

**Error and remedy constraints:** First Amendment tailoring and Rule 65(d) specificity apply term by term. A defect in one speech restriction does not automatically defeat supported physical-access and safety relief; retention of jurisdiction does not validate every term.

**Lawful paths:** These alternatives are unranked and imply no vote or order of decision.

Alternative: Sustain sufficiently definite physical-access and objective health-protective terms.

Alternative: Vacate listener-controlled or insufficiently tailored speech suppression.
