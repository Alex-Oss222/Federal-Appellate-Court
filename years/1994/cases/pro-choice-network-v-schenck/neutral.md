# Pro-Choice Network of Western New York v. Schenck

## Case identification and simulation point

**Dockets:** 92-7302 and 93-7918. **Decision date:** September 6, 1994. **Citation:** 67 F.3d 359. **Court below:** Western District of New York, Judge Richard J. Arcara. **Historical argument:** March 24, 1994. The simulation is the 1994 panel decision, not the later en banc decision reported at 67 F.3d 377. *Madsen*, decided during the appeal and addressed through supplemental submissions, is available law. [1994 panel opinion](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/67/359/584635/).

## Material facts and proceedings below

The Pro-Choice Network, clinics, and physicians sued individuals and organizations associated with anti-abortion demonstrations in western New York. The complaint filed September 24, 1990 asserted a conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. § 1985(3) and state-law claims, including trespass and New York civil-rights claims. The litigation concerned obstruction and harassment affecting access to medical facilities as well as protected advocacy on public sidewalks.

An initial restraining order prohibited obstruction of clinic access. A planned demonstration then proceeded without the anticipated blockade, and the order continued by agreement. The later record nevertheless included crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment, and contempt proceedings against several defendants. The significance of that history was disputed: plaintiffs treated it as proof that ordinary prohibitions had failed; defendants emphasized occasions on which the restraining order protected access and argued that the record did not justify excluding peaceful speakers.

The preliminary injunction prohibited obstruction and trespass, regulated health-endangering noise, and established fifteen-foot zones around entrances and driveways and around people and vehicles seeking access. It permitted two sidewalk counselors within specified areas but required them to cease counseling when an approached person declined it. Thus the decree combined fixed geographic restrictions, moving restrictions, and a listener-triggered command. Those provisions require separate analysis rather than one conclusion about whether anti-abortion protest is permissible.

After *Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic*, 506 U.S. 263 (1993), the district court dismissed the federal claim with leave to amend but retained the pendent state claims and declined to dissolve the injunction. The appeals from the original injunction and refusal to vacate were considered together. The principal district opinions are 799 F. Supp. 1417 (1992) and 828 F. Supp. 1018 (1993).

## Questions, jurisdiction, preservation, and review

The injunction orders are reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1). The federal claim supplied the original federal question; the continued exercise of pendent jurisdiction is assessed under *United Mine Workers v. Gibbs*, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), and *Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill*, 484 U.S. 343 (1988). Because this action began before § 1367's effective application to newly filed actions, the analysis should not mechanically invoke that statute as its original jurisdictional source.

*Bray* rejected the proposition that opposition to abortion necessarily supplies the class-based animus required by the pertinent § 1985(3) clause. It also requires the appropriate connection to the protected federal right, rather than incidental interference with interstate travel. Those federal limitations do not automatically extinguish otherwise cognizable state-law claims or invalidate the discretionary retention of a substantially developed case.

The legal First Amendment standard is reviewed independently; factual findings receive clear-error review; the equitable decree is reviewed for abuse of discretion within correct legal limits. The court must exercise the independent constitutional judgment required for speech restrictions, without inventing events not found below. New abstention and state-law arguments not properly raised in the relevant proceedings cannot become grounds for reversal merely because the federal claim changed. The parties remain free to present properly preserved questions of state-law remedial authority on remand.

## Governing law and independent neutral analysis

*Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc.*, 512 U.S. 753, 764-66 (1994), requires a content-neutral injunction affecting speech to burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest. This is more demanding than ordinary time, place, and manner review, but it is not strict scrutiny simply because an injunction binds identified anti-abortion defendants. Selection based on their conduct is different from suppression based on their viewpoint.

The interests in unobstructed clinic access, public safety, and safe medical treatment are substantial. The First Amendment does not authorize trespass, physical obstruction, assault, or threats. Conversely, a listener's objection to a message does not itself authorize the government to suppress public-sidewalk advocacy. *Boos v. Barry*, 485 U.S. 312 (1988), forecloses treating emotional offense to political expression as a general justification for suppressing it. Restrictions tied to medically consequential noise must be distinguished from restrictions justified merely by distress at the message.

The strongest defense of the zones is operational: a prohibition on actual obstruction may be difficult to enforce once a crowd surrounds a patient or blocks a doorway, and the prior violations can justify preventive space. *Madsen* itself sustained an entrance-area restriction on its record. A court need not await a completed medical injury before protecting access.

The strongest challenge concerns fit. A moving fifteen-foot exclusion can prevent peaceful communication even when a person is walking freely and no doorway is obstructed. A fixed zone may have a stronger access justification, but its location and extent must be connected to the actual obstruction problem. The existence of a numerical limit does not establish tailoring. The cease-and-desist provision goes further still if it prohibits otherwise lawful speech solely because a listener has rejected the message, rather than prohibiting following, intimidation, or interference with departure.

The noise provision is not necessarily invalid for using health and safety language. Read against the record and the medical setting, an objective prohibition on excessively loud sound that endangers patients can be enforceable without requiring a protester to know each patient's private condition. It would be different if enforcement turned on whether clinic occupants disliked the speaker's viewpoint. Rule 65(d)'s specificity requirement and the First Amendment both constrain that distinction.
