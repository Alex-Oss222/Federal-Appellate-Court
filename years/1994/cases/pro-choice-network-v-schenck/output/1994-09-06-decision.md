## Decision summary

Anti-abortion demonstrators and the organizations associated with them appealed from a preliminary injunction that protects access to medical clinics in western New York, and from the district court's later refusal to dissolve it. The Second Circuit affirmed some parts of the injunction, vacated others, and sent the case back to the district court.

The court first held that the district court could keep the New York state-law claims, and could leave the injunction in place as a whole, after the federal claim was dismissed. The court then tested the injunction term by term under Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994), which requires a content-neutral injunction that affects speech to burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest.

Applying that test, the court:

- vacated the fifteen-foot zones that move with each person or vehicle seeking access to a clinic, because the findings did not tie that burden on peaceful speech to the access problem;
- vacated the fifteen-foot zones fixed at entrances and driveways, and remanded for differentiated, site-specific necessity findings, while holding that a fixed zone is not categorically invalid;
- vacated the command that sidewalk counselors stop counseling when an approached person declines, because it silenced peaceful speech that obstructs no one;
- affirmed the noise term as an objective limit on noise that endangers patients' health, and directed the district court to conform its wording to Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65(d); and
- affirmed the terms against obstruction, trespass, crowding, pushing and threats.

Judge Meskill wrote the opinion of the court. Judge Stone-Khoury joined it in full. Judge Oakes joined all of it except Part IV.B and the fixed-zone instruction in Part VII, and dissented in part on that single point: he would have affirmed the fixed zones. The vacatur takes effect only when the mandate issues, and no mandate has issued.

## The case and the result

**Decisions under review.** The defendants, individuals and organizations associated with anti-abortion demonstrations in western New York, appeal from the preliminary injunction reported at 799 F. Supp. 1417 (W.D.N.Y. 1992) and from the later refusal to vacate it, reported at 828 F. Supp. 1018 (W.D.N.Y. 1993). They ask for reversal of the injunction and its dissolution. The clinics and physicians ask that the injunction and its challenged restrictions be affirmed. The orders are appealable under 28 U.S.C. 1292(a)(1). The separate entry dates of the orders and the timeliness of the notices of appeal are not before the panel for decision.

**Material record.** The plaintiffs sued on September 24, 1990, pleading a conspiracy under 42 U.S.C. 1985(3) and state-law claims including trespass and claims under New York's civil-rights law. The suit concerned obstruction and harassment affecting access to medical facilities, together with advocacy on public sidewalks that is protected speech. An initial restraining order forbade obstruction of clinic access. A planned demonstration then went forward without the blockade that had been anticipated, and the order continued by agreement. The later record nonetheless showed crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment, and contempt proceedings against several defendants. The parties disputed what that record means. The plaintiffs say it shows that ordinary prohibitions had failed. The defendants point to occasions on which the restraining order did protect access and say the record does not justify excluding peaceful speakers.

The preliminary injunction forbade obstruction and trespass, regulated health-endangering noise, and created fifteen-foot zones around entrances and driveways and around persons and vehicles seeking access. It permitted two sidewalk counselors in specified areas but required them to stop counseling when an approached person declined. The decree thus combined fixed zones, zones that move with persons and vehicles, and a command triggered by a listener's refusal.

After the Supreme Court decided Bray v. Alexandria Women's Health Clinic, 506 U.S. 263 (1993), on January 13, 1993, the district court dismissed the federal claim with leave to amend, retained the pendent state claims, and declined to dissolve the injunction. Whether the federal claim was later amended is not before the panel.

**Issues and standards of review.**

1. Whether the district court could and should retain the pendent state claims, and refuse to dissolve the injunction, after the federal claim was dismissed. The federal legal premise is reviewed independently; retention is reviewed for abuse of discretion under United Mine Workers v. Gibbs, 383 U.S. 715 (1966), and Carnegie-Mellon University v. Cohill, 484 U.S. 343 (1988).
2. Whether the fixed and the moving fifteen-foot restrictions are lawful. The legal burden is reviewed independently; findings of fact for clear error; equitable choices for abuse of discretion within the correct legal standard.
3. Whether the cease-on-refusal command, the noise term and the physical-access commands are lawful. Review is independent as to the First Amendment and Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(d), with clear-error review of findings and abuse-of-discretion review of remedial choices.

**Preservation.** Abstention and state-law remedial-authority arguments that were not properly raised in the proceedings under review cannot become grounds for reversal because the federal claim changed. The standard of review for each term is applied term by term.

**Result.** The court affirmed the retention of the pendent claims and the refusal to dissolve the injunction as a whole; vacated the moving zones, the fixed zones as presently justified, and the cease-on-refusal command; affirmed the noise term subject to conformity with Rule 65(d); and affirmed the obstruction, trespass and physical-interference terms. The case is remanded for modification of the decree and for the findings the opinion requires. The components and votes are set out in the table above.

## Opinion of the court

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision.*

Meskill, Senior Circuit Judge, delivered the opinion of the court, in which Oakes, Senior Circuit Judge, and Stone-Khoury, Circuit Judge, join, except that Judge Oakes does not join Part IV.B or the fixed-zone instruction in Part VII.

### I. Scope

The defendants oppose abortion and demonstrate outside clinics. They challenge an injunction protecting access to those clinics and the district court's refusal to dissolve it after the federal claim fell away. Two questions are presented: whether the federal court could keep the state-law case, and whether each of the decree's distinct speech and access terms survives review under Madsen v. Women's Health Center, Inc., 512 U.S. 753 (1994).

Neither the clinics' lawful medical work nor the defendants' religious and political advocacy is optional in the analysis. New York law protects against trespass and interference, the First Amendment protects peaceful communication in traditional public forums, and no special doctrine gives either side an automatic advantage. Madsen supplies the test. We apply it term by term, because a defect in one speech restriction does not defeat supported access and safety relief, and retaining jurisdiction does not validate every term.

### II. Pendent jurisdiction

A federal court has power over state-law claims that share a common nucleus of operative fact with a substantial federal claim. Gibbs, 383 U.S. at 725-27. Power is measured at the outset. A federal claim fails to support it only if it is so insubstantial that "its unsoundness so clearly results from the previous decisions of this court as to foreclose the subject." Hagans v. Lavine, 415 U.S. 528, 536-37 (1974). Once power exists, the later loss of the federal claim does not remove it.

Whether to keep the pendent claims is then a matter of discretion guided by judicial economy, convenience, fairness and comity. Cohill, 484 U.S. at 350 & n.7. Gibbs's observation that state claims should ordinarily be dismissed when federal claims are dismissed before trial describes the usual result when those factors favor declining jurisdiction. It is not a mandatory rule to be applied inflexibly. Id. We review the federal premise independently and the decision to retain for abuse of discretion. The action was commenced before 28 U.S.C. 1367 applied to newly filed actions, so that statute is not the source of the court's power, and we do not rely on it.

The complaint pleaded a substantial federal claim under 42 U.S.C. 1985(3) when it was filed. Bray later rejected the two theories on which such a claim depended, that opposition to abortion supplies the class-based animus the statute requires and that incidental interference with interstate travel suffices. A holding that a claim fails on the merits is not a holding that prior decisions had foreclosed it when it was brought. Bray therefore removed no power. It bore only on discretion, and discretion was not abused.

The trespass and civil-rights claims arise from the same obstruction and harassment at the same facilities as the federal claim. By the time Bray was decided the district court had entered a restraining order, conducted the proceedings that produced the preliminary injunction, and handled contempt proceedings. The access dispute continued, and the state claims reach it. Judicial economy and fairness favored completing a developed case over starting it again elsewhere. Arguments based on abstention or on the limits of New York's remedial authority were not properly raised in the proceedings under review and cannot supply a ground for reversal now; any such question that was properly preserved remains open on remand.

The defendants' principal argument is that once Bray eliminated the federal theory the court should have relinquished the state controversy and dissolved the injunction. That describes one permissible exercise of discretion, not a command. Dissolving the whole decree would also have stripped away protections that are independently lawful, as Parts V and VI explain. We affirm the retention of the pendent claims and the refusal to dissolve the injunction as a whole. We do not decide whether the dismissed federal claim was or may be repleaded, and we decide no question arising under federal legislation enacted after the injunction issued.

### III. The governing standard

An injunction that is content-neutral and that affects speech must burden no more speech than necessary to serve a significant governmental interest. Madsen, 512 U.S. at 764-66. That test is more demanding than ordinary time, place and manner review, but it is not strict scrutiny, and the fact that a decree binds defendants who oppose abortion does not make it viewpoint based. The question is whether each term is justified by what these defendants did, not by what they say.

The interests in unobstructed access, public safety and safe medical treatment are significant. The First Amendment does not protect trespass, physical obstruction, assault or threats. On the other side, a listener's objection to a message is not a censor's authority, and offense at political expression is not a general ground for suppressing it. Boos v. Barry, 485 U.S. 312 (1988). Those who defend a term that burdens speech must show, through findings, that it is needed. A prior violation can support necessity, but it is not a prerequisite to every preventive restriction, and a court need not wait for a completed injury before protecting access. Rule 65(d) separately requires that an injunction be specific in its terms and describe in reasonable detail the acts restrained.

### IV. The fifteen-foot restrictions

#### A. The moving zones (Judges Meskill, Oakes and Stone-Khoury)

A zone that travels with every person or vehicle seeking access forbids speakers to stand or walk in public space near that person wherever the person goes. It burdens peaceful communication even when no doorway is blocked and no one is followed. Under Madsen it stands only if the findings connect that burden to the access problem. That some defendants obstructed patients on other occasions is not enough. A decree may reach persons acting in concert with wrongdoers, Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(d), but association does not erase the requirement that the prohibited conduct be adequately connected to the violation.

The findings describe crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment and contempt. They justify firm conduct-based protection. They do not explain why access could be secured only by forbidding a counselor to be within fifteen feet of a moving person who is not blocked, followed or encircled. A counselor who is simply standing or walking may be forced away as the protected person moves. On this record the actual burden is insufficiently tied to access.

The plaintiffs answer that a prohibition on obstruction is hard to enforce once a crowd has surrounded a patient and that earlier violations show ordinary prohibitions failed. Difficulty of enforcement can justify a preventive measure, but only one that fits the difficulty. Madsen sustained a zone fixed at a clinic's entrances and driveway on findings that the protesters had interfered with access despite an earlier, narrower injunction. It did not sustain an exclusion that moves with each person. Nor do we treat the occasions on which the restraining order secured access as proof that conduct-based terms would suffice. Our holding rests on the absence of findings tying the moving exclusion to access.

We do not hold that every fifteen-foot zone is invalid. The moving zones are vacated. On specific findings the district court may adopt narrower protections defined by conduct, such as bars on blocking, encircling or following persons seeking access, and if the findings show those terms have failed it may consider what further restriction Madsen permits.

#### B. The fixed zones (Judges Meskill and Stone-Khoury)

A zone fixed at doorways and vehicle entrances is not invalid in principle, and its validity does not depend on a completed blockade. Entrances and driveways can require preventive clearance, and a peaceful demonstration under the earlier order does not disprove every need for it. But a fixed zone is a distinct burden and needs its own necessity findings: why excluding speakers from that area is needed to keep entrances and approaches open when the decree's obstruction and trespass commands remain in force, whether passage remains genuinely possible, and why the chosen dimension is necessary. A numerical limit does not establish tailoring.

As the findings stand, the same rationale supports the fixed and the moving zones alike. They do not separately connect a fifteen-foot exclusion at these entrances and driveways to the actual layout of those places or explain why the surviving obstruction and trespass terms cannot keep them open. We do not reject any found fact, and we respect clear-error review. We hold that the findings are legally insufficient to show that the fixed zones burden no more speech than necessary. A less restrictive alternative is not dispositive by itself; it is part of the necessity showing Madsen requires.

The plaintiffs and the dissent rely on Madsen's sustaining of a thirty-six-foot zone at a clinic's entrances and driveway, and observe that these zones are narrower. Madsen sustained its zone on findings that interference with access had continued despite an earlier injunction, and it set aside a buffer where the record showed no interference from that location. Madsen thus confirms that an entrance zone can be justified. It does not hold that any zone smaller than thirty-six feet is justified, and a comparison of numbers is no substitute for findings about this site.

We hold that no fixed zone is categorically barred, we announce no footage rule, and we do not require the district court to await another blockade. We decide nothing about whether fifteen feet, or any other dimension, could be sustained on supported findings. The fixed zones are vacated without prejudice to a renewed fixed access zone, separately justified by differentiated, site-specific findings under Madsen.

### V. The cease-on-refusal command (Judges Meskill, Oakes and Stone-Khoury)

A person approached on a public sidewalk may decline to listen and go on freely. A decree may secure that freedom, and the freedom to leave, refuse literature and obtain treatment, by forbidding blocking, coercive following and harassment, each defined through objective conduct. It may not make a listener's refusal alone sufficient to silence otherwise peaceful advocacy in a public forum. Madsen invalidated a provision that barred approaching persons seeking clinic services unless they indicated a desire to communicate, because it burdened more speech than necessary to prevent intimidation and to ensure access. Madsen, Part III-D. Boos forecloses treating a listener's displeasure with a message as a general ground for suppression.

The command here attached whenever an approached person declined, whether or not the counselor blocked, followed, threatened or otherwise interfered. The interests in free passage and freedom from intimidation can be served by objective conduct terms, and the decree retains such terms. The command added a prohibition on peaceful speech that obstructs no one. The initial permission to counsel does not cure an unjustified prohibition on all further counseling after a refusal. We rest the ruling on Madsen's necessity requirement and on the absence of any objective conduct limit. We do not rest it on any captive-audience theory or on the emotive impact of the message.

The plaintiffs answer that patients must be able to refuse contact and obtain treatment without harassment. They must, and the surviving terms secure that freedom. The command is vacated as written. We reserve a properly supported restriction on objectively coercive following or harassment, and particular enforcement questions not presented.

### VI. The noise and physical-access terms (Judges Meskill, Oakes and Stone-Khoury)

**Noise.** The First Amendment does not require patients at a medical facility to make Herculean efforts to escape the din of protest, and Madsen sustained limits on noise at a clinic. Madsen, Part III-B. As described, the decree regulates noise that endangers patients' health. Read that way, it is an objective prohibition keyed to the effect of sound on patients, not to the message or the speaker's viewpoint, and it does not require a protester to know any patient's private condition. Enforcement may not turn on whether clinic occupants dislike the speaker.

The defendants argue that health language is unclear and invites viewpoint-based enforcement. Rule 65(d) and the First Amendment answer that concern by requiring definite terms, and the remedy is conformity, not vacatur. We affirm the term so read and direct that on remand its wording be conformed to Rule 65(d), stating in reasonable detail, in objective terms, what noise is prohibited.

**Physical access.** The prohibitions on obstruction and trespass, and the decree's terms against crowding, pushing and threats directed at persons seeking to enter or leave, as the decree states them, regulate conduct that New York law forbids and the First Amendment does not protect. The record of crowding, physical interference, pushing, harassment and contempt supports them. They operate independently of the zones and the cease-on-refusal command. The defendants' challenge to them fails, and they remain in force.

### VII. Disposition and remand (Judges Meskill, Oakes and Stone-Khoury, except the fixed-zone instruction, which Judges Meskill and Stone-Khoury join)

The retention of the pendent claims and the refusal to dissolve the injunction are affirmed. The moving zones and the cease-on-refusal command are vacated. The noise term is affirmed and remanded for conformity with Rule 65(d). The obstruction, trespass and physical-interference terms are affirmed. The two-counselor allowance, which operated together with the zones and the cease-on-refusal command, is to be conformed on remand to those rulings; we decide nothing about the independent validity of any limit on the number of counselors. The fixed zones are vacated and remanded for the differentiated findings described in Part IV.B. The case is remanded for modification of the decree and for the findings this opinion requires, with the district court to proceed as it deems appropriate.

## Separate opinions

### Opinion dissenting in part (Oakes, Senior Circuit Judge), not joined by any judge

*Simulated judicial opinion based on the finalized decision. The final record states this partial dissent in summary form, and it is reported here as the record states it.*

Judge Oakes dissents from Part IV.B and from the fixed-zone instruction in Part VII. No judge joins his opinion.

Judge Oakes agrees with the court on retention of the pendent claims, on vacating the moving zones and the cease-on-refusal command, on the noise term and on the obstruction, trespass and physical-interference terms. He would conform the two-counselor allowance to the moving-zone and cease-on-refusal rulings. He dissents from vacating the fixed entrance and driveway zones and from the instruction to reconsider them.

In his view the fixed zones satisfy Madsen. They are narrower than the thirty-six-foot zone sustained around a clinic's entrances and driveway there, and they rest on the district court's findings about the obstruction, crowding and interference the defendants' conduct produced. Those findings are reviewed for clear error, and the majority does not find them clearly erroneous. He would not affirm on the bare reasonableness of a number. He would affirm on the findings, on the importance of protecting patients' access, and on the district court's remedial judgment. A limited remand for site-specific findings would be appropriate only if the findings were shown to be undifferentiated, and nothing before the court shows that. He would not apply strict scrutiny, and he would not invalidate fifteen-foot zones as a class.

## What the decision does

**Controlling holding.** The opinion is published. It is precedential in this circuit to the extent of propositions joined by at least two judges of the panel.

- Joined by all three judges (Parts II, III, IV.A, V and VI, and the rest of Part VII): after Bray, a federal court that had power over pendent state claims at the outset may retain them in its discretion under Gibbs and Cohill, and Bray's rejection of the federal theory does not by itself remove that power; 28 U.S.C. 1367 is not the source of power in an action commenced before it applied. An injunction affecting speech at a clinic is tested under Madsen term by term. A zone that moves with each person or vehicle seeking access is not shown necessary by a record of prior obstruction by some defendants, absent findings connecting the actual burden to access. A command that ends peaceful advocacy solely because a listener has declined it burdens more speech than necessary. An objective limit on noise that endangers patients' health may stand if its wording conforms to Rule 65(d), and the decree's obstruction, trespass and physical-interference terms stand.
- Joined by Judges Meskill and Stone-Khoury (Part IV.B and the fixed-zone instruction in Part VII): a fixed zone at entrances and driveways is not categorically invalid and needs no completed blockade, but it requires its own differentiated necessity findings; on the findings in this case the fifteen-foot fixed zones are legally insufficient under Madsen. This is a majority ruling of the panel on two joins. Judge Oakes's partial dissent is not circuit law.

**Prior decisions.** Gibbs, Cohill, Hagans, Bray, Madsen and Boos are followed and applied. No decision of this court is overruled or displaced, and no earlier decision of this court on these issues is applied. The panel exercises no power to depart from any decision of the Supreme Court.

**Limits.** The holdings rest on the decree and findings as described. They set no footage rule, announce no categorical rule for or against buffer zones, and decide no question about the dismissed federal claim or its amendment, preserved state-law objections, claims under legislation enacted after the injunction, the independent validity of a limit on the number of counselors, or the timeliness of the appeals.

**Effect on the parties.** The judgment is affirmed in part, vacated in part, and remanded. The affirmed provisions (obstruction, trespass, physical interference, and the noise term as to be conformed) remain in force. The state-law case continues in the district court. The vacatur, the remand instructions and the modification of the decree bind the parties to this litigation. On remand the district court is bound by the rulings that the moving zones, the fixed zones as presently justified and the cease-on-refusal command are vacated and by the standards stated here. Statements describing what the district court may consider on remand are guidance on the scope of the remand and are not independent holdings.

**Scope of remand.**

1. Modify the decree to delete the moving zones and the cease-on-refusal command.
2. Conform the two-counselor allowance to those rulings and to the fixed-zone ruling.
3. Conform the noise term to Rule 65(d).
4. Reconsider any fixed access zone only on differentiated, site-specific findings applying Madsen, which may include a narrower or differently placed zone.
5. Adopt, on specific findings, any narrower protection defined by objective conduct, such as bars on blocking, encircling or coercive following.

**Mandate status.** No mandate has issued. Under Fed. R. App. P. 40(a) as in force on this date, a petition for rehearing may be filed within 14 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order or by local rule. Under Fed. R. App. P. 41(a) as in force on this date, the mandate issues 21 days after entry of judgment unless the time is shortened or enlarged by order, and a timely petition for rehearing delays issuance as Rule 41(a) provides. No order shortening or enlarging either time has been entered, and no post-decision filing is before the court. The vacatur takes effect on issuance of the mandate.
