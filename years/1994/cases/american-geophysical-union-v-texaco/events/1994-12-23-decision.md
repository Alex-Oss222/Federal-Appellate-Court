### Posture and issues

**Decision under review.** An opinion and order of the district court dated October 26, 1992, 802 F. Supp. 1, holding that Texaco's copying of eight articles from the Journal of Catalysis, retained in the files of one of its researchers, is not fair use, and certifying that ruling for interlocutory appeal under 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b). Id. at 29. The order is not a final judgment. It does not decide the legality of every photocopy made by every scientist, the damages for any claimed infringement, or the scope of an injunction. Texaco seeks reversal of the fair-use ruling. The publishers seek affirmance as to the selected copying practice.

**Material record.** The American Geophysical Union and eighty-two other publishers sued Texaco over unauthorized photocopying of scientific and technical articles. Texaco employed approximately 400 to 500 researchers nationally and maintained a substantial research library at Beacon, New York. Its subscriptions to the Journal of Catalysis rose from one to two in 1983 and to three in 1988. The parties narrowed the first trial to a written record on the fair-use defense, randomly selected one researcher, Dr. Donald H. Chickering II, a chemical engineer conducting catalysis research, and focused on eight articles from the Journal retained in his files. He or other Texaco employees made complete copies for his use. Six articles came to his attention through circulated issues and two through references in other publications. The copies were retained principally for possible later consultation rather than immediate use in an experiment, and he did not ultimately use five of them. Academic Press generally owned the copyrights in the individual articles through assignments from authors, who were not paid for publication. The articles are predominantly factual scientific writings. The record also addressed the Copyright Clearance Center's licensing arrangements, the feasibility of licensing institutional copying of this kind, and the limited evidence of added subscription demand if photocopying were unavailable. The district court found that convenient and reasonably priced licensing procedures were available to Texaco for this reproduction. 802 F. Supp. at 18, 25.

**Jurisdiction and dates.** The district court had jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1338(a). The appeal comes to this court on the district court's written certification under § 1292(b), which permits a court of appeals, in its discretion, to allow an appeal from a certified order on application made within ten days after the order's entry. The papers before the panel do not include the application, the order allowing the appeal, or docket entries bearing on its timeliness, so the appeal is decided as certified and presented, and nothing outside the certified order is reached. Relevant dates: district court opinion and certification, October 26, 1992; argument, May 20, 1993; resubmission on the papers and decision, December 23, 1994.

**Issue and standard of review.** One issue: whether Texaco's complete photocopies of the eight retained articles are fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The ultimate fair-use determination is a mixed question of law and fact, reviewed independently, and subsidiary findings of fact are reviewed for clear error. Twin Peaks Productions, Inc. v. Publications International, Ltd., 996 F.2d 1366, 1374 (2d Cir. 1993). Texaco bears the burden on its affirmative defense. The fair-use defense was tried and decided on the stipulated record, which also fixes the reach of the question: eight articles in one researcher's files, with damages and the scope of relief not submitted.

**Controlling propositions.**

1. *Framework, burden and review (Part II).* Fair use is decided by weighing the four statutory factors together in light of copyright's purposes, with no bright-line rule. Research is a listed purpose that counts but is no exemption. A commercial setting and the absence of transformation are not disqualifying. Texaco, as proponent of an affirmative defense, bears the burden, and will have difficulty carrying it without favorable evidence on the relevant markets. The ultimate question is reviewed independently and subsidiary findings for clear error. Authority: 17 U.S.C. §§ 106, 107, 108(f)(4); Campbell; Harper & Row; Sony; Twin Peaks. Newman, Winter, Stone.
2. *Factors one to three (Parts III.A to III.C).* The research purpose and the factual character of the works favor Texaco, the first factor only modestly at most because the copies are nontransformative duplicates; the reproduction of each article in full favors the publishers. Newman, Winter, Stone.
3. *Fourth factor and the licensing mechanism (Part III.D).* Loss of licensing revenue is cognizable market harm only where the market is for the kind of use at issue (a reproduction that substitutes for an authorized copy), the licensing mechanism is shown by evidence to be actually operating for that kind of use at reasonable price and convenience, and the copying in fact substitutes for what the mechanism supplies. The existence of a fee never proves harm by itself. Applied: the retained complete copies, which serve the reading and reference function of authorized copies, substitute for reproductions obtainable through the mechanism the district court found operating, so the fourth factor weighs most heavily against Texaco and decides the balance on this record. Newman, Winter, Stone.
4. *Scope and remedy (Parts IV and V).* The holding is limited to the eight retained copies on the stipulated record. Newman, Winter, Stone.

**Opinion of the court.**

NEWMAN, Chief Judge, for the court, in which WINTER and STONE, Circuit Judges, join.

This certified interlocutory appeal asks whether complete photocopies of eight scientific articles, made for one Texaco researcher and kept in his files, are a fair use under 17 U.S.C. § 107. The district court held that they are not. We affirm that limited determination, on the stipulated record and for the reasons below, and we decide nothing beyond it.

**I. The record and the question**

The publishers sued Texaco over photocopying of scientific and technical articles. The parties narrowed the first trial to a written record on fair use and selected one researcher at random, Dr. Chickering, a chemical engineer doing catalysis research. The record concerns eight Journal of Catalysis articles copied in full and kept in his files, chiefly for possible later consultation, five of which went unused. The articles are predominantly factual, and Academic Press generally owns the copyrights through assignments from unpaid authors. The stipulation frames a representative inquiry. It does not make one researcher's files a legislative record about scientific institutions generally.

**II. The governing framework**

Section 106(1) gives the copyright owner the exclusive right to reproduce the work in copies. Section 107 provides that fair use of a work for purposes such as scholarship or research is not an infringement, and it directs courts to consider the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount and substantiality of the portion used, and the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the work. The task "is not to be simplified with bright-line rules." Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 577 (1994). Each factor is explored and the results are weighed together in light of copyright's purposes. Id. at 578. They are not four votes to be counted.

Research is a listed purpose and it counts, but it is not an automatic exemption. A commercial setting does not by itself make a use unfair, because the listed purposes are generally pursued for profit. Id. at 584-85. Transformation strengthens a claim of fair use but is not absolutely necessary to one. Id. at 579; see Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417, 449-51 (1984) (complete copying of whole programs for private viewing held fair). Fair use is an affirmative defense, so Texaco bears the burden, and a proponent will have difficulty carrying it without favorable evidence about the relevant markets. Campbell, 510 U.S. at 590; Harper & Row, Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enterprises, 471 U.S. 539, 561 (1985). We review the ultimate determination independently and the subsidiary findings for clear error. Twin Peaks, 996 F.2d at 1374. Because the stipulated record and the district court's findings suffice to evaluate each factor, no remand for further factfinding is needed. Harper & Row, 471 U.S. at 560.

Section 108 does not alter the inquiry. Subsection (f)(4) preserves the right of fair use under § 107, so neither compliance nor noncompliance with a library provision decides this appeal. We express no view on whether Texaco's library qualifies under § 108.

**III. The four factors**

*A. Purpose and character.* Texaco's strongest argument is that these were copies for a scientist's actual research, not for sale, public distribution, or a competing publication. Scholarship and research are within the statute's list, and our decisions give uses of that kind real weight under the first factor. Wright v. Warner Books, Inc., 953 F.2d 731, 736 (2d Cir. 1991); New Era Publications International, ApS v. Carol Publishing Group, 904 F.2d 152, 156 (2d Cir. 1990). That Dr. Chickering did not put five of the articles to use in an experiment does not deprive the copying of a research purpose. Reviewing the literature, identifying possible future work, and keeping references at hand are ordinary parts of research. Nor does Texaco's profit motive make a scientist's work something other than research.

The first factor also asks what the copy does. These copies added nothing to the articles. Each reproduced an article whole, to be read and consulted as an authorized copy would be. Wright and New Era concerned new works of biography that used the plaintiffs' writings, and they do not fix the weight of this factor for nontransformative duplicates. The district court, deciding before Campbell, treated the copying as neither transformative nor noncommercial and resolved the factor for the publishers. 802 F. Supp. at 13. On independent review, crediting the research purpose fully and giving the commercial setting no presumptive force, we regard the factor as mixed and, at most, modestly favorable to Texaco. Labels such as "archival" or "institutional" do not decide it. A researcher's working file is still his file, and what matters is the reproduction function it serves.

*B. Nature of the works.* Published scientific articles are protected in their expression, but the facts and discoveries they report are not. Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340, 344, 349-50 (1991). There is a greater need to disseminate factual works than works of fiction or fantasy, Harper & Row, 471 U.S. at 563, and the district court so found, 802 F. Supp. at 17. This factor favors Texaco, and the weight is real. It does not, however, make every complete copy of a factual publication fair, and it says little about substitution.

Texaco adds that scientific authors seek dissemination, not royalties. Copyright does not protect only works written for pay. The authors assigned their rights, and the publishers perform the institutional work of assembling and disseminating the journals. That work remains a legally recognized interest. Conversely, the point gives publishers no right to monopolize facts, to prevent independent experiments, or to collect a fee whenever a researcher learns from an article. The law protects expression and permitted uses separately, and we hold the publishers only to the former.

*C. Amount and substantiality.* The relevant works are the individual articles, each separately authored and owned, not the bound volume. Comparing eight articles with a two-hundred-page volume does not make the copying small. 802 F. Supp. at 17. Complete copying does not automatically defeat fair use, Sony, 464 U.S. at 449-50, and a scientist may need an entire article to follow its methods or results. That explains why excerpts would be less useful to him. It does not show that the full amount is needed for a use different from the article's own function, because the retained copy performs the whole article's ordinary reference function. Necessity to a researcher's preferred convenience is not, by itself, necessity enough to displace the reproduction right. The third factor favors the publishers.

*D. Effect on the potential market.* Two markets are in play and must not be confused.

The first is the market for subscriptions. The district court did not rest principally on lost subscriptions, 802 F. Supp. at 19-21, and neither do we. The record contains limited evidence that Texaco would have bought more subscriptions without photocopying, and nothing justifies assuming that it would have bought one for every researcher. We give no weight to a speculative subscription loss, and the thinness of that proof does not affect the result.

The second is the market for authorized reproduction. The fourth factor looks beyond the defendant's own copying to whether unrestricted and widespread conduct of the same sort would substantially and adversely affect the potential market for the original. Campbell, 510 U.S. at 590; see Harper & Row, 471 U.S. at 566-68. The potential markets that count are those that owners would in general develop or license others to develop. A use such as criticism, which owners are unlikely to license, falls outside that notion. Campbell, 510 U.S. at 592. An owner also cannot manufacture a market injury by announcing a fee for conduct that would otherwise be fair, for then every fair use would vanish whenever permission was offered for sale. But a licensing practice that owners actually serve for a particular kind of reproduction is a cognizable market, and copies that substitute for what it supplies injure it.

We therefore hold that lost licensing revenue is cognizable under the fourth factor only where each of three conditions is met. First, the market is for the kind of use at issue, a reproduction that serves as a substitute for an authorized copy, and not for a use that the law leaves free of permission, such as criticism, commentary, or the use of facts. Second, the licensing mechanism is shown by evidence to be actually operating for that kind of use, with permission available at a reasonable price and with reasonable convenience, and is not merely announced. Third, the copying in question in fact substitutes for what the mechanism supplies. The existence or availability of a fee is never proof of harm by itself. The harm is the substitution, shown by these conditions together, and the burden on the fair-use defense remains with Texaco. We need not decide when a mechanism not yet operating is a cognizable potential market.

The conditions are met here. The district court found that convenient and reasonably priced licensing procedures were available for this reproduction. 802 F. Supp. at 18, 25. We accept those findings. They concern a mechanism operating for the very activity at issue, institutional photocopying of journal articles, and not a fee announced to defeat an otherwise fair use. The copies are of the kind that mechanism supplies. They are complete, retained, working duplicates of articles, made at the employer's expense, that serve the same reading and reference function as authorized copies, and the researcher could consult them in place of further authorized copies. The publishers do not claim a right to control criticism or the use of facts. The relevant loss is not a subscription for every employee. It is the substitution of unlicensed duplicates for authorized reproductions in a market connected to the statutory reproduction right. Texaco's showing, which centers on the thinness of the subscription loss, does not answer the licensing evidence.

Texaco's principal contrary argument is circularity. It contends that a permission market depends on the legal ruling it is offered to support, and that ordinary research photocopying may produce public benefits far exceeding any demonstrated subscription loss. The objection is sound as far as it goes, and we accept its premise: a newly asserted price cannot make an otherwise fair use unlawful, and a bare offer of a license establishes no protected entitlement. But circularity arises when the existence of the market is the question being decided. Here the record shows a functioning, workable mechanism for this kind of copying, and the question is whether duplicates that substitute for what that mechanism supplies remain fair. That is a different question from whether owners may charge for uses the law protects without permission, and Campbell places those uses outside the licensing market. The public benefit of research is real. It is weighed in the first and second factors, and it does not erase the market effect shown.

*E. Weighing.* The second factor, and modestly the first, favor Texaco. The third favors the publishers. The fourth weighs most heavily on this record and decides the balance. We do not reach that conclusion by counting factors or by judging scientific work unworthy, and we do not hold the fourth factor supreme in every case. We hold that the combination of complete retained substitute copies and a demonstrated, workable licensing mechanism for that very reproduction defeats Texaco's defense, even after full credit to the genuine research purpose and the factual character of the articles. Texaco has not carried its burden.

**IV. Remaining contentions**

Texaco relies on Sony and on Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States, 487 F.2d 1345 (Ct. Cl. 1973), aff'd by an equally divided Court, 420 U.S. 376 (1975). We accept Sony's lesson, that completeness and the absence of transformation neither decide nor foreclose fair use, and we apply it. But Sony found no meaningful likelihood of harm to a market, 464 U.S. at 450-51, and here an operating mechanism for the very reproduction is shown. The equally divided affirmance in Williams & Wilkins supplies no controlling reasoning from the Supreme Court. The Court of Claims decision is persuasive authority, not binding, and it differs from this case in institutional purpose, in the copying system, and in licensing-market conditions. It cannot be dismissed merely because it is older, and it does not govern materially different copying.

The older inquiry in Folsom v. Marsh, 9 F. Cas. 342, 348 (C.C.D. Mass. 1841), into the nature and objects of the use, the quantity and value of the material used, and its effect on the original, and the emphasis on productive use in Leval, Toward a Fair Use Standard, 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1105 (1990), are persuasive materials that accord with our emphasis on substitution. Neither binds us, and we rest nothing on them.

**V. Scope and disposition**

We decide the certified question for the eight retained complete copies on the stipulated record, and no more. We do not hold that all corporate research copying is commercial and therefore unlawful, that all complete copies fail, that transformation is indispensable to fair use, or that a license offer alone establishes a protected market. We do not hold that the journal issue rather than the article supplies the measure of the amount copied. We do not decide the legality of other scientists' copying, or of temporary, spontaneous, critical, or otherwise justified uses of scientific literature, and our holding does not establish that a scientist must purchase permission for every such use. We do not decide any period in which the asserted licensing opportunity was not genuinely available, or when a mechanism not yet operating is a cognizable market. We do not decide damages, including whether a license fee could measure damages for earlier conduct, a proposition that would require its own proof. Cf. Business Trends Analysts, Inc. v. The Freedonia Group, Inc., 887 F.2d 399, 404-05 (2d Cir. 1989) (rejecting a hypothetical saved-cost measure under 17 U.S.C. § 504(b) on the record there). We do not decide the scope of any injunction, and a ruling on fair use does not automatically determine it. Our holding applies the ordinary law of copyright to a commercial defendant. It is not a judgment about the relative worth of publishers and scientists.

The determination that the copying of the eight articles is not fair use is affirmed, and the case is remanded to the district court for further proceedings consistent with this opinion.
