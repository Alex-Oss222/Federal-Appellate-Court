## Stone's Position

### Component 1

**Component:** Fair use of the eight complete scientific articles retained for research

**Jurisdiction and reach:** The parties’ stipulated interlocutory record concerns the eight articles in one researcher’s files. Texaco bears the fair-use burden. Damages and the full scope of relief were not submitted for this determination. The statutory route and unsettled jurisdictional predicates in the neutral baseline remain controlling; this proposal does not assume them away.

**Proposed disposition:** Propose affirmance of the limited determination that this stipulated copying is not fair use.

**Governing law:** Independent review of the ultimate mixed fair-use question on established facts, with clear-error review of subsidiary findings. Twin Peaks Productions, Inc. v. Publications International, Ltd., 996 F.2d 1366, 1374 (2d Cir. 1993). Sections 106 and 107 require a contextual balance of purpose and character, nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and actual or potential market effect. Research is a favored statutory purpose, not an automatic exemption; profit status and lack of transformation are not categorical disqualifiers. Claimed licensing injury must concern a cognizable market, not permission fees invented to defeat otherwise fair use.

Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), rejects mechanical commercial-use presumptions and requires contextual analysis. Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417 (1984), demonstrates that complete nontransformative copying can be fair. Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340 (1991), separates protected expression from facts. The equally divided affirmance of Williams & Wilkins, 420 U.S. 376 (1975), supplies no controlling Supreme Court rationale. 17 U.S.C. §§ 106, 107, 108; Wright v. Warner Books, Inc., 953 F.2d 731, 736 (2d Cir. 1991); New Era Publications International, ApS v. Carol Publishing Group, 904 F.2d 152, 156 (2d Cir. 1990); Twin Peaks. Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States, 487 F.2d 1345 (Ct. Cl. 1973), is persuasive. Folsom v. Marsh, 9 F. Cas. 342, 348 (C.C.D. Mass. 1841), and Leval’s 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1105 (1990) article are historical/persuasive materials, not controlling circuit holdings.

**Legal relationship:** Applying the statutory fair-use balance and date-eligible precedent to the stipulated practice.

**Proposed holding:** The combination of complete retained substitute copies and a demonstrated, workable reproduction-licensing market defeats the defense on this record, while genuine research and the factual character of the works receive real weight.

**Legal reasoning:** Stone should propose affirmance of the limited fair-use ruling, with a narrower rationale than a categorical preference for licensed corporate copying. The case concerns complete articles placed in an employer-supported reference collection under conditions in which a practical reproduction-licensing market exists. It does not establish that a scientist must purchase permission for every temporary, spontaneous, critical, or otherwise justified use of scientific literature.

The analysis should credit the research purpose fully. A scientific article need not be used in an immediate experiment to assist research. Nor does an employer's profit motive make the scientist's intellectual activity legally fictitious. The first factor therefore contains considerations favoring Texaco, alongside the nontransformative creation of convenient substitutes for authorized copies. Stone should describe that mixed assessment rather than treat corporate status or the word “archival” as dispositive. A personal working file is still the researcher's file; its reproduction function, not an inflated institutional label, supplies the relevant concern.

The second factor favors Texaco because the works disseminate factual scientific knowledge. That weight is real. Yet § 107 does not make all complete copies of factual publications fair. The third factor examines the reproduction of each protected article in full. The research purpose can explain why excerpts would be less useful, but the resulting copy still performs the complete article's ordinary reference function. Necessity to the user's preferred convenience is not by itself necessity sufficient to defeat the reproduction right.

The market analysis must avoid circularity. A newly asserted price cannot make an otherwise fair use unlawful. Here, however, the claim concerns an ordinary market for additional usable copies, with evidence of an operational licensing system for institutional article reproduction. The relevant loss is not a speculative subscription for every employee, and the court need not assume that such purchases would occur. It is substitution for authorized reproductions in a demonstrated market connected to the statutory right. That gives the fourth factor substantial weight without making it supreme in every case.

On the combination of complete retained copies, their substitute function, and the workable licensing evidence, the defense should fail. The conclusion does not follow from counting factors or from finding the use unworthy. It follows from identifying a particular reproduction practice whose public research benefit, although substantial, does not on this record justify displacing the existing means of obtaining additional authorized copies.

Stone should expressly answer the concern that scientific authors often seek dissemination rather than royalties. Copyright does not protect only works written for cash payment. Authors may assign rights, and publishers incur the institutional work of assembling and disseminating scientific journals. Conversely, that observation supplies no right to monopolize facts, prevent independent experiments, or collect a fee whenever a researcher learns from an article. The existing statutory balance protects expression and permitted uses separately.

The mandate should affirm the interlocutory determination and return the remaining proceedings to the district court. It should not set damages, direct a universal licensing system, or automatically enjoin all research copying. The opinion should reserve materially different copying and any period in which the asserted licensing opportunity was not genuinely available. This is an application of ordinary copyright law to a commercial defendant, not a constitutional judgment about the relative civic worth of publishers and scientists.

**Independent or alternative considerations:** A license offer does not create its own legal entitlement. The proposed market rationale is limited to ordinary substitute reproductions in an evidenced market, not criticism, transformative uses, or every act of research.

**Preferred scope:** The combination of complete retained substitute copies and a demonstrated, workable reproduction-licensing market defeats the defense on this record, while genuine research and the factual character of the works receive real weight. The lawful outer boundary is the issue and remedial limit stated here, not a broader rule about unpresented facts.

Case-level proposed scope: the complete, retained reference copies on the stipulated record are not fair use when their substitute function and the demonstrated institutional licensing market are weighed with the genuine research purpose and factual character of the works.

**Fallback position:** Proposed: affirm only for the eight retained articles and the demonstrated market conditions, without a categorical first-factor rule against corporate researchers.

Case-level nonjoin limits, also proposed: all corporate research copying is commercial and therefore unlawful; transformation is an indispensable element of fair use; a license offer alone establishes a legally protected market; a journal issue rather than the individually copyrighted article necessarily supplies the denominator; or a ruling about fair use automatically determines damages and injunctive scope.

**Remedy and remand:** Affirm the interlocutory determination and return remaining proceedings to the district court. Do not set damages or order a universal licensing system.

**Reserved questions:** Temporary, spontaneous, critical, or materially different copying; earlier periods without a genuinely available licensing mechanism; particular damages and injunction questions.
