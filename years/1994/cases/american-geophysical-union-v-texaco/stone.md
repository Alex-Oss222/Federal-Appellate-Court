# American Geophysical Union v. Texaco, Inc.

**Status:** Version 1; proposed, not user-approved. **Approval reference:** None. The source brief states that every Stone position, alternative, and negotiating limit is a proposal pending approval.

## Stone's proposed judicial position

Stone should propose affirmance of the limited fair-use ruling, with a narrower rationale than a categorical preference for licensed corporate copying. The case concerns complete articles placed in an employer-supported reference collection under conditions in which a practical reproduction-licensing market exists. It does not establish that a scientist must purchase permission for every temporary, spontaneous, critical, or otherwise justified use of scientific literature.

The analysis should credit the research purpose fully. A scientific article need not be used in an immediate experiment to assist research. Nor does an employer's profit motive make the scientist's intellectual activity legally fictitious. The first factor therefore contains considerations favoring Texaco, alongside the nontransformative creation of convenient substitutes for authorized copies. Stone should describe that mixed assessment rather than treat corporate status or the word “archival” as dispositive. A personal working file is still the researcher's file; its reproduction function, not an inflated institutional label, supplies the relevant concern.

The second factor favors Texaco because the works disseminate factual scientific knowledge. That weight is real. Yet § 107 does not make all complete copies of factual publications fair. The third factor examines the reproduction of each protected article in full. The research purpose can explain why excerpts would be less useful, but the resulting copy still performs the complete article's ordinary reference function. Necessity to the user's preferred convenience is not by itself necessity sufficient to defeat the reproduction right.

The market analysis must avoid circularity. A newly asserted price cannot make an otherwise fair use unlawful. Here, however, the claim concerns an ordinary market for additional usable copies, with evidence of an operational licensing system for institutional article reproduction. The relevant loss is not a speculative subscription for every employee, and the court need not assume that such purchases would occur. It is substitution for authorized reproductions in a demonstrated market connected to the statutory right. That gives the fourth factor substantial weight without making it supreme in every case.

On the combination of complete retained copies, their substitute function, and the workable licensing evidence, the defense should fail. The conclusion does not follow from counting factors or from finding the use unworthy. It follows from identifying a particular reproduction practice whose public research benefit, although substantial, does not on this record justify displacing the existing means of obtaining additional authorized copies.

Stone should expressly answer the concern that scientific authors often seek dissemination rather than royalties. Copyright does not protect only works written for cash payment. Authors may assign rights, and publishers incur the institutional work of assembling and disseminating scientific journals. Conversely, that observation supplies no right to monopolize facts, prevent independent experiments, or collect a fee whenever a researcher learns from an article. The existing statutory balance protects expression and permitted uses separately.

The mandate should affirm the interlocutory determination and return the remaining proceedings to the district court. It should not set damages, direct a universal licensing system, or automatically enjoin all research copying. The opinion should reserve materially different copying and any period in which the asserted licensing opportunity was not genuinely available. This is an application of ordinary copyright law to a commercial defendant, not a constitutional judgment about the relative civic worth of publishers and scientists. Master Jurisprudence §§ 6, 8, 20, 24-25; Statutory Interpretation; Markets and Private Power; Records, Communications, and New Instruments.

## Proposed negotiating limits

**Preferred and broadest defensible holding:** the complete, retained reference copies on the stipulated record are not fair use when their substitute function and the demonstrated institutional licensing market are weighed with the genuine research purpose and factual character of the works.

**Acceptable narrower proposal:** affirm only this eight-article determination, expressly reserving other research practices, copying without a workable relevant licensing market, and distinct historical periods. The first factor can be treated as mixed, or its precise direction reserved, provided the overall statutory balance is explained rather than mechanically counted.

**Non-join limits:** all corporate research copying is commercial and therefore unlawful; transformation is an indispensable element of fair use; a license offer alone establishes a legally protected market; a journal issue rather than the individually copyrighted article necessarily supplies the denominator; or a ruling about fair use automatically determines damages and injunctive scope.
