## Record and issues

**Material record:** The American Geophysical Union and eighty-two other publishers sued Texaco over unauthorized photocopying of scientific and technical articles. Texaco employed approximately 400 to 500 researchers nationally and maintained a substantial research library at Beacon, New York. Its subscriptions to the Journal of Catalysis increased from one to two in 1983 and to three in 1988.

The parties deliberately narrowed an initial trial to a written record addressing the fair-use defense. They randomly selected researcher Dr. Donald H. Chickering II and focused on eight articles copied from the Journal of Catalysis and retained in his files. Chickering was a chemical engineer conducting catalysis research. He or other Texaco employees made complete copies for his use. Six articles came to his attention through circulated journal issues; two through references in other publications.

The copies were principally retained for possible later consultation rather than used immediately in a particular experiment. Chickering did not ultimately use five of them. That fact does not mean the copying lacked a research purpose: reviewing literature, identifying possible future work, and maintaining accessible references are ordinary parts of research. It does matter to the distinct question whether the copying created convenient substitutes for additional authorized copies.

Academic Press generally owned copyrights in the individual articles through assignments from authors, who were not paid for publication. The articles were predominantly factual scientific writings. The record also addressed the Copyright Clearance Center's licensing arrangements, the feasibility of licensing this kind of institutional copying, and the limited evidence of additional subscription demand absent photocopying.

### Issue 1: Fair use of the eight complete scientific articles retained for research

**Preservation and reach:** The parties’ stipulated interlocutory record concerns the eight articles in one researcher’s files. Texaco bears the fair-use burden. Damages and the full scope of relief were not submitted for this determination.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the ultimate mixed fair-use question on established facts, with clear-error review of subsidiary findings. Twin Peaks Productions, Inc. v. Publications International, Ltd., 996 F.2d 1366, 1374 (2d Cir. 1993).

**Governing test and burdens:** Sections 106 and 107 require a contextual balance of purpose and character, nature of the work, amount and substantiality, and actual or potential market effect. Research is a favored statutory purpose, not an automatic exemption; profit status and lack of transformation are not categorical disqualifiers. Claimed licensing injury must concern a cognizable market, not permission fees invented to defeat otherwise fair use.

Section 106 establishes the copyright owner's reproduction right, subject to the limitations that include § 107. Section 107 identifies research as a potentially fair purpose and requires consideration of purpose and character, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on the actual or potential market. Those considerations must be weighed together. They are not votes on a four-item scorecard, and transformation is not an extra statutory element that every fair use must satisfy.

**Purpose and character.** Texaco's strongest argument is that these were copies for a scientist's actual research, not resale, public redistribution, or replacement publication. *Wright v. Warner Books, Inc.*, 953 F.2d 731, 736 (2d Cir. 1991), and *New Era Publications International, ApS v. Carol Publishing Group*, 904 F.2d 152, 156 (2d Cir. 1990), give genuine weight to the listed purposes. Employment by a profit-making company does not strip scientific activity of that character. *Campbell* rejects an automatic commercial-use presumption and makes the particular use important.

The publishers respond that the relevant act is complete reproduction for a personal reference collection supplied at the employer's expense. The research that may eventually produce new knowledge does not transform every reproduced input. Keeping a permanent working copy can fulfill substantially the same reading and reference function as an authorized copy. That is a legitimate consideration, although it does not itself defeat fair use: *Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc.*, 464 U.S. 417 (1984), demonstrates that complete, nontransformative copying can be fair in an appropriate setting.

**Nature of the work.** Published scientific articles receive copyright protection in their expression, but their factual character favors fair use. The underlying facts and discoveries are not copyrighted merely because the article reporting them is protected. See *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991). The unpaid status of authors is relevant to the incentive argument, but does not negate their assignments or erase the publisher's legally recognized interest in producing and distributing journals.

**Amount and substantiality.** The relevant copyrighted works are the individual articles, not the journal issue as a whole. Copying eight complete articles is not rendered quantitatively small by comparing each with a two-hundred-page volume. Nevertheless, completeness must be assessed against the legitimate purpose: a scientist may need an entire methods or results discussion to understand a paper. The question is whether the amount is justified in the particular fair-use balance, not whether any complete copy automatically fails.

**Market effect.** Lost subscriptions and copying-license revenues are different markets. The evidence does not justify assuming that Texaco would buy a subscription for every researcher if photocopying were unavailable. The publishers' stronger contention is that a functioning mechanism existed for licensing this repeated institutional reproduction. A traditional, reasonable, or likely-to-develop licensing market can be cognizable under *Campbell*, but the owner cannot establish market injury merely by announcing a fee for conduct that would otherwise be fair. Otherwise, every fair use would disappear when a copyright owner offered to sell permission.

The material distinction is between the market for ordinary substitute reproductions and an asserted right to control criticism, commentary, or another use the law protects without permission. The record concerning actual institutional licensing and the nature of the archived copies supports the former characterization here. The competing argument remains substantial: the market itself can depend on the legal ruling, and ordinary research photocopying may produce public benefits far exceeding any demonstrated subscription loss. The court must evaluate those concerns rather than assume that a license demand proves its own validity.

*Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States*, 487 F.2d 1345 (Ct. Cl. 1973), affirmed by an equally divided Court, 420 U.S. 376 (1975), provides an important comparison involving governmental medical-library copying. The equally divided affirmance did not create controlling Supreme Court reasoning. The Court of Claims decision is persuasive, with differences concerning institutional purpose, the factual copying system, and licensing-market conditions. It cannot be dismissed merely because it is older, or treated as binding on materially different copying.

The older fair-use inquiry in *Folsom v. Marsh*, 9 F. Cas. 342, 348 (C.C.D. Mass. 1841), asks about purpose, quantity, value, and substitution. Pierre N. Leval's *Toward a Fair Use Standard*, 103 Harv. L. Rev. 1105 (1990), is useful persuasive writing on productive use, not a statute or binding precedent. Section 108's library provisions also do not displace § 107: failure to qualify for a specific library exception does not independently defeat fair use.

**Historical Supreme Court authority:** Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569 (1994), rejects mechanical commercial-use presumptions and requires contextual analysis. Sony Corp. of America v. Universal City Studios, Inc., 464 U.S. 417 (1984), demonstrates that complete nontransformative copying can be fair. Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co., 499 U.S. 340 (1991), separates protected expression from facts. The equally divided affirmance of Williams & Wilkins, 420 U.S. 376 (1975), supplies no controlling Supreme Court rationale.

**Other governing authority:** 17 U.S.C. §§ 106, 107, 108; Wright v. Warner Books, Inc., 953 F.2d 731, 736 (2d Cir. 1991); New Era Publications International, ApS v. Carol Publishing Group, 904 F.2d 152, 156 (2d Cir. 1990); Twin Peaks. Williams & Wilkins Co. v. United States, 487 F.2d 1345 (Ct. Cl. 1973), is persuasive. Folsom v. Marsh, 9 F. Cas. 342, 348 (C.C.D.
