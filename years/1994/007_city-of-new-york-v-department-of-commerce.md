### Record and issues

**Material record:** Cities, states, organizations, individual residents, and intervenors challenged the refusal to adjust the 1990 census to account for differential undercounting. Their interests included representation and distributions of public funds. Wisconsin and Oklahoma opposed adjustment. The controversy concerned whether an enumeration supplemented by a post-enumeration survey, or PES, provided a better population measure, not whether every person could literally be counted without error.

A 1989 stipulation required a fresh consideration of adjustment, development of review guidelines, a survey capable of producing adjusted figures, and an explained decision. The Bureau surveyed approximately 170,000 households, compared the resulting information with the enumeration, and developed adjustment factors for 1,392 population categories. Its estimates indicated an overall undercount of approximately 5.3 million people. The estimated undercount was substantially higher for several racial and ethnic minorities than for non-Hispanic whites. These are survey estimates in the record, not findings of an independently known exact population. *City of New York*, 34 F.3d at 1117-23.

The Secretary acknowledged that adjusted figures would probably improve numerical accuracy nationally and in jurisdictions containing approximately two-thirds of the population. He nevertheless emphasized distributive accuracy, meaning the proportions assigned to different places, uncertainty in local estimates, possible changes in research findings, and the implications for later censuses. The advisory panel divided evenly; the Bureau's director favored adjustment while other senior advisers opposed it. The Secretary expressly disclaimed a belief that the 1990 survey had been manipulated.

After a thirteen-day bench trial, Judge McLaughlin found that adjustment produced a more accurate count for most purposes and that plaintiffs mounted a compelling attack on several aspects of the decision. He nevertheless found the Secretary's choice and application of the eight guidelines not arbitrary or capricious. He considered a focus on distributive accuracy compatible with the census's representative function and stated that disagreement over the better exercise of discretion was insufficient to overturn the decision. Judgment dismissed the complaint. 822 F. Supp. 906, 916, 920-29 (E.D.N.Y. 1993).

#### Issue 1: Constitutional review of census nonadjustment

**Preservation and reach:** The appeal challenges the Secretary’s census decision and the district court’s constitutional treatment after a bench trial. It does not establish racial purpose merely from unequal undercount.

**Standard of review:** Independent review of the governing constitutional standard; historical fact findings for clear error; the level of deference to the Secretary’s good-faith census judgment must be identified rather than assumed.

**Governing test and burdens:** Constitutional representation requirements constrain the census, but mathematical perfection and identical accuracy in every locality are impossible. Determine whether the government’s actual reasons constitute a permissible good-faith judgment; do not import a redistricting burden shift without establishing that its rationale governs enumeration.

Article I, § 2 and the Fourteenth Amendment's apportionment provision connect representation to population. *Wesberry v. Sanders*, 376 U.S. 1 (1964), *Kirkpatrick v. Preisler*, 394 U.S. 526 (1969), and *Karcher v. Daggett*, 462 U.S. 725, 730-31, 740 (1983), require good-faith efforts toward population equality in congressional districting. They do not directly decide how the federal government must choose between disputed nationwide census estimates. The difference is material: drawing boundaries with accepted population data and estimating the underlying population present different uncertainties and institutional choices.

*Department of Commerce v. Montana*, 503 U.S. 442, 459-66 (1992), recognizes the constitutional constraints and unavoidable compromises in interstate apportionment. *Franklin* examines whether the challenged census judgment is consistent with constitutional language and the goal of equal representation. Neither decision confers unreviewable discretion. Neither commands that any proposed increase in aggregate numerical accuracy automatically defeats concerns about distribution among states and smaller jurisdictions. Montana; Franklin.

Plaintiffs' strongest case is therefore not simply that the survey counted more people. It is that the Secretary demanded superior accuracy at every level, discounted gains affecting most of the population, and preferred speculative risks in future censuses over the demonstrated advantages of the unmanipulated current survey. Those facts could show that the asserted accuracy rationale failed to engage the constitutional task. The government's strongest answer is that numerical and distributive accuracy differ, substantial experts disagreed, and the trial court found the actual guideline choices rational after extensive evidence. A preferable adjustment is not necessarily a constitutionally required adjustment.

Racial impact must be kept separate from those questions. *Washington v. Davis*, 426 U.S. 229 (1976), and *Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp.*, 429 U.S. 252, 265-68 (1977), do not permit automatic racial strict scrutiny from unequal effects alone. An apportionment claim can exist without proof of racial purpose, but that does not establish that the state-districting burden of justification transfers unchanged to every federal census judgment. Such a transfer would be a doctrinal extension requiring justification, not an already binding holding.
