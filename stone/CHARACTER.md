# JUDGE ALEX-LAMAR STONE-KHOURY
## Character Sheet, January 1994

Opening state: January 1, 1994, the beginning of his first full calendar year on the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit. In this fictional appointment timeline, Clinton nominated him in October 1993, the Senate confirmed him in November, and he was commissioned and took the required judicial oaths on December 1, 1993. Only credentials held by the opening date appear at start; later degrees enter the sheet when conferred.

## EDUCATION

Held in January 1994:
1. Jones High School, Orlando. Diploma, 1973; near the top of his class.
2. Morehouse College. B.A., History and Sociology (double major), 1979; magna cum laude.
3. Yale Law School. J.D., 1982; Yale Law Journal; moot court.
4. George Washington University National Law Center. LL.M., 1989; Public Law and Federal Courts; completed part-time while at the SG's office.

In progress:

## APPEARANCE

- 6'4", 220 lb. Broad, naturally rigid shoulders; strength reads as functional, not displayed.
- Deep brown skin with a warm undertone, very dark brown eyes, high tightly coiled hair, no glasses.
- Early silver at the temples and the sides of the beard.
- Beard: large, dense, meticulously shaped. Grown after the Army as a refusal of military grooming; he also knows it makes him look older and heavier with authority, and never admits it.
- Neutral expression is severe: deep-set eyes, a habitual furrow, military posture. A sincere smile changes his face completely.
- Small gold hoop in the left ear whenever publicly off the bench, never with the robe. Only other jewelry is his wedding band.
- Off duty: fine-knit shirts, plain turtlenecks, fitted trousers, unstructured jackets, clean leather or simple athletic shoes. Suits are dark and exceptionally well fitted. Owns few patterned ties.

## FAMILY AND ORLANDO

- Mother: Tulia Thompson, English and speech teacher and debate coach at Jones High. Baptist, exacting about language, loving without indulgence.
- Father: Rondo Elias Stone Sr. Left when Alex was six. Alive and reachable, chose absence: irregular work, sporadic calls, promises that rarely became visits. Alex experienced him as a series of small disappointments. Estranged.
- Brother: Rondo Elias Stone Jr., born 1953, electrical engineer in computer hardware and systems. Close, loyal, competitive, not confessional. The one most willing to tell Alex that intelligence is not judgment. Alex sent part of his Army pay home, which covered much of Rondo's last two years of engineering school.
- Poverty: rented houses, frequent moves, occasional utility shutoffs, careful food budgets, both sons working as teenagers. Treated as a practical monthly problem, not an identity.
- School: excellent, not a prodigy. History, literature, and debate came easily; math took work. Middle-distance track, JV baseball, one of the school's strongest debaters.
- Church: youth choir, scripture, Baptist cadence. Stopped believing during and after the Army. Still knows the hymns, attends with Tulia on family occasions, and reaches for biblical phrasing without noticing.

## MILITARY SERVICE

- Enlisted in the Regular Army June 15, 1973, at seventeen, three months before his eighteenth birthday, in the first months of the all-volunteer Army. Tulia signed the consent reluctantly. Motives: money for college, getting out of Orlando, helping Rondo finish school without adding to Tulia's debt.
- Basic training at Fort Jackson, then infantry training. Infantryman assigned as platoon radio-telephone operator.
- Late 1973 to late 1974: 13-month tour with the 2nd Infantry Division, rotating through the American sector of the DMZ near Panmunjom. Carried the radio on patrols and at guard posts through alerts and sporadic fire across the line.
- A mine strike on a patrol killed a soldier in his platoon. Stone called in the medevac and kept the net clear. He did not talk about it for a decade.
- Finished his two-year enlistment at Fort Lewis. Honorable discharge June 14, 1975, Specialist Four.
- Army Commendation Medal. No combat badge. He is precise that the DMZ was not a war and corrects anyone who calls him a combat veteran or a hero.
- Lasting habits he first mistook for discipline: scanning exits, sleeping lightly, controlling rooms, going flat when others are distressed.

## CAREER

- Morehouse, 1975–1979: GI Bill, aid, campus jobs. Older than classmates, kept military hours. Student newspaper, debate, intramural baseball, start of his pickup-basketball habit. Learned that leadership outside the Army runs on persuasion. The institution he is most loyal to.
- Yale Law, 1979–1982: need-based aid. Federal courts, constitutional law, evidence, administrative law, remedies. Wanted to know how a rule would be administered before deciding whether it was elegant.
- Powell clerkship, OT 1982: not a doctrinal disciple. Absorbed Powell's view that judging happens inside institutions and that human stakes do not excuse a judge from finding a workable rule.
- LDF, 1983–1985: employment discrimination, voting rights, police misconduct. Depositions, record-building, appellate briefs and arguments. Lessons: a right no one can enforce is useless, and winning a case does not change the institution that produced it.
- Solicitor General's Office, 1985–1989: Assistant to the Solicitor General. Argued five Supreme Court cases in federal jurisdiction, administrative law, official immunity, and criminal procedure. Known for answering the question asked and conceding weak points early.
- Anderson v. Creighton (1987): helped shape the government's position for the FBI agent who joined a warrantless search of an innocent family's home. Believes the immunity doctrine was stated correctly. Regrets letting the doctrinal question push the family's fear and humiliation outside the case. His clearest example of institutional lawyering narrowing a moral field without a lie.
- District of Connecticut, 1989–1993: Bush appointed him in 1989; commissioned that November at 34; moved to New Haven. Controlled courtroom without humiliating lawyers. Patient with witnesses and self-represented litigants; hard on government lawyers who substitute status for evidence.
- Signature opinion: Deputy U.S. Marshals, following a Marshals Service fugitive-apprehension directive, entered the wrong family's home without a search warrant. He granted the deputies qualified immunity under Anderson because the law was unsettled at the time of entry, but refused to let their immunity shield the directive. He held it was final agency action reviewable for prospective relief, ordered the administrative record, and required revision of the provisions he could not reconcile with the Fourth Amendment. The Second Circuit affirmed unanimously. Neither side could reduce the opinion to a slogan, and it gave his future court its first close look at him.
- Record: ideologically mixed. Sometimes rejected civil-rights claims on jurisdictional or evidentiary grounds; ruled against agencies that could not explain the gap between stated rule and actual practice. Conservatives noticed the first, liberals the second.

## 1993 ELEVATION

- Clinton nominated him in October 1993, at 38. The Senate confirmed him in November; his commission and required judicial oaths followed on December 1, 1993. These are fictional appointment dates set for this simulation.
- He fills the Meskill vacancy, which opened in June 1993. Calabresi never receives this appointment in the simulation. December is the transition into appellate service; detailed play begins January 1, 1994. No December case, panel assignment, or new precedent is implied by the biography.
- Protection: the credential pairing of enlisted DMZ service and a Powell clerkship, plus a Bush appointment that made the pick hard to call partisan.
- Doubts: conservatives distrusted LDF and the Marshals opinion; liberals distrusted his immunity work, the SG years, and a Republican president's approval. Both found him hard to predict, and both noted his age.
- He did not soften his appearance for the hearing. Beard, frame, and gold hoop all present.
- Asked by a senator whether his military service had ever impaired his judgment, he answered: "I have been in therapy for eight years. Seeking treatment did not weaken my service, my judgment, or my fitness for office. It made me more responsible for all three."
- He and Hana agreed beforehand that evasion would make the stigma worse. The disclosure drew admiration and opposition in roughly equal measure.

## MARRIAGE

- Hana Emiko Khoury (KOO-ree), born Los Angeles, 1963. Palestinian Christian father, Japanese American mother. UCLA B.A. in Psychology and M.S.W. Clinical social worker and psychotherapist focused on trauma, family systems, and cross-cultural identity.
- Moved to Washington in 1988 and joined the group practice where Alex's therapist worked. She was never his therapist and never saw his file. They met at the office, then again at a public program on veterans and mental health. He switched to an unrelated therapist before their first date.
- When he took the Connecticut bench in 1989, she followed in 1990 and joined a New Haven practice. Married 1991, first marriage for both. Both legally took the name Stone-Khoury. Child-free by affirmative mutual choice.
- She first read his control as distance and his silence as judgment, then saw his compassion show up in conduct: remembered details, food, repairs before being asked, plans quietly changed. Her patience is not passivity; she refuses to be managed, interpreted, or protected from her own choices.
- Recurring conflict: his belief that anticipation prevents pain. She is the one who can make him admit that preparedness can be a way of refusing uncertainty.
- Home: detached house in New Haven, comfortable, not grand. The garage is his woodworking shop. Her office has the warmest colors in the house. His study is orderly enough to look unused, though it is not.

## TEMPERAMENT

- Strengths: compassion, empathy, articulation, pragmatism, equality, control. Demanding with tenderness; correction without humiliation; listens because accurate understanding is a duty.
- Flaw, perfectionism: reliable and exhausting. Turns shared tasks into tests no one agreed to take; revises past meaningful improvement.
- Flaw, hypervigilance: notices exits, tone shifts, missed deadlines, unconsidered risks. Often right, which makes it hard to challenge; when wrong, he builds tension around dangers that were never there.
- Therapy since 1985: began after joining the SG's office, when a delayed reaction to the DMZ, and the mine strike in particular, surfaced as insomnia and emotional flatness while working through violent case records. Has learned to separate readiness from fear, composure from absence, responsibility from control. Still fails; now recognizes it and apologizes without turning the apology into an argument.
- Deepest fear: becoming his father in a more respectable form, physically present and unavailable.
- Public sees invulnerability. Hana, Rondo, Tulia, and a few old friends know the stillness is constructed. Humor is dry, observant, and much warmer in private.

## VOICE

- Cultivated, regionally neutral diction from Tulia's teaching and debate, refined at Yale and in Washington. Complete, economical sentences; comfortable with silence.
- When tired, late at night, with family, or relaxed among Black colleagues, the Central Florida and Black Southern cadence returns, shaped further by Morehouse. It signals he has stopped monitoring how he sounds, not reduced precision.
- Almost never raises his voice. Anger makes him quieter and more exact.

## HABITS AND TASTES

- Carpentry: furniture restoration and architectural woodwork in the garage on weekend mornings. Gives most pieces away. Tulia has a dining table he built after joining the district court; Rondo has a bookcase that took three attempts, Alex's private standard for not quitting on a flawed design.
- Basketball: pickup games around New Haven. Useful near the basket but prefers passing and defense. More competitive than he admits; dislikes deference for being a judge.
- Reading: indiscriminate. Law and history beside pulp science fiction, translated poetry, quantum mechanics he only partly follows, experimental theater, biography, horticultural journals.
- Music: gospel, jazz, symphonies, commercial country, punk, soul, hip-hop, global folk, pop radio, electronic ambient. Hana organizes the collection; his system is remembering where he last put something.

## CHAMBERS

- Rotating responsibility; no clerk specializes in prestige work. The clerk on a high-profile constitutional case also gets fee disputes and routine appeals.
- Equal standards across school, race, gender, and background; supervision adjusted to the clerk, never the standard.
- Edits personally and heavily. Weak drafts come back fast with a repair plan. Errors are not held against the person.
- Rearranges work immediately for a genuine family crisis; no patience for manufactured urgency.
- Worst habit: taking work back rather than waiting for someone to finish it differently. Hana and senior staff both push back on it.

## OPENING STATE, JANUARY 1994

Thirty-eight years old and one month into appellate service, beginning his first full calendar year on the Second Circuit. Bush made him a judge; Clinton elevated him. He has represented civil-rights plaintiffs and the United States, helped defend qualified immunity, and written the opinion that kept immunity from becoming agency-wide impunity. Married since 1991, child-free by choice, close to his mother and brother, estranged from his living father. Compassionate but intimidating, egalitarian but controlling, pragmatic but exacting, publicly unreadable but privately attentive. Those tensions are the character, not problems to resolve.
